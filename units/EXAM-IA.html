Rules: groups and academic honesty

Working in a group

Groups of up to three can share a setup and help each other collect data, but:

  • Each person needs their own research question: a different IV, or the same IV and a different DV.
  • No two people may hand in the same raw data.
  • Each person writes their own report. Group reports are not allowed.
  • Name your group members when you submit Stage 1.

Academic honesty

  • AI tools are not allowed for any part of the IA (FCPS policy).
  • Cite every source you use: websites, textbooks and class notes.
  • Your report is checked against your earlier stages and with plagiarism software.
  • Asking for help is expected and never penalised. Ask early.

Other things that are allowed

  • A simulation or database instead of a hands-on experiment, but it needs deeper analysis (for example, getting a physical constant from it).
  • SL and HL use the same task and the same criteria.

Stage 1 · Topic Approval

At home · Due Nov 20 · submit the form below

Goal: one independent variable, one dependent variable, a system to study, and a reason the physics predicts a relationship.

  1. Look for “how does y depend on x?” Change one thing and measure one thing. Choose something with real physics to dig into: textbook labs everyone has done (a simple pendulum, a mass on a spring) are too easy to score well on. Browse the topic ideas or adapt an experiment you liked.
  2. Find the physics. Look for an equation linking your IV and DV. It tells you what to plot to get a straight line, and what the gradient means.
  3. Run the feasibility check below. If you can’t tick a box, change the topic now: it’s much cheaper than changing it later.
  4. Write your question in the form below: what you change, what you measure, and how you’ll measure it. Leave out the range; you choose it in Stage 2.
  5. State your objective: what you expect to find, e.g. “find how the frequency depends on the string’s length, and use the gradient to find the wave speed”.

Feasibility check

Avoid

  • Two IVs at once, or an IV you can’t set to chosen values.
  • Categories instead of numbers (“different materials”). You want an IV you can set to 5 or more values.
  • A question with an obvious answer and no physics to test, e.g. distance = speed × time.
Example: a weak and a strong research question

All the examples on this page come from one made-up investigation: how the vibrating length of a monochord string affects its frequency, read from a phone app.

Weak

“How does string length affect sound?”

Strong

“How does the vibrating length of a steel monochord string (0.200 m to 0.800 m), held at a constant tension of 19.6 N, affect its fundamental frequency, measured with a phone frequency-analyser app?”

The strong version names the IV, the DV and how it is measured, and the system. (The range goes in at report time.)

Stage 2 · Procedure Approval

At home · Due Dec 18 · submit the planner below

Goal: a plan detailed enough that another student could repeat it exactly. Don’t collect data until it’s approved.

You can start the planner now; you don’t have to wait for your question to be approved. Save draft as you go, and Submit when it’s ready. Mr. Holmes leaves a note on each part and marks it Approved or Revise. Once it’s approved, the planner makes the start of your report as a Google Doc.

  1. Variables. IV: what you change, its unit, the values and the number of repeats. DV: what you measure and with what. For both: the instrument’s resolution and your reading uncertainty. Controls: what each one is, why it matters, and how you keep it constant.
  2. Range and values. As wide a range as you practically can, at least 5 values spread evenly, at least 3 repeats of each. Say why you chose them.
  3. Apparatus, with sizes and the resolution of every measuring instrument.
  4. Labelled diagram, clear enough to rebuild the setup from: Google Drawings, or on paper and photographed.

Stage 3 · Collect data

In class, or at home · nothing to turn in: it goes in your draft

Goal: complete, honest raw data before the setup comes apart.

Collecting at home? Only once your procedure is approved, and finish before the class starts collecting data in school. Then you analyse your data in class while the others collect. (Phones can’t be used in school, so phone-based experiments are done at home.)

  1. Set up your raw data table first: headings with quantity, unit and uncertainty, and a column for each repeat.
  2. Record every reading exactly as read, to the instrument’s precision. Don’t average in your head and don’t round.
  3. Keep observation notes (required): anything odd, anything that went wrong, anything you changed or redid. Your evaluation comes from these.
  4. Sketch a quick graph as you go. A point off the trend can be re-measured now, not after the equipment is put away.
  5. Measure the fixed quantities you’ll need later (masses, lengths, diameters) rather than trusting a label.
  6. Photograph the setup if you can, with no faces in it.

Stage 4 · Analyse

In class · nothing to turn in: it goes in your draft

Goal: turn raw data into a result with an uncertainty.

  1. Processed data table: means, uncertainties, and calculated quantities (like 1/L or T²), each column with quantity, unit and uncertainty.
  2. One sample calculation for each kind of processing, including how the uncertainty was found.
  3. Straighten the graph. Rearrange your equation into y = mx + b and plot what it says to plot.
  4. Graph: labelled axes with units, error bars, best-fit line, steepest and shallowest lines. Make it with the Graph Maker in the P1-00 sims ↗.
  5. Gradient ± uncertainty (and intercept), and the physical quantity the gradient stands for.
  6. Compare with the theoretical or published value. Should the line pass through the origin? Does it?

Every rule for uncertainties and graphs is in Reference → Uncertainties and graphs.

Marked on: Data analysis

Top band (5–6): recording and processing are both clear (easy to follow) and precise (units, labels, consistent decimal places); uncertainties handled properly; processing correct and relevant to the question.

  • Uncertainties belong in the raw table, the processed table, the graph and the final result, not just in a paragraph.
  • Don’t force the line through the origin unless the physics says it must.
  • Every bit of processing should lead back to the research question.

Stage 5 · Rough draft

At home · due TBA · turn in on Schoology

Goal: a complete report. This is the one draft Mr. Holmes comments on, so make it as finished as you can.

  1. Use the report template headings, in order. Your Stage 1–4 work goes straight into them.
  2. Write the conclusion (see below).
  3. Write the evaluation (see below).
  4. Count your words and put the number at the top.
  5. Go through the checklist at the bottom of this stage, then turn it in.

Marked on: Conclusion

Top band (5–6): a conclusion fully consistent with your analysis including its uncertainties, justified by comparing it with accepted science (cited).

  • Answer the research question in your first sentence, with a number and its uncertainty.
  • Say what the gradient and intercept mean physically.
  • Compare with theory or a published value: is it inside your uncertainty range? What is the percentage difference?
  • Explain any anomalies. A high R² is not proof: it shows how well a line fits, not that the physics is right.

Marked on: Evaluation

Top band (5–6): explains the relative impact of specific weaknesses and limitations (which mattered most, and how they affected the result), with realistic improvements that match them.

  • At least three specific weaknesses. For each: random or systematic, which way it pushed your result, and how big its effect was compared with the others. “Human error” and “more trials” are generic and score low.
  • Use your numbers: compare your percentage uncertainty with your percentage difference from the accepted value.
  • Limitations: how far your conclusion reaches (the range, the system, your assumptions).
  • For each weakness, an improvement: exactly what you’d change, and how it would change the outcome. Not a new investigation.

Examples: conclusion, weakness, improvement, limitation

Conclusion

Weak

“As the string got shorter the frequency went up, so my hypothesis was correct and the experiment worked.”

Strong

“The graph of f against 1/L is a straight line through all seven error bars, so the frequency is inversely proportional to the vibrating length, as the standing-wave equation predicts (Tsokos, 2023):f=12LFμThe gradient is (68.2 ± 0.6) m s⁻¹. Theory predicts a gradient of12Fμ=(69.3±0.3) m s−1using F = 19.6 N and μ = (1.02 ± 0.01) g m⁻¹, measured by weighing 1.000 m of the same string. My result is 1.6 % below the prediction, which is more than its 0.9 % uncertainty, and the two ranges just miss each other (up to 68.8 m s⁻¹ against from 69.0 m s⁻¹). The intercept is +1.3 Hz instead of zero. Both point to a small systematic error in the measured length (see Evaluation).”

It answers with numbers and uncertainties, explains the gradient, compares with a cited prediction, says what the gap means, and notices the intercept.

A weakness

Weak

“There may have been human error in measuring, and background noise could have affected the app.”

Strong

“The most significant weakness was the vibrating length. I measured between the inner edges of the two bridges, but the string touches each bridge at its rounded top, about 1–2 mm further out, so every length was slightly too short. This is a systematic error with a bigger effect on short lengths (3 mm is 1.5 % of 0.200 m but only 0.4 % of 0.800 m). It makes the points at large 1/L fall below the line, which lowers the gradient and gives a positive intercept, exactly what I found. By comparison, the app’s ±0.1 Hz is under 0.2 % of every reading, so its effect is negligible.”

An improvement

Weak

“Use more accurate equipment and do more trials.”

Strong

“Replace the rounded bridges with triangular knife-edge bridges, so the string has a sharp contact point, and measure the length between the two contact points. This removes the systematic shortfall in L, so I would expect the intercept to move towards zero and the gradient towards the predicted 69.3 m s⁻¹.”

A limitation

Strong

“Only one string and one tension were tested, so the conclusion is confirmed only for 19.6 N and lengths of 0.200–0.800 m. The theory also assumes a perfectly flexible string; a real steel string’s stiffness slightly raises the frequency of very short lengths, so the relationship may not hold well below 0.200 m.”

How it’s markedThe four criteria and their mark bands, in plain words.

How the marking works

Your report is marked on four criteria, each out of 6 (24 in all, each worth a quarter). Mr. Holmes marks it, then the IB checks a sample of the class’s marks (moderation).

  • For each criterion the marker picks the band that fits your work best overall. You don’t need every point in a band to get it, and a top band doesn’t mean perfect.
  • Doing well on one criterion doesn’t carry over to the others. Each is judged on its own.
  • The examiner can only reward what is on the page. If you thought about something but didn’t write it, it doesn’t count.

Watch the verbs. The bands step up from state (just say it) to describe (say what, in detail) to explain (say what and why). A common way to lose marks is describing when the top band asks you to explain.

Research design 6 marks

What it checks: how well you explain your question and how you collected your data.

MarksYour report…
1–2States the question with no context. Lists a few method decisions. Someone else couldn’t repeat it.
3–4Puts the question in a broad context. Describes the method decisions. Someone could repeat it, with a few gaps.
5–6Puts the question in a specific, fitting context. Explains the method decisions (why, not just what). Someone could repeat it exactly.

What the IB means

  • Context = your question names the IV and DV, briefly describes the system you’re studying, and comes with the physics theory that applies.
  • Method decisions = how you measure the IV and DV; the range, interval and number of values; how many repeats; how precise your instruments are; which variables you control and how; and safety, ethical and environmental issues. For a simulation or database: why you chose it and how you picked the data.
  • Repeatable = specific materials and precise steps, with no padding or repetition.

Data analysis 6 marks

What it checks: how you record, process and present your data so that it answers your question.

MarksYour report…
1–2Recording and processing is neither clear nor precise. Little sign of uncertainties. Processing has major gaps or errors.
3–4Clear or precise, but not both. Uncertainties are considered but with significant gaps or mistakes. Processing has significant gaps or errors.
5–6Both clear and precise. Uncertainties are handled properly. Processing is correct and relevant to the question.

What the IB means

  • Clear = a reader can easily follow how you processed the data.
  • Precise = you follow the conventions: labelled tables and graphs, units, consistent decimal places and significant figures.
  • A major problem stops you reaching a valid conclusion. A significant one still lets you conclude, but limits how far.

Conclusion 6 marks

What it checks: how well you answer your question using your analysis and accepted physics.

MarksYour report…
1–2States a conclusion that isn’t supported by the analysis. Only a surface comparison with accepted science.
3–4Describes a conclusion that isn’t fully consistent with the analysis. Makes some relevant comparison with accepted science.
5–6Justifies a conclusion that is fully consistent with the analysis, including its uncertainties. Justifies it through a relevant comparison with accepted science.

What the IB means

  • Fully consistent means you interpret your processed data with its uncertainties.
  • Accepted scientific context = published values, textbooks, course notes, papers or reliable websites. Cite them so a reader could find them.

Evaluation 6 marks

What it checks: how well you judge your method and how good your suggested improvements are.

MarksYour report…
1–2States generic weaknesses. States realistic improvements.
3–4Describes specific weaknesses or limitations. Describes realistic improvements that match them.
5–6Explains the relative impact of specific weaknesses or limitations (which mattered most, and how they affected your result). Explains realistic improvements that match them.

What the IB means

  • Generic = could be said about almost any experiment (“human error”, “more trials”, “better equipment”).
  • Weaknesses are about controlling variables, the precision of your measurements, or the spread in your data.
  • Limitations are about how far your conclusion reaches: the range of data you collected, the system you used, or the assumptions you made.

Format rules

  • Font: Arial or Times New Roman, size 11. Spacing: single or larger. Text: justified.
  • Page numbers on every page.
  • Tables: text and numbers centred in the cells; every column heading has the quantity, unit and uncertainty.
  • Anonymous: no name and no school anywhere, only your candidate code.
  • No appendices. Anything that matters goes in the report; markers won’t look elsewhere.
  • Length: about 12 pages is typical.

The 3,000-word limit

Not counted: charts and diagrams, data tables, equations, formulas and calculations, citations and references, the bibliography, and headings. Everything else counts. State your word count honestly; it can be checked.

Uncertainties and graphsClass uncertainty rules, writing results, graphs, outliers.

Class rules for uncertainty

ReadingUncertaintyExample
Analogue scale (thermometer, protractor, meter)± half the smallest division(21.5 ± 0.5) °C
Length on a ruler± one smallest division: you read both ends, each ± half a division(50.0 ± 0.1) cm
Digital display (stopwatch, balance, meter, app)± 1 in the last decimal place(2.34 ± 0.01) s
Repeated readingsmean ± half the range2.31, 2.40, 2.35 s → (2.35 ± 0.05) s
  • If half the range is smaller than the reading uncertainty (for example, all your repeats are the same), use the reading uncertainty.
  • Timing n cycles? Divide both the time and its uncertainty by n.
  • Put the uncertainty in each column heading, e.g. t / s (±0.01), with one sentence under the table saying where it comes from.

Writing and processing results

  • A value and its uncertainty end at the same decimal place as the measurement: (2.34 ± 0.01) s, not (2.34 ± 0.005) s or (2.3 ± 0.01) s.
  • Keep extra digits while you calculate. Round only the final answer.
  • Propagate uncertainties with the rules in your data booklet, and show one sample calculation for each step.

Graphs

  • Plot whatever makes your relationship a straight line.
  • Don’t know the relationship? Plot ln y against ln x. If it’s a straight line, y = kxn, and the gradient is the power n.
  • Label axes as quantity / unit, e.g. t / s.
  • Error bars on every point, or a note saying they are too small to see.
  • Don’t force the best-fit line through the origin unless the physics says it must.
  • Gradient uncertainty = (steepest − shallowest) ÷ 2. The same goes for the intercept.
  • Software is fine, but say what you used. R² is not an uncertainty.

Outliers and errors

  • Outlier: circle it, explain the likely cause, and re-measure if you can. You may leave it out of the fit if you say why.
  • Random error spreads readings both ways. Repeats reduce its effect.
  • Systematic error shifts every reading the same way. Repeats don’t fix it, and it often shows up as an unexpected intercept.
  • Accepted value: is it inside your ± range? If your percentage difference is much bigger than your percentage uncertainty, look for a systematic error.
