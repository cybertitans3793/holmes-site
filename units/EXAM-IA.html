Internal Assessment

What the IA is

The internal assessment (IA) is one experiment you design, run and write up yourself. You choose a question, collect your own data, analyse it and write a report of at most 3,000 words.

20 %of your IB Physics grade
24 marks4 criteria × 6 marks
3,000word limit
~10 hof class time in all
  • Your data must be numbers (quantitative). Add written observations where they help.
  • Most IAs in this class are hands-on experiments. A simulation or database is allowed, but it needs deeper analysis (for example, getting a physical constant from it), not just a graph of numbers.
  • Your topic does not have to be original. Ideas from books, websites or the topic list are fine. The data, analysis and writing must be yours.
  • SL and HL use the same task and the same criteria.

The six stages

You turn in each stage on Schoology. Stages 1 and 2 need Mr. Holmes’s approval before you move on: don’t collect data until your procedure is approved.

Working in a group

You may work in a group of up to three. You can share the setup and help each other collect data, but:

  • Each person needs their own research question, with a different IV, or the same IV and a different DV.
  • No two people may hand in the same raw data.
  • Each person writes their own report: their own analysis, conclusion and evaluation. Group reports are not allowed.
  • Tell Mr. Holmes who is in your group when you turn in Stage 1.

Academic honesty

  • AI tools are not allowed for any part of the IA (FCPS policy).
  • Cite every source you use: websites, textbooks and class notes.
  • Your report is checked against your earlier stages and with plagiarism software.
  • Asking for help is expected and is never penalised. Ask early.

How the marking works

Your report is marked on four criteria, each out of 6 (24 in all, each worth a quarter). Mr. Holmes marks it, then the IB checks a sample of the class’s marks (moderation).

  • For each criterion the marker picks the band that fits your work best overall. You don’t need every point in a band to get it, and a top band doesn’t mean perfect.
  • Doing well on one criterion doesn’t carry over to the others. Each is judged on its own.
  • The examiner can only reward what is on the page. If you thought about something but didn’t write it, it doesn’t count.

Watch the verbs. The bands step up from state (just say it) to describe (say what, in detail) to explain (say what and why). A common way to lose marks is describing when the top band asks you to explain.

Research design 6 marks

What it checks: how well you explain your question and how you collected your data.

MarksYour report…
1–2States the question with no context. Lists a few method decisions. Someone else couldn’t repeat it.
3–4Puts the question in a broad context. Describes the method decisions. Someone could repeat it, with a few gaps.
5–6Puts the question in a specific, fitting context. Explains the method decisions (why, not just what). Someone could repeat it exactly.

What the IB means

  • Context = your question names the IV and DV, briefly describes the system you’re studying, and comes with the physics theory that applies.
  • Method decisions = how you measure the IV and DV; the range, interval and number of values; how many repeats; how precise your instruments are; which variables you control and how; and safety, ethical and environmental issues. For a simulation or database: why you chose it and how you picked the data.
  • Repeatable = specific materials and precise steps, with no padding or repetition.

Tips

  • For every variable, say how you changed or measured it, with what instrument, its units, and its limits.
  • Justify your range and number of values: why this range, why this many?
  • Always include safety, ethics and environment, even if it’s one sentence saying why there are no concerns and what precautions you took.

Common mistakes

  • Controls listed but not explained (no “how” or “why”).
  • Two independent variables in one question.
  • Theory that is about the topic in general, not about your relationship.

Data analysis 6 marks

What it checks: how you record, process and present your data so that it answers your question.

MarksYour report…
1–2Recording and processing is neither clear nor precise. Little sign of uncertainties. Processing has major gaps or errors.
3–4Clear or precise, but not both. Uncertainties are considered but with significant gaps or mistakes. Processing has significant gaps or errors.
5–6Both clear and precise. Uncertainties are handled properly. Processing is correct and relevant to the question.

What the IB means

  • Clear = a reader can easily follow how you processed the data.
  • Precise = you follow the conventions: labelled tables and graphs, units, consistent decimal places and significant figures.
  • A major problem stops you reaching a valid conclusion. A significant one still lets you conclude, but limits how far.

Tips

  • Uncertainties must show up in the raw table, the processed table, the graph and the final result, not just in a paragraph.
  • Show one sample calculation for each kind of processing, including the uncertainty.
  • Use the class uncertainty rules and make your graph with the P1-00 Graph Maker.

Common mistakes

  • Axis labels with no units, or no error bars.
  • A best-fit line forced through the origin when the physics doesn’t require it.
  • No steepest and shallowest lines, so no uncertainty on the gradient.
  • Processing that doesn’t lead back to the research question.

Conclusion 6 marks

What it checks: how well you answer your question using your analysis and accepted physics.

MarksYour report…
1–2States a conclusion that isn’t supported by the analysis. Only a surface comparison with accepted science.
3–4Describes a conclusion that isn’t fully consistent with the analysis. Makes some relevant comparison with accepted science.
5–6Justifies a conclusion that is fully consistent with the analysis, including its uncertainties. Justifies it through a relevant comparison with accepted science.

What the IB means

  • Fully consistent means you interpret your processed data with its uncertainties.
  • Accepted scientific context = published values, textbooks, course notes, papers or reliable websites. Cite them so a reader could find them.

Tips

  • Answer the research question in your first sentence, with a number and its uncertainty.
  • Say what the gradient and intercept mean physically, and compare them with theory or a published value. Is the accepted value inside your uncertainty range?
  • Make every claim traceable to a number in your tables or graph.

Common mistakes

  • Restating results without interpreting them.
  • No comparison with theory or a published value.
  • Calling a high R² “proof”. It shows how well a line fits, not that your physics is right.

Evaluation 6 marks

What it checks: how well you judge your method and how good your suggested improvements are.

MarksYour report…
1–2States generic weaknesses. States realistic improvements.
3–4Describes specific weaknesses or limitations. Describes realistic improvements that match them.
5–6Explains the relative impact of specific weaknesses or limitations (which mattered most, and how they affected your result). Explains realistic improvements that match them.

What the IB means

  • Generic = could be said about almost any experiment (“human error”, “more trials”, “better equipment”).
  • Weaknesses are about controlling variables, the precision of your measurements, or the spread in your data.
  • Limitations are about how far your conclusion reaches: the range of data you collected, the system you used, or the assumptions you made.

Tips

  • Aim for at least three specific weaknesses. For each: is it random or systematic, which way did it push your result, and how big was its effect compared with the others?
  • Use your numbers: compare your percentage uncertainty with your percentage difference from the accepted value.
  • Make each improvement specific and realistic in a school lab: what equipment or change, and how it would improve the data.

Common mistakes

  • Generic weaknesses and vague fixes (“be more careful”).
  • Improvements that are really a new investigation, not a fix.
  • Weaknesses that aren’t linked to any effect on the data.

Also on the checklist

These aren’t part of the four criteria, but they are on the checklist:

  • Under 3,000 words; title, candidate code and word count at the top; no name or school anywhere.
  • Arial or Times New Roman 11, single spacing (or more), justified text, table text centred, page numbers.
  • Method as numbered steps; for each improvement, how it would change the outcome.

Stage 1 · Topic Approval

Goal: one independent variable, one dependent variable, a system to study, and a reason the physics predicts a relationship. Homework.

  1. Look for “how does y depend on x?” These questions work best. Change one thing and measure one thing. Choose something with real physics to dig into: textbook labs everyone has done (a simple pendulum, a mass on a spring) are too easy to score well on. Browse the topic ideas or adapt an experiment you liked.
  2. Find the physics. Look for an equation linking your IV and DV. It tells you what to plot to get a straight line, and what the gradient means.
  3. Run the feasibility check (below). If any answer is no, change the topic now. It’s much cheaper than changing it later.
  4. Write your question in the form below: what you change, what you measure, and how you’ll measure it. Leave out the range; you choose it in Stage 2.
  5. State your objective: what you expect to find, e.g. “find how T depends on L, and use the gradient to measure g”.

Feasibility check

Turn in

  • Research question · IV · DV and how you’ll measure it · the system · your objective · one or two sentences of theory (with the equation) · group members, if any.

Avoid

  • Two IVs at once, or an IV you can’t set to chosen values.
  • A question whose answer is obvious and has no physics to test, e.g. distance = speed × time.
  • Categories instead of numbers (“different materials”). You want an IV you can set to 5 or more numerical values.

Stage 2 · Procedure Approval

Goal: a plan detailed enough that another student could repeat it exactly. Homework. Don’t collect data until it’s approved.

  1. Variables table. IV: how you change it, the range, how many values, and the instrument and its uncertainty. DV: how you measure it, the instrument and its uncertainty, how many repeats. Controls: what each one is, how you keep it constant, and why it matters.
  2. Choose the range and values. Use as wide a range as you practically can, with at least 5 values spread evenly and at least 3 repeats of each. More is better if time allows. Say why you chose them.
  3. Apparatus list, with sizes, and the resolution of every measuring instrument.
  4. Labelled diagram of the setup (Google Drawings works well).
  5. Method as numbered steps, 10 at most. Be specific (“release from 10° marked on a protractor”) and skip the obvious (“gather the equipment”).
  6. Safety, ethics, environment. Always address all three, even if briefly: hazards and precautions, any ethical issues, and disposal or waste.
  7. Do a quick trial run. Class time for data collection is short, so your procedure has to work the first time. A trial run finds the problems while they’re still easy to fix.

Stage 3 · Collect data

Goal: complete, honest raw data before the setup comes apart. In class, or at home. You can collect data at home, but only after your procedure is approved. Phones can’t be used in school, so phone-based experiments are done at home.

  1. Prepare your raw data table first: headings with quantity, unit and uncertainty, and a column for each repeat.
  2. Record every reading exactly as read, to the instrument’s precision. Don’t average in your head and don’t round.
  3. Keep observation notes as you go (required): anything odd, anything that went wrong, anything you changed or had to redo. These notes are where your evaluation comes from: a weakness you didn’t notice now is hard to explain later.
  4. Sketch a quick graph as you go. A point off the trend can be re-measured now, not after the equipment is put away.
  5. Measure the fixed quantities you’ll need later (masses, lengths, diameters). Measure them rather than trusting a label.
  6. Take a photo of the setup if you can (at home, or ask Mr. Holmes in class), with no faces in it.

Before you leave

Turn in

  • Your raw data table and your observation notes.

Stage 4 · Analyse

Goal: turn raw data into a result with an uncertainty. In class.

  1. Processed data table: means, uncertainties, and any calculated quantities (like 1/L or T²), each column with its quantity, unit and uncertainty.
  2. One sample calculation for each kind of processing, including how the uncertainty was found.
  3. Straighten the graph. Rearrange your equation into y = mx + b and plot what it says to plot.
  4. Graph: labelled axes with units, error bars, a best-fit line, and steepest and shallowest lines.
  5. Gradient ± uncertainty (and intercept). Work out the physical quantity the gradient stands for.
  6. Compare with the theoretical or published value. Should the line pass through the origin? Does it?

All the rules are on the Uncertainties and graphs tab. Make your graph with the Graph Maker in the P1-00 sims: it draws error bars, max and min lines and gives the gradient with its uncertainty, ready to paste into your report.

Turn in

  • Processed table · sample calculations · graph · gradient with its uncertainty.

Stage 5 · Rough draft

Goal: a complete report. This is the one draft Mr. Holmes comments on, so make it as finished as you can. Homework.

  1. Use the report template headings, in order. Your Stage 1–4 work goes straight into them.
  2. Write the conclusion:
    • Answer the question with your result and its uncertainty.
    • Describe the relationship your graph shows.
    • Say what the gradient and intercept mean.
    • Compare with theory or a published value (cited): is it inside your uncertainty range? What is the percentage difference?
    • Explain any anomalies.
  3. Write the evaluation:
    • At least three specific weaknesses. For each: random or systematic, which way it pushed your result, and how big an effect compared with the others.
    • Limitations: the range, the system, your assumptions.
    • For each weakness, an improvement: what exactly you’d do (equipment or method), and how it would change the outcome.
  4. Count your words and put the number at the top.
  5. Go through the checklist yourself before you submit.

Turn in

  • The full draft. Go through the checklist first.

Stage 6 · Final draft

Goal: your final report. There’s no more feedback after this, and once it’s submitted to the IB it can’t be taken back. Homework.

  1. Act on every comment from your rough draft. Mr. Holmes can advise but can’t edit, so the changes must be yours.
  2. Recheck your numbers: every calculation, unit and decimal place.
  3. Word count: under 3,000 and stated honestly.
  4. Anonymous: no name, no school, no teacher’s name, and no faces in photos. Only your candidate code.
  5. Citations: every source cited in the text and listed in the references.
  6. Clean up: remove template prompts and notes to yourself. Save as a PDF.

Turn in

  • The final report, as a PDF.

Format rules

  • Font: Arial or Times New Roman, size 11. Spacing: single or larger. Text: justified.
  • Page numbers on every page.
  • Tables: text and numbers centred in the cells; every column heading has the quantity, unit and uncertainty.
  • Anonymous: no name and no school anywhere, only your candidate code.
  • No appendices. Anything that matters goes in the report; markers won’t look elsewhere.
  • Length: about 12 pages is typical.

The 3,000-word limit

Not counted: charts and diagrams, data tables, equations, formulas and calculations, citations and references, the bibliography, and headings. Everything else counts. State your word count honestly; it can be checked.

Class rules for uncertainty

ReadingUncertaintyExample
Analogue scale (thermometer, protractor, meter)± half the smallest division(21.5 ± 0.5) °C
Length on a ruler± one smallest division: you read both ends, each ± half a division(50.0 ± 0.1) cm
Digital display (stopwatch, balance, meter, app)± 1 in the last decimal place(2.34 ± 0.01) s
Repeated readingsmean ± half the range2.31, 2.40, 2.35 s → (2.35 ± 0.05) s
  • If half the range is smaller than the reading uncertainty (for example, all your repeats are the same), use the reading uncertainty.
  • Timing n cycles? Divide both the time and its uncertainty by n.
  • Put the uncertainty in each column heading, e.g. t / s (±0.01), with one sentence under the table saying where it comes from.

Writing and processing results

  • A value and its uncertainty end at the same decimal place as the measurement: (2.34 ± 0.01) s, not (2.34 ± 0.005) s or (2.3 ± 0.01) s.
  • Keep extra digits while you calculate. Round only the final answer.
  • Propagate uncertainties with the rules in your data booklet, and show one sample calculation for each step.

Graphs

  • Plot whatever makes your relationship a straight line.
  • Don’t know the relationship? Plot ln y against ln x. If it’s a straight line, y = kxn, and the gradient is the power n.
  • Label axes as quantity / unit, e.g. t / s.
  • Error bars on every point, or a note saying they are too small to see.
  • Don’t force the best-fit line through the origin unless the physics says it must.
  • Gradient uncertainty = (steepest − shallowest) ÷ 2. The same goes for the intercept.
  • Software is fine, but say what you used. R² is not an uncertainty.

Outliers and errors

  • Outlier: circle it, explain the likely cause, and re-measure if you can. You may leave it out of the fit if you say why.
  • Random error spreads readings both ways. Repeats reduce its effect.
  • Systematic error shifts every reading the same way. Repeats don’t fix it, and it often shows up as an unexpected intercept.
  • Accepted value: is it inside your ± range? If your percentage difference is much bigger than your percentage uncertainty, look for a systematic error.

Weak and strong, side by side

All the examples come from one made-up investigation, so you can see how the parts connect: how does the vibrating length of a string affect its fundamental frequency? A monochord string is kept under constant tension by a 2.000 kg hanging mass, and the frequency is read from a phone app.

Research question Research design

Weak

“How does string length affect sound?”

Strong

“How does the vibrating length of a steel monochord string (0.200 m to 0.800 m), held at a constant tension of 19.6 N, affect its fundamental frequency, measured with a phone frequency-analyser app?”

The strong version names the IV with its range, the DV and how it is measured, and the system.

A control variable Research design

Weak

“Controlled variable: tension.”

Strong

“Tension: kept constant by hanging the same 2.000 kg mass from the string over the pulley for every trial, so the tension is its weight, 19.6 N. This matters because the frequency depends on √(tension): a 1 % change in tension would change f by 0.5 %.”

A method step Research design

Weak

“Pluck the string and record the frequency.”

Strong

“4. Pluck the string gently at its midpoint, hold the phone 10 cm above it, and record the frequency of the strongest peak once the reading is steady. Repeat three times for each length.”

Raw data heading and uncertainty Data analysis

Weak

Columns headed “Length” and “Frequency”, with lengths written as 0.2, 0.35, 0.500.

Strong

Columns headed “Vibrating length L / m (±0.001)” and “Frequency f / Hz (±0.1)”, with every length to 3 decimal places. Under the table: “Length uncertainty is half the smallest division (0.5 mm) at each end of the metre stick, so ±1 mm; frequency uncertainty is the last decimal place the app displays.”

A sample calculation Data analysis

Weak

“1/L was calculated for each length.”

Strong

“For L = (0.500 ± 0.001) m: 1/L = 2.000 m⁻¹. The percentage uncertainty in L is 0.001 ÷ 0.500 = 0.2 %, and taking the reciprocal (a power of −1) keeps it at 0.2 %, so 1/L = (2.000 ± 0.004) m⁻¹.”

Conclusion Conclusion

Weak

“As the string got shorter the frequency went up, so my hypothesis was correct and the experiment worked.”

Strong

“The graph of f against 1/L is a straight line through all seven error bars, so the frequency is inversely proportional to the vibrating length, as f = (1/2L)√(F/μ) predicts. The gradient is (68.2 ± 1.0) m s⁻¹. Theory predicts ½√(F/μ) = (69.3 ± 0.3) m s⁻¹, using F = 19.6 N and μ = (1.02 ± 0.01) g m⁻¹ measured by weighing 1.000 m of the same string (Tsokos, 2023). The two ranges overlap (69.0 to 69.2 m s⁻¹), so the result is consistent with theory, but it sits about 1.6 % low and the intercept is +2.4 Hz instead of zero. Both suggest a small systematic error in the measured length (see Evaluation).”

The strong version answers the question with numbers and uncertainties, explains what the gradient means, compares it with a cited prediction, and notices the intercept.

Evaluation: a weakness Evaluation

Weak

“There may have been human error in measuring, and background noise could have affected the app.”

Strong

“The most significant weakness was the vibrating length. I measured between the inner edges of the two bridges, but the string touches each bridge at its rounded top, about 1–2 mm further out, so every length was slightly too short. This is a systematic error with a bigger effect on short lengths (3 mm is 1.5 % of 0.200 m but only 0.4 % of 0.800 m). It makes the points at large 1/L fall below the line, which lowers the gradient and gives a positive intercept, exactly what I found. By comparison, the app’s ±0.1 Hz is under 0.2 % of every reading, so its effect is negligible.”

Evaluation: an improvement Evaluation

Weak

“Use more accurate equipment and do more trials.”

Strong

“Replace the rounded bridges with triangular knife-edge bridges, so the string has a sharp contact point, and measure the length between the two contact points. This removes the systematic shortfall in L, so I would expect the intercept to move towards zero and the gradient towards the predicted 69.3 m s⁻¹.”

Evaluation: a limitation Evaluation

Limitation

“Only one string and one tension were tested, so the conclusion is confirmed only for 19.6 N and lengths of 0.200–0.800 m. The theory also assumes a perfectly flexible string; a real steel string’s stiffness slightly raises the frequency of very short lengths, so the relationship may not hold well below 0.200 m.”
