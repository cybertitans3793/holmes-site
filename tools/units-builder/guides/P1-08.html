Study guide

Unit 8 · Rotation

How to use this: read the key ideas, then cover each worked example and try it yourself before reading the solution. Do the self-check questions on paper before you open the answers, mark the ones you miss, and come back to them in a day or two without looking. Rereading won't do much; answering questions will.

Key ideas

  • Angles are in radians: one revolution = 2π rad. Every point on a spinning rigid object turns through the same angle θ, with the same angular velocity ω = Δθ/Δt and angular acceleration α = Δω/Δt.
  • Linking to straight-line motion: a point at radius r moves a distance s = rθ, at speed v = rω, with tangential acceleration at = rα. Points farther out move faster.
  • Rotational kinematics works just like 1D kinematics with θ, ω and α in place of x, v and a (for constant α).
  • Torque τ = rF sinθ measures how well a force turns something. It's biggest when you push far from the pivot and at 90° to the lever. A force aimed at the pivot gives no torque.
  • Equilibrium needs both ΣF = 0 (no translation) and Στ = 0 (no rotation). Torques can be taken about any point, so pick one where an unknown force acts.
  • Rotational inertia I = Σmr² is resistance to spinning up. It depends on the mass and how far it is from the axis: a hoop has more than a solid disk of the same mass and radius. Formulas for shapes are given to you.
  • Parallel axis theorem: I = Icm + Md² for an axis a distance d from the center of mass.
  • Newton's 2nd law for rotation: Στ = Iα. With a massive pulley, the string's tension is different on the two sides; that difference is what spins the pulley.
  • Rotational kinetic energy K = ½Iω². Something rolling without slipping has both: K = ½mv² + ½Iω², with v = ωr.
  • In a race down a ramp, the shape with the smallest I compared with mr² wins: a solid sphere beats a solid cylinder, which beats a hoop. Mass and radius cancel out.
  • Work by a torque W = τΔθ changes rotational kinetic energy; power P = τω.
  • Angular momentum L = Iω for a spinning object, or L = mvr⊥ for a point mass. Angular impulse τΔt changes it.
  • Conservation of angular momentum: with no net external torque, L stays constant. A skater pulling her arms in lowers I, so ω goes up. Her kinetic energy increases (her muscles do work), even though L doesn't change.
  • Elliptical orbits: gravity points at the Sun, so it exerts no torque about it, and the planet's angular momentum is conserved. At the closest and farthest points, mvr is the same, so it moves fastest when it's closest. Total energy K + Ug (with Ug = −GMm/r) is also conserved.

Common mistakes

  • Putting degrees or revolutions into v = rω or at = rα. Convert to radians first.
  • Using the distance to the force instead of the lever arm, or forgetting sinθ when the force isn't at 90°.
  • Forgetting that torques have a direction. Pick clockwise or counterclockwise as + and stick to it.
  • Saying a heavier ball rolls down a ramp faster. The mass cancels; only the shape matters.
  • Setting the two tensions on a massive pulley equal.
  • Saying kinetic energy is conserved when a skater pulls her arms in. Angular momentum is conserved; kinetic energy goes up.
  • Drawing all the forces at the center of an object. For torque, draw each force where it acts.
  • Pointing friction down the ramp on a rolling object. It points up the ramp whether the object rolls down or up, and it's static friction, so it turns no energy into thermal energy.
  • Using conservation of linear momentum when something hits a pivoted object. The pivot pushes during the hit; only angular momentum about the pivot is conserved.
  • Saying an object moving in a straight line has no angular momentum. About a point off its path it has L = mvr⊥, and that stays constant.
  • Thinking zero net force means no spinning (or that zero net torque means no speeding up). Two equal, opposite forces at different points can spin an object without moving its center. Write both ΣF and Στ.
  • Using the height change of the end of a swinging rod for its gravitational energy. Use the height change of its center of mass.
  • Leaving the floor's normal force at the middle of a box that's about to tip. Just before it tips, the whole normal force and friction act at the edge it tips over.

How to solve a torque problem

  1. Draw the object (a bar or a disk) with each force drawn where it acts. This is an extended free-body diagram.
  2. Pick the pivot (axis). For equilibrium, choose a point where an unknown force acts, so its torque is zero.
  3. Choose + rotation (clockwise or counterclockwise) and give each torque a sign.
  4. Find each torque: τ = r⊥F, with r⊥ the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the force's line.
  5. Write Στ = Iα (or Στ = 0), and ΣF = ma (or ΣF = 0) if you need a second equation.
  6. Link the motions: a string coming off a wheel has a = αr. Then solve.

Which tool?

  • Forces and torques known, want α (or a): Στ = Iα and ΣF = ma.
  • Rolling down a ramp, want a speed: energy, with K = ½mv² + ½Iω² and v = ωr.
  • Something changes shape or grabs on (a skater, a person jumping onto a merry-go-round) with no outside torque: conservation of angular momentum.
  • Orbits: angular momentum for the speed at closest and farthest points; energy for speeds anywhere.

Static equilibrium

A person on a plank

A uniform 6.0 m plank weighing 300 N rests on supports at its two ends. A 600 N person stands 2.0 m from the left end. Find the force from each support.

Torques about the left support (counterclockwise = +):

R(6.0)−300(3.0)−600(2.0)=0 R=350N

Forces up = forces down:

L+R=300+600 L=550N

Putting the pivot at the left support makes that unknown force drop out of the torque equation. The left support takes more of the load because the person is closer to it.

Massive pulley

A hanging mass spins a pulley

A 2.0 kg mass hangs from a string wrapped around a solid-disk pulley of mass 4.0 kg (I = ½MR²) on a frictionless axle. Find the mass's acceleration and the string's tension.

The hanging mass (down = +):

mg−T=ma →20−T=2.0a

The pulley, with α = a/R:

TR=Iα=(12MR2)aR →T=12Ma=2.0a

Add the two equations:

20=4.0a a=5.0m/s2 T=10N

The radius cancels. The pulley's inertia acts like an extra ½M = 2.0 kg that has to be accelerated, which is why the mass falls at half of g.

Rolling energy

A solid ball against a hoop

A solid ball (I = ⅖mr²) and a hoop (I = mr²) roll without slipping from rest down a ramp 1.4 m high. Find each one's speed at the bottom.

Rolling without slipping, ω = v/r:

mgh=12mv2+12Iω2

Solid ball, I = ⅖mr²:

mgh=12mv2+12(25mr2)v2r2=710mv2 v=10gh7=10(10)(1.4)7 vball=4.5m/s

Hoop, I = mr²:

mgh=12mv2+12mv2=mv2 →v=gh vhoop=(10)(1.4)=3.7m/s

Both start with the same energy per kilogram. The hoop has to put half of it into spinning, the ball only 2/7, so the ball wins. Mass and radius never appear in the answer.

Angular momentum

A spinning skater pulls her arms in

A skater spins at 2.0 rad/s with her arms out (I = 4.0 kg·m²). She pulls them in, and her rotational inertia drops to 1.6 kg·m². Find her new angular velocity and compare her kinetic energy before and after.

No outside torque, so angular momentum is conserved:

Iiωi=Ifωf →(4.0)(2.0)=(1.6)ωf ωf=5.0rad/s Ki=12Iiωi2=12(4.0)(2.0)2=8.0J Kf=12(1.6)(5.0)2=20J

The ice exerts almost no torque, so L stays the same. Kinetic energy goes up because her arm muscles do work pulling her arms inward.

Torque → angular acceleration

Spinning up a wheel

A string wrapped around a solid disk (M = 2.0 kg, R = 0.10 m) on a frictionless axle is pulled with a steady 4.0 N. Find the angular acceleration, and the angular velocity after 2.0 s from rest.

I=12MR2=12(2.0)(0.10)2=0.010kg·m2 τ=RF=(0.10)(4.0)=0.40N·m α=τI=0.400.010 α=40rad/s2 ω=αt=(40)(2.0)=80rad/s

That's about 13 revolutions per second.

The same 4.0 N on a hoop of the same mass and radius would give half the angular acceleration, because a hoop's I is twice as big.

Angular momentum in a collision

Clay sticks to a hanging rod

A uniform rod (3.0 kg, 1.0 m long, I = ⅓ML²) hangs at rest from a frictionless pivot at its top end. A 0.50 kg lump of clay moving horizontally at 6.0 m/s hits the bottom end and sticks. Find the angular velocity just after the hit, and how much kinetic energy is lost.

System: rod + clay. The pivot pushes on the rod during the hit, so linear momentum isn't conserved. But that force acts at the pivot, so it has no torque about it: angular momentum about the pivot is conserved.

Before: only the clay moves, in a straight line that passes r⊥ = 1.0 m below the pivot:

Li=mvr⊥=(0.50)(6.0)(1.0)=3.0kg·m2/s

After: the rod and the clay at its end turn together:

If=13ML2+mL2=1.0+0.50=1.5kg·m2 ω=LiIf=3.01.5 ω=2.0rad/s

Kinetic energy before and after:

Ki=12(0.50)(6.0)2=9.0J Kf=12(1.5)(2.0)2=3.0J Ki−Kf=9.0−3.0=6.0J

Only L about the pivot carries through the hit. Kinetic energy isn't conserved (it's a sticky collision), and neither is linear momentum: it's 3.0 kg·m/s before, but after, the rod's center (moving at 1.0 m/s) and the clay (2.0 m/s) carry 4.0 kg·m/s. The difference is the pivot's push.

Static equilibrium with a hinge

A sign on a hinged beam held by a cable

A uniform 4.0 m beam weighing 200 N is hinged to a wall and held level by a cable that runs from its far end up to the wall, at 30° above the beam. A 300 N sign hangs from the far end. Find the cable's tension and the force from the hinge.

Pivot at the hinge, so the hinge's unknown force has no torque. Counterclockwise = +. The beam's weight acts at its center (2.0 m), and only the cable's perpendicular part, T sin30°, turns the beam:

Tsin30°(4.0)−200(2.0)−300(4.0)=0 2.0T=1600→T=800N

Horizontal forces: the cable pulls the beam toward the wall, so the hinge must push it away from the wall:

H=Tcos30°=(800)(0.866) H=690N away from the wall

Vertical forces:

V+Tsin30°=200+300→V+400=500 V=100N up

The hinge force is neither along the beam nor straight up, so give it two unknowns (H and V) and use ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0 after the torques. The cable pulls with 800 N to hold up only 500 N, because a shallow cable has a short lever arm.

Forces and torques together

A cylinder rolling down a ramp

A 2.0 kg solid cylinder (I = ½mR²) rolls without slipping down a 30° ramp. Find its acceleration and the friction force on it. What's the smallest coefficient of static friction that lets it roll without slipping?

Two equations, used together. Along the ramp (down = +):

mgsin30°−f=ma

About the center: gravity acts at the center and the normal force points through it, so only friction makes a torque. Rolling without slipping, α = a/R:

fR=(12mR2)aR→f=12ma

Put that into the first equation:

mgsin30°=32ma→a=23(10)(0.50) a=3.3m/s2 f=12(2.0)(3.3)=3.3N up the ramp

Static friction can be at most μsN, with N = mg cos30° = 17.3 N:

μs≥3.317.3=0.19

Friction points up the ramp: it's the only force with a torque about the center, so it's what spins the cylinder faster. It's static friction (the contact point doesn't slide), so no energy is lost, and the energy method gives the same answer. A frictionless block would speed up at 5.0 m/s²; the cylinder gets less because part of the work goes into spinning. Rolling up the ramp, friction still points up the ramp: there it's slowing the spin.

Rotational energy

A rod swings down from horizontal

A uniform rod 1.2 m long (I = ⅓ML² about one end) is pivoted at one end on a frictionless axle. It's held horizontal and released. Find its angular velocity and the speed of its tip when it hangs straight down. Then find its angular acceleration at the moment it's released.

System: rod + Earth. The pivot doesn't move, so it does no work, and mechanical energy is conserved. The rod's center of mass falls half its length, 0.60 m, and all its kinetic energy is rotation about the pivot:

Mg(0.60)=12(13M(1.2)2)ω2 →6.0=0.24ω2 ω=5.0rad/s vtip=ωL=(5.0)(1.2)=6.0m/s

At release, gravity acts at the center of mass with lever arm L/2 = 0.60 m (the pivot's force has no torque):

α=τI=Mg(L/2)13ML2=3g2L=3(10)2(1.2) α=12.5rad/s2

So the tip starts with at = αL = 15 m/s², more than g.

Gravitational energy uses the height change of the center of mass, not of the tip. The torque isn't constant (its lever arm shrinks as the rod swings), so rotational kinematics can't be used here, but energy can. α is largest at release and zero at the bottom, where gravity points through the pivot; ω is largest at the bottom. Every point beyond ⅔L = 0.80 m starts out falling faster than g: a coin resting on the far end gets left behind.

Tip or slide?

Pushing a tall box high or low

A uniform box 0.60 m wide and 1.2 m tall weighs 200 N. The coefficient of static friction with the floor is 0.40. You push horizontally on its side, slowly increasing the force. Does it slide or tip first if you push 1.0 m above the floor? What if you push 0.50 m above the floor?

Sliding starts when the push beats the most static friction can give:

Fslide=μsN=(0.40)(200)=80N

Just as it starts to tip, the box rests only on its front bottom corner, so the floor's normal force and friction both act there. Take torques about that corner so they drop out. The weight acts at the center, 0.30 m back from the corner:

Ftiph=(200)(0.30)→Ftip=60h

Pushing at h = 1.0 m:

Ftip=60N<80N, so it tips

Pushing at h = 0.50 m:

Ftip=120N>80N, so it slides

Work out both thresholds and see which force is reached first. Sliding doesn't care where you push; tipping does, because a higher push has a longer lever arm about the corner. The two are equal at h = 0.30/0.40 = 0.75 m. Turned around, the lowest height at which a box tips instead of sliding tells you μs.

Falling and spinning

A yo-yo unwinding from its string

A 0.10 kg yo-yo (I = 4.0 × 10−5 kg·m²) falls from rest as its string unwinds from an axle of radius 1.0 cm. The top of the string is held still. Find the yo-yo's acceleration, the tension in the string, and its speed after falling 0.50 m.

The yo-yo both falls and spins, so write both laws for it. Falling (down = +):

mg−T=ma→1.0−T=0.10a

Spinning about its center: only the string has a torque (weight acts at the center). The string unwinds without slipping, so α = a/r:

Tr=Iar→T=Ir2a=4.0×10−5(0.010)2a=0.40a

Add the two equations:

1.0=0.50a a=2.0m/s2 T=(0.40)(2.0)=0.80N

Speed after 0.50 m:

v=2ad=2(2.0)(0.50)=1.4m/s

Energy check: ½mv² = 0.10 J and ½Iω² = ½(4.0 × 10−5)(141 rad/s)² = 0.40 J, which add to mgh = (0.10)(10)(0.50) = 0.50 J.

There's no fixed axle: the yo-yo's center moves, so it needs ΣF = ma and Στ = Iα. Because the axle is so small, the string needs a lot of tension to spin it, so the tension is close to mg and the yo-yo falls at only g/5. Four-fifths of the energy ends up as spin.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. A turntable spins steadily. Compare the angular velocity and the speed of a point at the edge with one halfway to the center.
    Answer
    Same angular velocity: every point turns through the same angle in the same time. The edge point is twice as far out, so with v = rω it moves twice as fast.
  2. A wheel speeds up from rest to 12 rad/s in 4.0 s at a steady rate. Find its angular acceleration and how many revolutions it makes.
    Answer
    α = 12/4.0 = 3.0 rad/s². Δθ = ½(0 + 12)(4.0) = 24 rad, which is 24/2π ≈ 3.8 revolutions.
  3. You push a door at the handle, 0.90 m from the hinges. How hard would you have to push at 0.30 m from the hinges to get the same torque?
    Answer
    Three times as hard. Torque = r⊥F, so one-third the lever arm needs three times the force.
  4. You pull on a 0.25 m wrench with 40 N at 90° to it. What torque do you apply? What if you pull at 30° to the wrench instead?
    Answer
    At 90°: τ = (0.25)(40) = 10 N·m. At 30°: τ = (0.25)(40) sin30° = 5.0 N·m. Only the part of the force perpendicular to the wrench turns it.
  5. A 30 kg child sits 2.0 m from the pivot of a seesaw. Where should a 40 kg child sit on the other side to balance it?
    Answer
    Torques must match: (30)(10)(2.0) = (40)(10)d, so d = 1.5 m. The heavier child sits closer to the pivot.
  6. A hoop and a solid disk have the same mass and radius. Which is harder to spin up from rest with the same torque? Why?
    Answer
    The hoop. All its mass is at the rim, far from the axis, so its rotational inertia (MR²) is twice the disk's (½MR²), and α = τ/I is half as big.
  7. A solid ball and a hollow ball of the same mass and radius race down a ramp, rolling without slipping. Which wins?
    Answer
    The solid ball. Its mass is closer to the center, so a smaller share of its energy goes into spinning, leaving more for moving down the ramp.
  8. A diver in the air pulls into a tuck and halves her rotational inertia. What happens to her angular velocity?
    Answer
    It doubles. In the air there's no torque about her center of mass, so L = Iω stays constant.
  9. A child jumps onto the edge of a spinning merry-go-round. What happens to its angular velocity? Is kinetic energy conserved?
    Answer
    It slows down: the child's mass at the rim increases the total I, and angular momentum is conserved. Kinetic energy is not conserved: this is like a sticky collision, so some is lost.
  10. A comet's farthest point from the Sun is 4 times as far as its closest point. How does its speed at the closest point compare with its speed at the farthest point?
    Answer
    At those two points the velocity is perpendicular to the line to the Sun, so mvr is the same at both. One-quarter the distance means 4 times the speed.
  11. A thin rod 1.0 m long has mass 3.0 kg and Icm = 1/12ML² about its center. Find its rotational inertia about one end.
    Answer
    Icm = (3.0)(1.0)²/12 = 0.25 kg·m². The end is d = 0.50 m from the center: I = 0.25 + (3.0)(0.50)² = 1.0 kg·m² (which matches ⅓ML²).
  12. Who's right? A bike chain runs from the 10 cm front chainring (turned by the pedals) to a 4.0 cm rear sprocket fixed to the back wheel. Ana says: “The chain links them, so the sprocket turns at the same angular velocity as the chainring.” Ben says: “The chain gives both rims the same speed, so the smaller sprocket turns faster.” Who's right? If you pedal at 1.0 rev/s, how fast does a back wheel of radius 0.35 m carry the bike?
    Answer
    Ben. Every link of the chain moves at the same speed, so the two rims share v = rω, not ω. The sprocket's radius is 0.4 times the chainring's, so it turns 2.5 times as fast: 2.5 rev/s. The back wheel is on the sprocket's axle, so it shares the sprocket's ω: 2.5 × 2π = 15.7 rad/s, and v = rω = (0.35)(15.7) ≈ 5.5 m/s. Linked by a chain or belt: same rim speed. On the same axle: same ω.
  13. A uniform 4.0 m board weighing 120 N lies on a table with 1.5 m sticking out past the edge. A 50 N cat walks out along the overhanging part. How far past the edge can it go before the board tips? Where does the table push on the board at that moment?
    Answer
    Just as it starts to tip, the board lifts off everywhere except the table's edge, so the table's whole force (120 + 50 = 170 N) acts at the edge. Take torques about the edge. The board's weight acts at its center, 2.0 − 1.5 = 0.50 m back from the edge: 50x = 120(0.50), so x = 1.2 m, 0.30 m short of the end.
  14. A 2.0 kg bar floats at rest in space. (a) Two 3.0 N forces push on it at its two ends, perpendicular to the bar and in opposite directions. (b) Just one 3.0 N force pushes perpendicular to the bar at one end. In each case, does the center of the bar start to move, and does the bar start to spin?
    Answer
    (a) The net force is zero, so the center stays put, but both forces turn the bar the same way, so it spins about its center. (b) The net force is 3.0 N, so the center accelerates at 3.0/2.0 = 1.5 m/s², and the bar spins, because the force has a torque about the center. Net force decides how the center of mass moves; net torque decides how the spin changes. You need to check both.
  15. A grinding wheel (I = 0.50 kg·m²) spins at 30 rad/s. When the motor is switched off, a steady friction torque of 0.25 N·m slows it. How long does it take to stop? How much work does friction do?
    Answer
    Angular impulse: τΔt = ΔL, and L = Iω = (0.50)(30) = 15 kg·m²/s, so Δt = 15/0.25 = 60 s. Friction removes all the kinetic energy: W = ½Iω² = ½(0.50)(30)² = 225 J. Check with W = τΔθ: α = 0.25/0.50 = 0.50 rad/s², Δθ = 30²/(2 × 0.50) = 900 rad, and (0.25)(900) = 225 J.
  16. In a lab, hanging masses spin up a platform. Students plot torque (vertical axis) against angular acceleration and get a straight line through (5.0 rad/s², 0.11 N·m) and (15 rad/s², 0.31 N·m). What do the slope and the vertical intercept tell you?
    Answer
    Slope = (0.31 − 0.11)/(15 − 5.0) = 0.020 kg·m²: from τ = Iα, that's the platform's rotational inertia. The intercept is 0.11 − (0.020)(5.0) = 0.010 N·m: the torque needed before it speeds up at all, which is the friction torque in the axle. The line really says τstring − τfriction = Iα.
  17. Three identical solid cylinders start from rest at the same height on three identical ramps. Ramp A has enough friction for rolling without slipping. Ramp B is slick, so its cylinder rolls but also slips. Ramp C is frictionless. Rank their speeds at the bottom, and their total kinetic energies.
    Answer
    Speed: C > B > A. On C nothing exerts a torque about the center, so it doesn't spin at all and all its energy is translational. B's kinetic friction is weaker than the static friction on A, so B spins less and more of its energy goes into moving down the ramp. Total kinetic energy: A = C > B. Static friction on A does no dissipative work and C has no friction, so both end with all of mgh. On B the contact point slides, so kinetic friction turns some energy into thermal energy.
  18. Who's right? A 0.20 kg puck on a frictionless table moves in a circle of radius 0.60 m at 2.0 m/s, held by a string that passes down through a hole at the center. Someone under the table pulls the string until the radius is 0.30 m. Kai says: “The string always pulls toward the hole, at right angles to the motion, so it does no work. The puck keeps its 2.0 m/s.” Lena says: “The string's pull has no torque about the hole, so mvr stays the same and the puck speeds up to 4.0 m/s. The string does work on it.” Who's right? How much work is done?
    Answer
    Lena. The pull points at the hole, so angular momentum about the hole is conserved: (0.20)(2.0)(0.60) = (0.20)v(0.30) gives v = 4.0 m/s. Kai's mistake: while the string is being pulled in, the puck spirals inward, so it moves partly along the string and the pull does positive work. K goes from ½(0.20)(2.0)² = 0.40 J to ½(0.20)(4.0)² = 1.6 J, so the person pulling does 1.2 J of work. If the speed stayed at 2.0 m/s, mvr would halve, which would need a torque.
