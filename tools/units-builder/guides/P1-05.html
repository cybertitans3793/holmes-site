Study guide

Unit 5 · Circular Motion and Gravity

How to use this: read the key ideas, then cover each worked example and try it yourself before reading the solution. Do the self-check questions on paper before you open the answers, mark the ones you miss, and come back to them in a day or two without looking. Rereading won't do much; answering questions will.

Key ideas

  • Moving in a circle at constant speed is still accelerating: the velocity's direction keeps changing. The acceleration points toward the center, ac = v²/r.
  • A real force (or several) must point toward the center: tension, friction, a normal force, gravity. “Centripetal force” is the name for the net inward force, not an extra force.
  • Speeding up or slowing down on a circle (like a ball on a string in a vertical circle) adds a tangential acceleration along the path. The total acceleration is the vector sum of the tangential and centripetal parts.
  • Vertical circles: at the top, gravity points toward the center and helps; at the bottom it points away, so whatever holds the object up has to push or pull harder.
  • Apparent weight is what a scale or seat pushes on you (the normal force). It changes around a loop or over a hill; your true weight mg doesn't.
  • Any two masses attract: Fg = Gm₁m₂/r², with r measured center to center. A sphere or planet acts like a point mass at its center for anything outside it.
  • Gravitational field strength g = Fg/m = GM/r²: the force on each kilogram placed at that point (N/kg, the same as m/s²). Fields from two masses add as vectors.
  • In a circular orbit, gravity is the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r. Solve that for whatever the question asks.
  • Kepler: orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one focus; a planet sweeps equal areas in equal times (fastest when closest); T² ∝ r³ for everything orbiting the same central body.
  • The mass that resists acceleration (inertial mass) and the mass that gravity pulls on (gravitational mass) are the same, which is why everything falls with the same g.
  • Astronauts in orbit still feel gravity. They float because they and their spacecraft are falling together.

Common mistakes

  • Drawing a “centripetal force” or “centrifugal force” arrow on a free-body diagram. Only draw real forces.
  • Saying a released object flies outward. It moves off along the tangent, in a straight line.
  • Using the height above the surface as r. Gravity's r is measured from the planet's center.
  • Forgetting to square: 3× the distance gives 1/9 the force, not ⅓.
  • Plugging in rpm or revolutions per second as if it were a speed. Find T in seconds first.
  • Writing N = mg over a hill, in a dip or in a loop. N = mg only when nothing accelerates vertically: over a crest N < mg, in a dip N > mg.
  • Assuming a bigger circle always means a smaller centripetal acceleration. At the same speed, yes (v²/r); on something that turns as one piece, like a merry-go-round, the outer rider has the same period, moves faster and needs more (4π²r/T²).
  • Saying a pendulum has zero acceleration at the end of its swing. Its speed is zero for an instant, but gravity's part along the path, g sin θ, is not.
  • Writing N = mg cos θ on a banked curve. That is the ramp result, where the acceleration runs along the slope. On a banked curve the acceleration is horizontal, so N cos θ = mg and N is more than mg.
  • Putting the orbiting object's mass into v = √(GM/r) or the T²-vs-r³ slope. M is always the mass being orbited; the satellite's or moon's own mass cancels.
  • Assuming a bigger planet has weaker surface gravity because you are farther from its center. Its mass grows too: at the same density, mass goes as r³, so g = GM/r² grows in proportion to r.

How to solve a circular motion problem

  1. Find the circle. Where is its center, and what is r?
  2. Draw a free-body diagram with real forces only. No “centripetal force” arrow.
  3. Call “toward the center” positive. Forces pointing away from the center are negative.
  4. Write Newton's 2nd law along the radius: ΣFtoward center = mv²/r. Add a second equation for any direction with no acceleration (often vertical: ΣF = 0).
  5. Substitute the force rules (mg, μN, Gm₁m₂/r²) and solve, in symbols first.
  6. Check it makes sense. Do the units work? Does the answer change the right way if v or r gets bigger?
Vertical circle

Ball on a string: tension at the top and bottom

A 0.50 kg ball on a string swings in a vertical circle of radius 0.80 m. It moves at 4.0 m/s at the top and again at the bottom. Find the tension at each point.

The inward force needed (same speed at top and bottom):

mv2r=(0.50)(4.0)20.80=10N

Top (the center is down, so tension and gravity both point inward):

T+mg=mv2r →T=10−(0.50)(10) Ttop=5N

Bottom (the center is up, so gravity points outward):

T−mg=mv2r →T=10+5 Tbottom=15N

At the top, gravity supplies part of the inward force, so the string pulls less. At the bottom, the string has to hold the ball up and pull it toward the center. At the same speed, the difference is always 2mg.

Flat curve with friction

What coefficient of friction does the car need?

A 1200 kg car rounds a flat, unbanked curve of radius 50 m at 15 m/s. What is the smallest coefficient of static friction that keeps it from sliding?

Vertical: N − mg = 0, so N = mg. Toward the center, friction is the only inward force:

fs=mv2r fs≤μsN=μsmg

For the smallest μₛ, friction is at its maximum:

μsmg=mv2r →μs=v2gr μs=(15)2(10)(50) μs=0.45

The mass cancels, so a truck needs the same μs at the same speed and radius. Doubling the speed would need four times the friction coefficient.

Gravity by ratios

Surface gravity on another planet

A planet has 3 times Earth's mass and 2 times Earth's radius. What is the gravitational field strength at its surface? (Earth: g = 10 N/kg.)

g=GMr2 →g∝Mr2 gplanet=gEarth×322=10×34 gplanet=7.5N/kg

No need to look up G or Earth's mass: “shrink” Earth into the new planet. Multiply by the mass factor, divide by the radius factor squared.

Orbit

A satellite's speed and period

A satellite orbits Earth in a circle with radius r = 1.28 × 10⁷ m (twice Earth's radius). Earth's mass is 6.0 × 10²⁴ kg. Find the satellite's speed and period.

Gravity is the centripetal force:

GMmr2=mv2r →v=GMr v=(6.67×10−11)(6.0×1024)1.28×107 v=5.6×103m/s T=2πrv=2π(1.28×107)5.6×103 T=1.4×104s≈4.0 hours

The satellite's mass m cancels. Only Earth's mass and the orbit radius matter, and a bigger orbit means a slower speed and a longer period.

Speeding up on a circle

A pendulum partway through its swing

A 0.20 kg bob hangs on a 1.25 m string. On its way down, when the string is 30° from vertical, the bob moves at 2.5 m/s. Find the tension in the string and the bob's acceleration (size and direction).

Use two directions: along the string (toward the pivot, which is the center) and along the path. Tension lies along the string. Gravity splits into mg cos 30° pointing away from the center and mg sin 30° along the path.

Along the string, toward the center positive:

T−mgcos30°=mv2r T=(0.20)(10)(0.866)+(0.20)(2.5)21.25 T=1.73+1.0 T≈2.7N

Along the path only gravity acts, so it speeds the bob up. The two parts of the acceleration:

at=gsin30°=5.0m/s2 ac=v2r=(2.5)21.25=5.0m/s2 a=at2+ac2=5.02+5.02 a≈7.1m/s2

The two parts are equal, so a points 45° between the string (toward the pivot) and the path (forward and down).

The acceleration does not point at the center: its center part turns the bob, its path part speeds it up. Tension is more than mg cos θ because part of it does the turning. At the end of the swing the speed is zero for an instant, so ac = 0 and T = mg cos θ, but at = g sin θ is still there.

Apparent weight

A car over the crest of a hill

A 1000 kg car drives over the top of a hill at 15 m/s. The top of the hill is curved with a radius of 40 m. What normal force does the road exert on the car? How fast could the car go before it leaves the road?

The center of the curve is below the car, so call down positive. Gravity points toward the center; the normal force points away:

mg−N=mv2r N=(1000)(10)−(1000)(15)240 N=10000−5625 N≈4400N(0.44 of its weight)

The car leaves the road when the road no longer needs to push: N = 0, and gravity alone supplies the inward force.

mg=mvmax2r→vmax=gr=(10)(40) vmax=20m/s

Over a crest N < mg, so the riders feel lighter (here 0.44 of their weight). Driving faster or over a sharper crest (smaller r) lowers N until the car leaves the road and becomes a projectile; the mass cancels. In a dip it's the other way round: the center is above, N − mg = mv²/r, and at the same speed and radius N ≈ 15 600 N, so riders feel 1.56 times heavier.

Friction holds you up

The spinning rotor ride

On a rotor ride, people stand against the inside wall of a drum of radius 2.5 m. Once it spins fast enough, the floor drops away. The coefficient of static friction between a rider and the wall is 0.40. What is the slowest speed that keeps riders from sliding down, and the longest period that gives it?

The center is on the drum's axis, straight in from the rider. The wall's normal force points inward: it is the only inward force. Static friction from the wall points up, and nothing accelerates vertically.

vertical: fs=mginward: N=mv2r

At the slowest speed, friction is at its maximum, fs = μsN:

mg=μsmv2r→v=grμs=(10)(2.5)0.40 v≈7.9m/s T=2πrv=2π(2.5)7.9 T≈2.0s per turn

Here friction is vertical and holds you up, while the normal force does the turning. The mass cancels, so every rider is equally safe: for a 70 kg rider N = 1750 N, so friction can reach 700 N, exactly the weight. A slipperier wall or a smaller drum needs faster spinning. Spinning faster than the minimum raises N, but friction stays at mg.

Banked curve

Tilting the road so no friction is needed

Engineers want cars to take a curve of radius 60 m at 15 m/s even on an icy day, with no help from friction. At what angle θ should the road be banked? What normal force does the road exert on a 1500 kg car taking the curve at that speed?

With no friction, only two forces act: mg straight down and N perpendicular to the road. The car moves in a horizontal circle, so its acceleration is horizontal, toward the center. Use horizontal and vertical axes, not axes along the slope. N tilts toward the center, so it splits into N cos θ (up) and N sin θ (toward the center).

vertical: Ncosθ=mg toward center: Nsinθ=mv2r

Divide the second equation by the first; N and m cancel:

tanθ=v2gr=(15)2(10)(60)=0.375 θ≈21° N=mgcosθ=(1500)(10)cos20.6° N≈16000N(more than mg = 15 000 N)

Check: N sin θ = (16 020)(0.351) ≈ 5600 N, and mv²/r = (1500)(15²)/60 = 5625 N. ✓

On a banked curve the normal force does the turning: its sideways part is the centripetal force. Because the acceleration is horizontal, N is bigger than mg, not mg cos θ as on a ramp. The mass cancels, so every car (and truck) gets the same “design speed”; only v and r set the angle. Slower than 15 m/s, the car would slide down the bank unless friction holds it; faster, it would slide up and out.

Orbit data → mass

Weighing a planet with its moons (T² vs r³)

A space probe tracks four moons in circular orbits around a distant planet:

r (× 10⁸ m)2.0 · 4.0 · 6.0 · 8.0
T (× 10⁵ s)0.97 · 2.75 · 5.06 · 7.79

Show that T² is proportional to r³, and use a graph to find the planet's mass.

Start from physics, not from the data. Gravity is the centripetal force, and v = 2πr/T for a circle:

GMmr2=mv2r=m(2πr/T)2r

Put the two things you measured (T and r) on one side and the constants on the other:

T2r3=4π2GM

So a graph of T² (y-axis) against r³ (x-axis) should be a straight line through the origin with slope 4π²/GM. Square and cube the data: r³ = 8, 64, 216, 512 (× 10²⁴ m³) and T² = 0.94, 7.56, 25.6, 60.7 (× 10¹⁰ s²). The points do lie on a line through the origin. Slope from the origin to the last point:

slope=60.7×1010s2512×1024m3=1.19×10−15s2/m3

Set the slope equal to what the equation says it stands for:

M=4π2G·slope M=39.5(6.67×10−11)(1.19×10−15) M≈5.0×1026kg

A plot of T against r curves, so you can't read a constant off it. Rearrange the equation until the two measured quantities sit on one side; that tells you what to graph, and the other side tells you what the slope means. Every moon gives the same slope because the moons' masses cancel: the line measures the planet being orbited, never the moons. For a body whose mass is already known, the same slope gives G instead.

Adding gravitational pulls

Where Earth's and the Moon's pulls cancel

Earth's mass is 81 times the Moon's, and their centers are 3.84 × 10⁸ m apart. A spacecraft travels straight from Earth toward the Moon. At what distance from Earth's center is the net gravitational force on it zero?

The point must lie between them, where the two pulls point opposite ways. Call its distance from Earth's center x, so it is d − x from the Moon's. Set the two pulls equal (the spacecraft's mass m cancels):

GMEmx2=GMMm(d−x)2 →x2(d−x)2=MEMM=81

Take the square root of both sides:

xd−x=9→x=9d−9x→x=910d x=0.9(3.84×108)≈3.5×108m

That is 9 times as far from Earth as from the Moon (about 3.8 × 10⁷ m from the Moon's center).

Pulls from two bodies add as vectors, so “zero net force” means equal sizes in opposite directions. Because force goes as 1/r², the distances compare as the square root of the mass ratio: 9, not 81. The balance point sits much closer to the lighter body. Anywhere nearer Earth, Earth's pull wins; past it, the spacecraft is pulled toward the Moon.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. A puck on a string slides in a circle on frictionless ice. The string snaps when the puck is moving due north. Describe the puck's path afterward.
    Answer
    A straight line due north at constant speed. Once the string breaks there is no net horizontal force, so nothing changes the velocity. It does not fly outward.
  2. A car turns left on a flat road at constant speed. Which force on the car points toward the center of the turn? On an icy day the car takes the same turn too fast. What happens, and why?
    Answer
    Static friction from the road on the tires. On ice the most friction the road can supply is less than mv²/r, so the car can't curve that sharply: it follows a wider, straighter path than the road. Nothing pushes it outward; it just isn't pulled in enough.
  3. A washing-machine drum of radius 0.25 m spins at 10 revolutions per second. Find the centripetal acceleration of a sock stuck to the wall. Which force provides it?
    Answer
    T = 1/10 = 0.10 s. v = 2πr/T = 2π(0.25)/0.10 ≈ 15.7 m/s. ac = v²/r ≈ 990 m/s², about 100 times g. The drum wall's normal force pushes the sock toward the center.
  4. You swing a bucket of water in a vertical circle of radius 0.80 m. What is the slowest speed at the top that keeps the water in the bucket?
    Answer
    At the slowest speed the bucket pushes on the water with zero force, so gravity alone provides the centripetal force: mg = mv²/r, so v = √(gr) = √(10 × 0.80) ≈ 2.8 m/s. Any slower and gravity pulls the water into a tighter curve than the bucket follows, so it falls out.
  5. Earth pulls on the Moon with a gravitational force F. How hard does the Moon pull on Earth? Why is it the Moon that does most of the moving?
    Answer
    Exactly F (Newton's 3rd law). Earth's mass is about 81 times the Moon's, so the same force gives Earth about 1/81 of the Moon's acceleration.
  6. Satellite A is 2 Earth radii from Earth's center; satellite B is 6 Earth radii from the center. How does the gravitational field strength at B compare with that at A?
    Answer
    g ∝ 1/r². B is 3 times as far from the center, so its field strength is (⅓)² = 1/9 of A's.
  7. IB Sketch the gravitational field lines around Earth as a whole, then just above your classroom floor. What do the direction and spacing of the lines tell you?
    Answer
    Around Earth: lines point radially inward toward the center and spread apart with distance (the field gets weaker). Near the floor: parallel, evenly spaced lines pointing straight down (a uniform field). Closer lines mean a stronger field; the arrows show the direction of the force on a mass.
  8. Mars orbits the Sun at about 1.5 times Earth's orbital radius. Estimate the length of a Martian year.
    Answer
    T² ∝ r³, so TMars/TEarth = 1.53/2 ≈ 1.8. A Martian year is about 1.8 Earth years (the real value is 1.88).
  9. A comet moves on a long, thin ellipse around the Sun. Where in its orbit is it moving fastest, and which law tells you?
    Answer
    Closest to the Sun. Kepler's 2nd law: the line from the Sun sweeps equal areas in equal times, so where that line is short the comet must cover more distance in the same time.
  10. Astronauts on the space station, about 400 km up, float around the cabin. Is gravity zero up there? Explain why they float.
    Answer
    No. 400 km is small compared with Earth's 6400 km radius, so gravity there is still about 90% of its surface value. The astronauts float because they and the station are in free fall together, both moving in the same orbit, so nothing pushes on them.
  11. Who's right? A go-kart takes a half-circle turn on a flat track, as fast as its tires allow without sliding (μs = 0.80). Maya says the wide outside line (r = 40 m) gets around the turn sooner, because the kart can go faster there. Jordan says the tight inside line (r = 20 m) is sooner. Who's right?
    Answer
    Jordan. Friction is the inward force, so μsmg = mv²/r and vmax = √(μsgr). The time for a half circle is t = πr/v = π√(r/(μsg)), which grows with r: doubling r doubles the distance but raises the top speed only by √2. Inside: v ≈ 12.6 m/s, t ≈ 5.0 s. Outside: v ≈ 17.9 m/s, t ≈ 7.0 s. Maya is right that the kart is faster on the outside, but faster doesn't mean sooner here.
  12. A 0.20 kg ball on a 0.50 m string swings in a horizontal circle, with the string at 30° from vertical. Find the radius of the circle, the tension and the ball's speed.
    Answer
    The radius is r = L sin 30° = 0.25 m, not the string length. Vertical (no acceleration): T cos 30° = mg, so T = 2.0/0.866 ≈ 2.3 N. Inward: T sin 30° = mv²/r, so 1.15 = (0.20)v²/0.25, v² ≈ 1.44 and v ≈ 1.2 m/s. The tension does two jobs: its vertical part holds the ball up and its horizontal part turns it.
  13. A planet is made of the same rock as Earth (same density) but has twice Earth's radius. What is the gravitational field strength at its surface?
    Answer
    20 N/kg. Same density means mass goes as volume, r³: twice the radius gives 2³ = 8 times the mass. Then g = GM/r² changes by 8/2² = 2, so g = 2 × 10 = 20 N/kg. At the same density, surface gravity is proportional to the radius.
  14. “Weigh” Earth: use g = 10 N/kg at the surface, Earth's radius 6.4 × 10⁶ m and G to find Earth's mass.
    Answer
    At the surface g = GM/R², so M = gR²/G = (10)(6.4 × 10⁶)²/(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹) ≈ 6.1 × 10²⁴ kg. Measuring G in a lab (with two lead balls) is what made this possible: g and R had been known long before.
  15. A ring-shaped space station of radius 90 m spins so that astronauts standing on the inside of the outer wall feel their normal weight (an inward acceleration of 10 m/s²). How long should one turn take? Which force pushes the astronauts toward the center?
    Answer
    ac = 4π²r/T², so T = 2π√(r/ac) = 2π√(90/10) = 2π(3) ≈ 19 s per turn (about 3 turns per minute). The floor (the outer wall) pushes them inward with a normal force; that push is what they feel as weight. Their heads, closer to the center, have a smaller r and feel slightly less.
  16. Who's right? Riley says the Moon pulls harder on Earth than the Sun does, because the Moon is about 400 times closer and it is the Moon that raises the tides. Sam says the Sun pulls harder, because it is so much more massive. Use the Sun's mass 2.0 × 10³⁰ kg at 1.5 × 10¹¹ m and the Moon's mass 7.3 × 10²² kg at 3.8 × 10⁸ m. Who's right?
    Answer
    Sam. In F = GMm/r², Earth's mass and G are the same in both, so compare M/r². The Sun has 2.0 × 10³⁰ / 7.3 × 10²² ≈ 2.7 × 10⁷ times the mass; its distance is (1.5 × 10¹¹)/(3.8 × 10⁸) ≈ 395 times as large, which divides the force by 395² ≈ 1.6 × 10⁵. Net: the Sun pulls about 2.7 × 10⁷ / 1.6 × 10⁵ ≈ 180 times harder (3.6 × 10²² N against 2.0 × 10²⁰ N with Earth's mass 6.0 × 10²⁴ kg). Riley's reasoning about distance is right, but the mass ratio wins. That pull is what keeps Earth in orbit around the Sun.
  17. Who's right? A curve is banked for 15 m/s, using tan θ = v²/(gr). On an icy day a car takes it at only 10 m/s. Ana says it will slide down the bank, toward the inside. Ben says it will slide up and out, because at any speed the car is pushed outward. Who's right? Point to the step in the derivation that tells you.
    Answer
    Ana. With no friction, the vertical equation N cos θ = mg fixes N, so the inward part N sin θ = mg tan θ is the same at any speed. At 10 m/s the car needs only mv²/r, which is less than mg tan θ: the inward push is too big, so the car curves in tighter than the road and slides down the bank. (At the design speed the two match exactly; faster, the inward push is too small and the car drifts up and out.) Nothing pushes the car outward; Ben is describing a centrifugal force, which isn't a real force.
