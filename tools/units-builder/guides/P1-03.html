Study guide

Unit 3 · Newton’s Third Law

How to use this: read the key ideas, then cover each worked example and try it yourself before reading the solution. Do the self-check questions on paper before you open the answers, mark the ones you miss, and come back to them in a day or two without looking. Rereading won't do much; answering questions will.

Key ideas

  • Forces come in pairs. When A pushes or pulls on B, B pushes or pulls back on A. Every force is one half of an interaction.
  • A third-law pair is the same type of force, equal in size, opposite in direction, and acting on two different objects. If you can say “A on B,” its partner is “B on A.”
  • Third-law pairs never cancel, because they act on different objects. Only forces on the same object can add to zero.
  • A book's weight and the table's normal force on it are not a third-law pair: both act on the book, and they're different types. The partner of the book's weight is the book's gravitational pull on Earth.
  • Equal forces don't mean equal accelerations. In a collision a truck and a car push each other equally hard, but a = F/m, so the smaller car gets the bigger acceleration.
  • A system is whatever group of objects you choose. Forces between objects inside it are internal; they come in pairs and cancel for the system as a whole.
  • For objects moving together, treat them as one system: ΣFexternal = mtotala. Then look at one object on its own to find an internal force (a contact force or a tension).
  • An ideal string (no mass, doesn't stretch) has the same tension all along it, even over an ideal pulley. Everything tied to it moves with the same size of acceleration.
  • A string holding an accelerating hanging mass does not pull with mg. If the mass speeds up downward, the tension is less than its weight.
  • Center of mass: the average position of a system's mass, xcm = Σmx/Σm. It sits closer to the heavier objects.
  • Only external forces can accelerate the center of mass. If two skaters push apart on frictionless ice, they fly off in opposite directions, but their center of mass stays put.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the bigger or faster object pushes harder in a collision. The forces are always equal; the effects aren't.
  • Pairing the normal force with the weight. They're both on the same object, so they can't be a third-law pair.
  • “The forces are equal and opposite, so nothing can move.” The pair acts on two different objects, so each object can still have a nonzero net force.
  • Setting a hanging mass's string tension equal to mg when the system is accelerating.
  • Using the system's total mass to find a force on just one object, or the other way round.
  • Including internal forces in the system's ΣF. They cancel.
  • Thinking the “reaction” comes after the “action.” Both forces come from one interaction and exist at the same instant; neither causes the other.
  • Assuming the center of mass must be inside the object. For a ring, an L-shape or a bent-over high jumper, it's in the empty space.
  • Thinking moving the origin moves the center of mass. It changes the number you get for xcm, not where the center of mass actually is.
  • Saying the winning side of a tug-of-war pulls harder on the rope. A light rope pulls both ends equally; the winner is the side the floor pushes harder.
  • Using the constant-acceleration equations when the net force changes as things move, like a rope sliding off a table. They only work when a is constant.

How to solve a connected-objects problem

  1. Do they move together? Touching blocks, or objects joined by a tight string, share the same size of acceleration.
  2. Treat them all as one system. Draw only the external forces, and pick the direction of motion as + (around a pulley, + follows the string).
  3. Find a: ΣFext = mtotala.
  4. Pick one object and draw its own free-body diagram. Now the contact force or tension is an outside force on it.
  5. Use ΣF = ma for that object with the same a to find the internal force.
  6. Check: does it make sense? A string's tension should lie between zero and the hanging weight if the hanging mass is speeding up downward.

Finding a third-law pair

  • Name the force as “type of A on B”, for example “gravitational force of Earth on the ball.”
  • Swap the two objects: “gravitational force of the ball on Earth.” That's its partner.
  • Same type, same size, opposite direction, on the other object. If any of these fails, it isn't a pair.

Choosing the system

  • Want the acceleration? Make the system big, so the unknown internal forces drop out.
  • Want a force between two objects? Make the system one of those objects, so that force becomes external.
System, then one object

Pushing two blocks

On a frictionless floor, you push a 3.0 kg block with 12 N, and it pushes a 1.0 kg block in front of it. Find their acceleration and the force between the blocks. What if you pushed the 1.0 kg block from the other side instead?

Both blocks as one system:

a=Fmtotal=123.0+1.0 a=3.0m/s2

The 1.0 kg block alone (the contact force is the only push on it):

Fcontact=(1.0)(3.0)=3.0N

Pushing from the other side, the 3.0 kg block is in front:

Fcontact=(3.0)(3.0)=9.0N

The acceleration is the same either way, but the contact force isn't: it only has to accelerate the block in front. The 3.0 kg block feels the 3.0 N (or 9.0 N) push back on it, too.

Pulley

A block on a table pulled by a hanging mass

A 2.0 kg block on a frictionless table is tied to a string that runs over a pulley at the edge to a hanging 1.0 kg mass. Find the acceleration and the tension in the string.

Both masses as one system, + along the string. The only unbalanced outside force is the hanging weight:

a=mhanggmtotal=(1.0)(10)2.0+1.0 a=3.3m/s2

The block on the table alone (tension is the only horizontal force):

T=(2.0)(3.33)=6.7N

Check with the hanging mass: 10 − 6.7 = (1.0)(3.3) ✓

The tension is less than the hanging mass's 10 N weight. If it were 10 N, the hanging mass would have no net force and wouldn't accelerate.

Third law

A truck and a car collide

A 2000 kg truck and a 1000 kg car collide head-on. At one instant the truck pushes on the car with 20 000 N. How hard does the car push on the truck? Compare their accelerations.

Third-law pair: the car pushes back on the truck with the same 20 000 N, the opposite way.

atruck=Fmtruck=200002000 atruck=10m/s2 acar=Fmcar=200001000 acar=20m/s2

Same force, but the car has half the mass, so it gets twice the acceleration. That's why the people in the smaller car are in more danger.

Static friction

A box on an accelerating truck

A box sits in the bed of a pickup truck. The coefficient of static friction between them is 0.40. What is the greatest acceleration the truck can have without the box sliding?

Static friction is the only horizontal force on the box, and it points forward:

fs=ma fs≤μsmg

At the largest acceleration, friction is at its maximum:

μsmg=mamax →amax=μsg amax=(0.40)(10)=4.0m/s2

Friction pushes the box forward, the way the truck accelerates. If the truck accelerates harder, nothing pushes the box backward: friction just can't speed it up fast enough, so it slides back relative to the truck.

Center of mass

Where's the balance point?

A light rod 1.0 m long has a 2.0 kg mass on its left end (x = 0) and a 3.0 kg mass on its right end (x = 1.0 m). Where is the center of mass?

xcm=m1x1+m2x2m1+m2 xcm=(2.0)(0)+(3.0)(1.0)2.0+3.0 xcm=0.60m from the left end

It's closer to the heavier mass. If the rod were thrown, it would spin, but the center of mass would follow a smooth path, as if all 5.0 kg were there.

Choosing the subsystem

Lifting a stack of hanging blocks

Three blocks hang one below another: 4.0 kg on top, 3.0 kg in the middle, 1.0 kg at the bottom. Rope A pulls the top block up, rope B joins the top and middle blocks, and rope C joins the middle and bottom blocks. Rope A is pulled so the whole stack speeds up upward at 2.0 m/s². Find the tension in each rope.

To find a rope's tension, choose a system that the rope pulls on from outside: everything below it. + is up. For any such system, T − mbelowg = mbelowa:

T=mbelow(g+a)=mbelow(12m/s2)

Rope A holds all three blocks (8.0 kg), rope B the bottom two (4.0 kg), rope C only the bottom block (1.0 kg):

TA=(8.0)(12)=96N TB=(4.0)(12)=48N TC=(1.0)(12)=12N

Check with the top block alone (A pulls up, B and its 40 N weight pull down): 96 − 48 − 40 = 8 N = (4.0)(2.0) ✓

The top rope carries the most, because it has to accelerate everything below it. Every tension is more than the weight it holds up, since the stack is speeding up upward. At constant velocity they'd be 80 N, 40 N and 10 N.

Friction on a system

Measuring friction with a hanging mass

In a lab, a 4.0 kg block on a table is tied by a string over a pulley to a hanging 2.0 kg mass. Released from rest, the hanging mass falls 0.80 m in 1.0 s. Find the coefficient of kinetic friction between the block and the table, and the tension in the string.

First the acceleration, from rest at constant a:

d=12at2→a=2(0.80)(1.0)2=1.6m/s2

Both masses as one system, + along the string. The hanging weight pulls forward and the table's friction on the block pulls back; both are external. The tension is internal and drops out:

mhangg−μkmblockg=mtotala 20−μk(40)=(6.0)(1.6)=9.6 μk=20−9.640=10.440 μk=0.26

The hanging mass alone (its weight down, tension up):

T=mhang(g−a)=(2.0)(8.4)=17N

Check with the block: 16.8 − 10.4 = 6.4 N = (4.0)(1.6) ✓

Friction acts on only one object, but it's still an outside force on the system, so it goes into ΣFext. This is how you measure μk in the lab: time the fall, then let Newton's second law do the rest. And if the table's greatest static friction, μsmblockg, were 20 N or more, the block would never start moving.

Center of mass in 2D

The center of mass of an L-shape

An L-shape is made of two uniform boards, each 0.20 m thick. A 3.0 kg board, 0.60 m long, lies along the x-axis with its corner at the origin. A 2.0 kg board, 0.40 m tall, stands on its left end. Find the center of mass.

Replace each uniform board by a point mass at its own center: the flat board's center is at (0.30 m, 0.10 m); the upright board's is at (0.10 m, 0.40 m). Then do x and y separately:

xcm=(3.0)(0.30)+(2.0)(0.10)5.0 xcm=0.22m ycm=(3.0)(0.10)+(2.0)(0.40)5.0 ycm=0.22m

The flat board only reaches y = 0.20 m and the upright one only reaches x = 0.20 m, so (0.22 m, 0.22 m) is in the empty corner of the L, outside both boards. The center of mass doesn't have to be inside the material: think of a ring, a boomerang, or a high jumper arched over the bar.

Object vs system

Who wins a tug-of-war?

An 80 kg adult and a 30 kg child have a tug-of-war on a gym floor. The rope's tension is 150 N, and the adult is winning: both people slide toward the adult's side with an acceleration of 0.50 m/s². Find the floor's friction force on each person. Then judge two claims. Claim 1: “The adult wins because he pulls harder on the rope.” Claim 2: “The adult wins because the floor pushes him harder than it pushes the child.”

+ is toward the adult's side. The child alone: the rope pulls her forward with 150 N, and the floor's friction pushes back on her feet:

T−fchild=mchilda 150−fchild=(30)(0.50) fchild=135N

The adult alone: the rope pulls him backward (toward the child) with 150 N, and the floor pushes his feet forward:

fadult−T=madulta fadult−150=(80)(0.50) fadult=190N

Check with the whole system (adult + rope + child). The rope's pulls are internal and drop out; only the two friction forces are external: 190 − 135 = 55 N = (110)(0.50) ✓

Claim 1 is wrong: a light rope pulls both people with the same 150 N, so neither one pulls harder on it. Claim 2 is right. Looking at the system makes it clear: the only outside horizontal forces are the floor's pushes, so the side with more grip wins. That's why a tug-of-war on ice is so hard to win.

Ratio reasoning

A runner tied to a box

A 60 kg runner is tied by a long, slack rope to a 40 kg box on a smooth floor. The ground pushes the runner forward with a steady 120 N. (1) Before the rope goes tight, what is the runner's acceleration? (2) The rope goes tight and the runner drags the box. Find the new acceleration. (3) The box then slides onto a rough patch, where friction on it is 30 N. Find the acceleration and the rope tension in parts 2 and 3.

(1) The runner alone:

a1=12060=2.0m/s2

(2) Runner + box as one system. The same 120 N outside push now accelerates 100 kg, so the acceleration drops by the factor 60/100:

a2=(2.0)60100=1.2m/s2

(3) Same mass, but the net outside force falls from 120 N to 120 − 30 = 90 N, a factor of 3/4:

a3=(1.2)34=0.90m/s2

Tension: use the box alone, since the rope is an outside force on it.

Part 2:T=(40)(1.2)=48N Part 3:T−30=(40)(0.90)→T=66N

Check part 3 with the runner: 120 − 66 = 54 N = (60)(0.90) ✓

Ratios do the work: more mass with the same net force scales a down by the mass ratio; less net force with the same mass scales it down by the force ratio. Notice the surprise in part 3: the system slows its speeding-up, yet the rope pulls harder, because now it has to fight the friction on the box as well.

Internal vs external

A train with friction on every car

An engine pulls three cars (3000 kg, 2000 kg and 1000 kg, in that order) with 9000 N. Each car feels rolling friction equal to 0.020 times its weight. Find the acceleration, the tension T1 in the coupling behind the first car, and T2 in the coupling behind the second car.

All three cars as one system. The couplings are internal; the outside forces are the 9000 N pull and the friction on all 6000 kg, (0.020)(60 000) = 1200 N:

a=9000−12006000 a=1.3m/s2

For T1, choose the last two cars (3000 kg): T1 is now external, along with their 600 N of friction:

T1−600=(3000)(1.3) T1=4500N

For T2, choose the last car alone (1000 kg, 200 N of friction):

T2−200=(1000)(1.3) T2=1500N

Check with the first car alone: 9000 − 4500 − 600 = 3900 N = (3000)(1.3) ✓

A coupling's tension is internal to the whole train but external to the cars behind it, so pick the system that puts it on the outside. Here T1 is 3000/6000 = ½ of the 9000 N and T2 is 1000/6000 = ⅙ of it, exactly as if there were no friction: when friction is proportional to each car's weight, each coupling's share is just the fraction of the mass behind it.

Changing force

A heavy rope sliding off a table

A uniform 2.0 kg rope, 1.0 m long, lies on a frictionless table with part of it hanging over a smooth edge. Find the rope's acceleration when 0.25 m hangs over, and when 0.50 m does. Find the tension in the rope at the edge in each case.

The whole rope as the system. The table's normal force balances the weight of the part lying on it, so the only unbalanced outside force is the weight of the hanging part, (y/L)Mg:

yLMg=Ma→a=yLg y=0.25m:a=(0.25)(10)=2.5m/s2 y=0.50m:a=(0.50)(10)=5.0m/s2

For the tension at the edge, choose the part still on the table. The rope at the edge is the only horizontal force on it:

y=0.25m:T=(1.5kg)(2.5)=3.8N y=0.50m:T=(1.0kg)(5.0)=5.0N

Check at y = 0.25 m with the hanging part (0.50 kg, 5.0 N weight): 5.0 − 3.75 = 1.25 N = (0.50)(2.5) ✓

As more rope goes over, the pulling force grows, so the acceleration isn't constant: it rises in a straight line with y, up to g when the last bit leaves the table. That means the constant-acceleration equations don't apply, and a v–t graph of the rope curves upward. Choosing the system is still the key: the whole rope gives a, and part of the rope gives the tension.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. You push on a wall with 50 N. How hard does the wall push on you? Why don't the two forces cancel?
    Answer
    50 N, back toward you. They act on different objects (one on the wall, one on you), so they can't cancel. Only forces on the same object can.
  2. An apple sits on a table. Name the two forces on the apple and the third-law partner of each.
    Answer
    Earth's gravity pulls the apple down; its partner is the apple's gravity pulling Earth up. The table pushes the apple up (normal force); its partner is the apple pushing down on the table. The apple's weight and the normal force balance, but they aren't a pair.
  3. A horse pulls a cart. If the cart pulls back on the horse just as hard, how can they ever start moving?
    Answer
    The two forces act on different objects. The cart moves because the horse's pull on it is bigger than the friction on the cart. The horse moves because the ground pushes it forward (friction on its hooves) harder than the cart pulls it back.
  4. A 3.0 kg mass and a 2.0 kg mass hang on either side of a pulley. Find the acceleration and the string tension.
    Answer
    a = (3.0 − 2.0)(10)/(5.0) = 2.0 m/s². For the 2.0 kg mass, accelerating upward: T − 20 = (2.0)(2.0), so T = 24 N, which lies between the two weights (20 N and 30 N).
  5. An engine pulls two 1000 kg cars with a total force of 4000 N on a frictionless track. Find the acceleration and the tension in the coupling between the two cars.
    Answer
    System of both cars: a = 4000/2000 = 2.0 m/s². The rear car alone: T = (1000)(2.0) = 2000 N. The front coupling carries 4000 N, because it pulls both cars.
  6. A 60 kg skater and a 40 kg skater push off each other on ice. Compare the forces on them and their accelerations.
    Answer
    The forces are equal (a third-law pair). a = F/m, so the 40 kg skater gets 1.5 times the acceleration of the 60 kg skater.
  7. After the push in the last question, where is the center of mass of the two skaters?
    Answer
    Exactly where it was before the push. The pushes are internal forces, and with no outside horizontal force the center of mass can't accelerate.
  8. A book sits on top of a second book. You push the bottom book and both speed up together. What force makes the top book speed up?
    Answer
    Static friction from the bottom book on the top book, pointing forward. Nothing else touches it horizontally.
  9. In a tug-of-war, each team pulls on the rope with 300 N and nobody moves. What is the tension in the rope?
    Answer
    300 N. A rope pulled with 300 N at each end has 300 N tension, not 600 N, just as a rope tied to a wall and pulled with 300 N would.
  10. When you drop a ball, Earth pulls it down. Does the ball pull Earth up? Why don't we notice?
    Answer
    Yes, with exactly the same force. Earth's mass is so enormous that its acceleration is far too tiny to notice.
  11. Two carts collide on a level, frictionless track. Motion sensors show that during the collision the 0.50 kg cart has an acceleration of 0.90 m/s² to the left, while the other cart's acceleration is 0.30 m/s² to the right. What is the mass of the other cart?
    Answer
    1.5 kg. The carts push on each other with equal and opposite forces (a third-law pair), so m1a1 = m2a2 in size: (0.50)(0.90) = m2(0.30), so m2 = 0.45/0.30 = 1.5 kg. The cart with one third of the acceleration has three times the mass.
  12. Who's right? A 4.0 kg mass and a 1.0 kg mass hang on either side of a pulley and are let go. Student A: “The string pulls harder on the 4.0 kg side. It has to hold up the heavier mass, so that tension is 40 N.” Student B: “The tension is the same on both sides, and it's somewhere between 10 N and 40 N.”
    Answer
    Student B. One ideal string over an ideal pulley has one tension. The 4.0 kg mass speeds up downward, so the string pulls up on it with less than its 40 N weight; the 1.0 kg mass speeds up upward, so the string pulls up with more than its 10 N weight. Working it out: a = (40 − 10)/5.0 = 6.0 m/s² and T = (1.0)(10 + 6.0) = 16 N. Student A's 40 N would leave the heavy mass with no net force, so it couldn't accelerate.
  13. Who's right? A 5.0 kg cart sits on a frictionless track with a string tied to it. Student A: “Pulling the string with my hand at 50 N gives the cart the same acceleration as hanging a 50 N weight from the string over a pulley.” Student B: “The hanging weight gives a smaller acceleration.”
    Answer
    Student B. With the hand, the cart is the only thing being accelerated: a = 50/5.0 = 10 m/s². With the hanging weight (5.0 kg), the same 50 N has to accelerate both masses: a = 50/10 = 5.0 m/s². The tension is then only (5.0)(5.0) = 25 N, not 50 N, because the hanging mass is speeding up downward.
  14. A 10 kg cart on a frictionless table is tied over a pulley to a hanging 0.50 kg mass. A student says the cart is too heavy to move. Is she right? And could a big enough hanging mass make the acceleration bigger than g?
    Answer
    No to both. With no friction, any hanging mass gives an unbalanced force on the system: a = (0.50)(10)/10.5 = 0.48 m/s². And a = mhangg/(mhang + mcart) is always less than g, since the hanging weight has to accelerate the cart as well as itself.
  15. A driver brakes hard and her seat belt holds her in place. Take the system to be car + driver. Which of these forces are internal and which are external: the seat belt on the driver, the road's friction on the tires, the driver on the seat belt? Which one slows the system?
    Answer
    The seat belt on the driver and the driver on the seat belt are a third-law pair inside the system, so they're internal and cancel. The road's friction on the tires is external, and it's the only one that can slow the car + driver system. (Choose the driver alone as the system and the belt's force becomes external: that's what slows her.)
  16. A 60 kg person stands at one end of a 90 kg raft floating on still water (ignore the water's drag). She walks 4.5 m along the raft to the other end. How far does she move relative to the shore, and how far does the raft move?
    Answer
    Her push on the raft and the raft's push on her are internal, so the center of mass stays put. She moves forward dp and the raft moves back dr, with dp + dr = 4.5 m and 60dp = 90dr. So the raft moves 1.8 m back and she moves 2.7 m forward relative to the shore. The lighter object moves farther.
  17. A 0.15 kg yo-yo falls as its string unrolls, and video shows its acceleration is 2.0 m/s². The string runs up over a pulley to a counterweight. What counterweight mass would stay perfectly still while the yo-yo falls?
    Answer
    0.12 kg. The yo-yo alone: mg − T = ma, so T = (0.15)(10 − 2.0) = 1.2 N. A counterweight at rest needs T = its weight, so its mass is 1.2/10 = 0.12 kg, less than the yo-yo's, because the string pulls the falling yo-yo with less than its weight.
