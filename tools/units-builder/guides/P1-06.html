Unit 6 · Work, Power, and Energy

How to use this: read the key ideas, then cover each worked example and try it yourself before reading the solution. Do the self-check questions on paper before you open the answers, mark the ones you miss, and come back to them in a day or two without looking. Rereading won't do much; answering questions will.

Key ideas

  • Energy is a scalar. It has no direction, so energies just add. A system can store it as kinetic energy (motion), gravitational potential energy (height, if Earth is in the system), elastic potential energy (a stretched or squashed spring) and thermal energy.
  • Pick the system first. Everything else is the surroundings. Energy only enters or leaves the system as work done by an outside force.
  • Work W = Fd cosθ uses only the part of the force along the displacement. A force along the motion does positive work, a force against it (like kinetic friction) does negative work, and a force at 90° (the normal force on a floor, the tension in a swinging pendulum) does none.
  • Work–energy theorem: the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy, Wnet = ΔK.
  • Work is the area under a force–position graph. That's how you handle a force that changes, like a spring's.
  • Gravity counts once. With the object alone as the system, gravity is an outside force that does work. With object + Earth as the system, gravity is inside, so it shows up as Ug = mgh instead. Never use both.
  • Conservation of energy: if no outside work is done and there's no friction, the mechanical energy K + U stays constant. Only the change in height matters, not the path.
  • Friction turns mechanical energy into thermal energy: ΔEth = fkd, where d is the distance slid.
  • Springs: Fs = kx and Us = ½kx², with x measured from the spring's natural length. Twice the stretch stores four times the energy.
  • Power is how fast energy is transferred: P = ΔE/Δt = W/Δt. A force pushing along the velocity delivers P = Fv.
  • IB Efficiency = useful energy (or power) out ÷ total energy (or power) in. It's always less than 100%.
  • Far from Earth, gravitational potential energy is Ug = −Gm₁m₂/r, with zero at infinite separation. It gets less negative (it increases) as the masses move apart. mgh is the shortcut for small height changes near the surface.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring h along a ramp. Gravitational PE uses the vertical height change.
  • Counting gravity twice: writing Wg = −mgh and using mgh as potential energy.
  • Saying the normal force or a string's tension “must do work because it's big.” A force at 90° to the motion does no work, however big it is.
  • Forgetting that kinetic energy goes as v². Twice the speed means four times the kinetic energy, so a car needs four times the distance to brake.
  • Measuring a spring's x from the wrong place. It's the stretch or compression from natural length, not the spring's total length.
  • Using the applied force F when finding friction on an angled pull. Pulling up at an angle reduces the normal force, so N = mg − F sinθ.
  • Saying energy is “lost.” It's transferred out of the system or turned into thermal energy; the total is conserved.

How to solve an energy problem

  1. Choose the system (for example, cart + Earth + track) and say so.
  2. Pick two moments: an initial state and a final state. Sketch both and mark h = 0 at the lowest point.
  3. List the energies at each moment: K, Ug, Us. An energy bar chart works well here.
  4. Add any energy that crosses the boundary: work by an outside force (+ in, − out), and friction's ΔEth.
  5. Write the bookkeeping equation: Ki + Ui + Wext = Kf + Uf + ΔEth. Cross out every term that is zero.
  6. Solve in symbols first, then substitute numbers. Check the units, and whether mass cancels.

Energy, or forces?

  • Use energy when the question links speeds, heights, stretches and distances, but not time. The path's shape doesn't matter.
  • Use forces and kinematics when it asks for time, acceleration, or a force at one instant.
  • Use both for loops and hills: energy gives the speed at a point, then ΣFtoward center = mv²/r (unit 5) gives the force there.
  • Use the work–energy theorem (Wnet = ΔK) when you know the forces on one object and the distance it moves.

Which forces do work?

  • Ask whether the force has a part along the motion. If not, its work is zero.
  • Static friction on a rolling wheel or a walking foot does no work: the contact point doesn't slide.
  • Gravity on a circular orbit does no work: the force stays at 90° to the velocity, so the speed stays constant.
Work–energy theorem

Pulling a crate at an angle

You pull a 20 kg crate 5.0 m across a floor (μk = 0.20), starting from rest. You pull with 100 N at 30° above horizontal. How fast is the crate moving at the end?

Vertical (no vertical acceleration):

FN+Fsin30∘=mg→FN=200−50=150N fk=μkFN=(0.20)(150)=30N

Work by each force:

WF=Fdcos30∘=(100)(5.0)(0.866)=433J Wf=−fkd=−(30)(5.0)=−150J

The normal force and gravity are at 90° to the motion, so they do no work.

Wnet=ΔK→433−150=12(20)v2 v=5.3m/s

Pulling upward at an angle wastes part of your force, but it also lifts some of the crate's weight off the floor, which cuts friction. That's why the best angle isn't 0°. Try it in the Pulling a Crate sim.

Energy + circular motion

The lowest start height for a loop

A cart rolls without friction from rest at height h into a loop of radius R = 3.0 m. What is the lowest h that lets it make it around the top of the loop?

At the top, the slowest it can go is when the track's push is zero, so gravity alone is the centripetal force:

mg=mvtop2R→vtop2=gR

Energy (cart + Earth), from the start to the top of the loop at height 2R:

mgh=mg(2R)+12m(gR) h=2R+12R=2.5R=2.5(3.0) h=7.5m

Starting at 2R isn't enough: the cart would need to reach the top with zero speed, but it can't stay on the track that slowly, so it falls off before it gets there. The extra R/2 is what keeps it moving fast enough at the top.

Spring → height

A spring launcher

A spring with k = 400 N/m is squashed 0.10 m and launches a 0.050 kg ball straight up. Ignoring air resistance, how high above its starting point does the ball go?

System: ball + spring + Earth. Start squashed and at rest; end at the top, at rest.

Us=12kx2=12(400)(0.10)2=2.0J

All of it becomes gravitational potential energy:

12kx2=mgh→h=2.0(0.050)(10) h=4.0m

Squash it twice as far and the spring stores four times the energy, so the ball goes four times as high. The launch speed only doubles, because K goes as v².

Friction stops it

Down a ramp onto a rough floor

A block slides from rest down a frictionless ramp 1.8 m high, then across a rough floor with μk = 0.30. How far along the floor does it slide before stopping?

System: block + Earth + floor. Start at the top at rest; end stopped on the floor.

mgh=ΔEth=fkd=μkmgd

The mass cancels:

d=hμk=1.80.30 d=6.0m

Mass cancels, so a heavier block slides the same distance. The ramp's shape doesn't matter either, only its height: the block reaches the floor at √(2gh) = 6.0 m/s either way.

Power

A car at highway speed

A car cruises at a constant 25 m/s. Air resistance and rolling friction add up to 600 N. What power must the engine deliver to the wheels?

Constant speed means zero net force, so the driving force equals the 600 N of resistance.

P=Fv=(600)(25) P=1.5×104W(15kW)

At constant speed the kinetic energy isn't changing: all 15 kW goes into thermal energy in the air and tires. Air resistance grows with speed, so driving faster needs much more power.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. You carry a box across a level room at constant velocity. How much work does your upward push do on the box? How much work do you do lifting it onto a shelf 1.2 m up, if it weighs 50 N?
    Answer
    Walking across: zero. Your push is vertical and the box moves horizontally, so the force is at 90° to the motion. Lifting: W = Fd = (50)(1.2) = 60 J, which becomes gravitational PE of the box + Earth system. (Your body still burns energy while walking, but that isn't work done on the box.)
  2. A car doubles its speed. By what factor does its kinetic energy change? With the same braking force, how much farther does it need to stop?
    Answer
    K ∝ v², so it has 4 times the kinetic energy. The brakes remove energy at f·d, so with the same force it needs 4 times the stopping distance.
  3. From the top of a cliff, one ball is thrown straight up and an identical ball is thrown horizontally, both at the same speed. Ignoring air resistance, which hits the ground below faster?
    Answer
    Neither: they land at the same speed. Each starts with the same K and the same height, so each has the same total energy at the ground. Energy is a scalar, so the direction of the throw doesn't matter (it changes the time and where it lands, not the speed).
  4. A force on a cart rises steadily from 0 to 30 N over the first 0.20 m, then stays at 30 N for the next 0.30 m. How much work does it do in total?
    Answer
    Area under the F–x graph: triangle ½(0.20)(30) = 3 J, plus rectangle (0.30)(30) = 9 J. Total 12 J.
  5. A pendulum bob is released from rest 0.45 m above its lowest point. Does the string's tension do any work on the bob? How fast is it moving at the bottom?
    Answer
    No work: the tension always points along the string, at 90° to the bob's motion. Energy: mgh = ½mv², so v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 10 × 0.45) = 3.0 m/s.
  6. A ball is dropped from rest. Describe what happens to its energy using (a) the ball alone as the system and (b) the ball + Earth as the system.
    Answer
    (a) Gravity is an outside force doing positive work on the ball, and that work becomes the ball's kinetic energy (Wg = ΔK). There's no potential energy, because Earth isn't in the system. (b) Nothing outside does work. The system's gravitational PE turns into kinetic energy (ΔUg + ΔK = 0). Both give the same speed.
  7. The same spring launcher is squashed twice as far. What happens to the stored energy and to the launch speed?
    Answer
    Us = ½kx², so 4 times the energy. All of it becomes K, and K ∝ v², so the launch speed doubles.
  8. An elevator lifts an 800 kg load 20 m at constant speed in 10 s. What average power does the motor deliver to the load?
    Answer
    Constant speed, so no change in K. W = mgh = (800)(10)(20) = 1.6 × 10⁵ J. P = W/Δt = 1.6 × 10⁴ W = 16 kW.
  9. IB A motor takes in 500 W of electrical power and lifts a 20 kg mass at a steady 1.5 m/s. What is its efficiency?
    Answer
    Useful output power = Fv = mgv = (20)(10)(1.5) = 300 W. Efficiency = 300/500 = 0.60, or 60%. The other 200 W becomes thermal energy (and sound) in the motor.
  10. A satellite moves in a circular orbit at constant speed. Does gravity do work on it? Another satellite moves on an ellipse and is heading toward Earth. Does gravity do work on that one?
    Answer
    Circular orbit: no. Gravity points to the center, at 90° to the velocity, so the speed stays constant. Ellipse: yes. Heading inward, part of gravity points along the velocity, so gravity does positive work and the satellite speeds up (Kepler's 2nd law shows the same thing).
  11. A spacecraft moves from 2 Earth radii to 4 Earth radii from Earth's center. Does the gravitational potential energy of the spacecraft–Earth system go up or down? Why is the energy negative at all?
    Answer
    It goes up. Ug = −GMm/r, and doubling r halves the size of a negative number, so it becomes less negative. It's negative because zero is defined at infinite separation, and the masses would need energy added to pull them that far apart.
