Study guide

Unit 4 · Work, Energy and Stars

How to use this: read the key ideas, then cover each worked example and try it yourself before reading the solution. Do the self-check questions on paper before you open the answers, mark the ones you miss, and come back to them in a day or two without looking. Rereading won't do much; answering questions will.

Work and power

  • Work is done when a force moves an object through a distance. W = F·d, measured in joules (1 J = 1 N·m).
  • You need a force and motion. Pushing on a wall that doesn't move does no work, however tired you get.
  • Positive work: the force points with the motion. It adds energy (a push that speeds up a cart).
  • Negative work: the force points against the motion. It takes energy away (friction, brakes, air resistance).
  • Zero work: the force is at 90° to the motion. Carrying a box across a level room: your upward force does no work on it.
  • Power is how fast work is done: P = W/t, in watts (1 W = 1 J/s). Same job in less time means more power, not more work.
  • For something moving at a steady speed, P = F·v.
  • Careful: W stands for work (in J), and W is also the unit watt.

Energy

  • Kinetic energy (KE) is energy of motion: KE = ½·m·v². Anything moving has KE. Twice the speed gives 4 times the KE.
  • Potential energy (PE) is stored energy of height: PE = m·g·h. You choose where h = 0, usually the lowest point.
  • Total mechanical energy: TME = KE + PE.
  • Only gravity doing work (no friction, air resistance, push or pull): TME stays the same. KE and PE just trade back and forth.
  • The normal force from a track or slope does no work, because it is at 90° to the motion. A smooth track still conserves TME.
  • Other forces doing work change TME: KEi + PEi + W = KEf + PEf. Positive W adds energy; negative W takes it away, mostly as heat.
  • A coasting cart with no friction can't climb higher than where it started. It would need more energy than it has.
  • Work–energy bar charts show the same equation as bars: the initial bars plus the W bar equal the final bars.

The Sun

  • The Sun is a star: a huge ball of hot gas held together by its own gravity.
  • The core is the center, about 15 million K. It is the only place hot and squeezed enough for fusion.
  • The energy made in the core takes a very long time to work its way out to the photosphere, the glowing surface we see, about 5800 K.
  • Sunspots are dark patches on the photosphere. They are cooler (about 4000 K) than the gas around them, so they look dark by comparison. Strong magnetic fields there block hot gas from rising.
  • The number of sunspots rises and falls in the solar cycle, about 11 years long. At solar maximum there are many spots and more flares (sudden bursts of energy); at solar minimum there are few.

Fusion in the Sun

  • In the core, hydrogen nuclei (single protons, 1H) fuse into helium. It takes huge temperature and pressure to push them together.
  • The proton–proton chain does it in three steps:
Step 11H + 1H → 2H + e+ + ν
Two protons make hydrogen-2 (2H: 1 proton, 1 neutron). One proton turns into a neutron, giving off a positron (e+) and a neutrino (ν).
Step 22H + 1H → 3He + γ
Hydrogen-2 and a proton make helium-3 (2 protons, 1 neutron) and a gamma ray (γ).
Step 33He + 3He → 4He + 1H + 1H
Two helium-3 make helium-4 (2 protons, 2 neutrons) and give two protons back.
  • Steps 1 and 2 happen twice to make the two helium-3 that step 3 needs.
  • Net: 4 1H → 4He + energy.
  • Positron: like an electron, but positive. Neutrino: tiny, no charge, passes through almost anything. Gamma ray: very high-energy light.
  • The helium-4 has about 0.7% less mass than the four hydrogen that made it. That lost mass became energy: E = mc². Since c² is enormous, a tiny bit of mass gives a huge amount of energy.

Star properties

  • Color shows temperature. Like a stove burner or a hot piece of metal: red is the coolest glow, then orange, yellow, white, and blue-white is the hottest. The Sun, yellow-white, is in the middle.
  • Careful: in art class blue is a “cool” color. For stars it's the opposite. Blue stars are the hottest; red stars are the coolest.
  • Luminosity is how much light a star really gives off. How bright a star looks from Earth also depends on how far away it is, so a dim star nearby can look brighter than a powerful star far away.
  • HR diagram: luminosity up the side, temperature along the bottom with hot on the left. Main-sequence stars make a band from upper left to lower right. Giants and supergiants sit at the upper right (cool but huge); white dwarfs at the lower left (hot but tiny).

The life of a star

  • A star forms when gravity pulls a cloud of gas and dust (a nebula) together into a ball that gets hotter as it shrinks. When the core is hot enough, it starts fusing hydrogen.
  • Main sequence: most of a star's life. Gravity pulls in, pressure from the hot core pushes out, and they balance.
  • As it fuses, the core's hydrogen fraction falls and its helium fraction rises. The Sun's core started about 70% hydrogen; now it's about a third. When the core's hydrogen runs out, the main sequence ends.
  • Sun-like star: red giant → planetary nebula → white dwarf.
  • Massive star: red supergiant → supernova → neutron star or black hole.
  • Mass decides the path. More mass means stronger gravity, so the core is squeezed hotter and denser. Only a massive star's core gets hot enough to keep fusing heavier and heavier elements, all the way up to iron, and its collapse at the end is violent enough to explode.
  • Massive stars live shorter lives. They have more fuel, but they burn it far faster. A star 10 times the Sun's mass shines thousands of times brighter and lasts about 30 million years; the Sun lasts about 10 billion.
  • Models have limits. The HR diagram and life-cycle charts are models. No one can watch one star live for billions of years, so they are built from snapshots of many stars at different stages. They simplify: they don't show how long each stage takes, and they can be wrong where data is thin.
  • Big Bang evidence: light from distant galaxies is stretched toward red (red-shifted), which means they are moving away, and farther galaxies move away faster. So the universe is expanding. A faint leftover glow from the hot early universe also comes from every direction.

Common mistakes

  • Saying work is done when nothing moves, or when the force is at 90° to the motion.
  • Forgetting that friction's work is negative.
  • KE: forgetting to square v, or thinking twice the speed means twice the KE. It's 4 times.
  • Putting minutes into P = W/t. Change to seconds first.
  • Saying TME stays the same when friction, air resistance or a push is doing work.
  • Counting gravity's work as W. Gravity is already in the PE terms.
  • Not cancelling terms: “starts from rest” means KEi = 0; “at the bottom” means PE = 0.
  • Thinking sunspots are hotter than the rest of the Sun, or holes in it. They are cooler, magnetic regions of the photosphere.
  • Forgetting that steps 1 and 2 of the proton–proton chain happen twice.
  • Thinking the star that looks brightest gives off the most light. A nearby star can look bright just because it is close.
  • Thinking blue stars are cool because blue is a “cool” color. Blue stars are the hottest.
  • Thinking massive stars live longer because they have more fuel. They burn it much faster.
  • Reading the HR diagram's temperature axis the wrong way. Hot is on the left.

How to solve a work–energy problem

  1. Pick the start (i) and the end (f). Sketch them.
  2. Choose where h = 0, usually the lowest point.
  3. Find W. Is friction, air resistance, a push or a pull doing work? If not, W = 0. Gravity and the normal force don't count.
  4. Cancel the zero terms. “Starts from rest”: KEi = 0. “Comes to a stop”: KEf = 0. “On the ground” or “at the bottom”: PE = 0. “Level road”: the PEs are equal and cancel.
  5. Write what's left of KEi + PEi + W = KEf + PEf, and put in ½·m·v², m·g·h and F·d.
  6. Solve. Check the answer makes sense: an object that coasts downhill should speed up.

Work and power problems

  • List every force. For each one, ask: with the motion (+), against it (−) or at 90° (0)?
  • W = F·d for each force, then add them for the total work.
  • Lifting at a steady speed: the force you need equals the weight, m·g.
  • Power: P = W/t with t in seconds, or P = F·v at a steady speed.

Work–energy bar charts

  • Draw a bar for KEi and PEi (no bar if it's zero).
  • Draw the W bar: up for positive work, down for negative work.
  • Draw KEf and PEf.
  • Check: the initial bars plus W add up to the final bars.

Reading the HR diagram

  • Up = brighter. Left = hotter.
  • Bright but cool (upper right)? The star must be huge: a giant or supergiant.
  • Hot but dim (lower left)? The star must be tiny: a white dwarf.
  • On the main sequence, the bright hot stars are the massive, short-lived ones.
  • Color gives the left–right spot: blue-white stars on the left, red stars on the right.

Reading a sunspot graph

  • Find the low points (solar minimum) or the high points (solar maximum). Read their years off the bottom axis.
  • One cycle goes from one low point to the next (or one peak to the next).
  • Count the gaps between low points, not the low points themselves: 3 low points make 2 cycles.
  • Cycle length = (last year − first year) ÷ number of cycles.
  • Peaks don't all reach the same height. The timing repeats; the size doesn't.

Keeping track in the p-p chain

  • The number on the upper left is the number of protons + neutrons. 1H = 1 proton; 2H = 1 p + 1 n; 3He = 2 p + 1 n; 4He = 2 p + 2 n.
  • Check each step: the top numbers add to the same total on both sides.
  • Check charge: in step 1, two + charges go in, and 2H has only one. The positron carries the other + away.
Work by each force

Pushing a crate across the floor

A worker pushes a 40 kg crate 6.0 m across a level warehouse floor with a 150 N horizontal force. Friction on the crate is 90 N. The crate starts at rest. Find the work done by each force, the total work and the crate's final speed.

Push (with the motion):

Wapp=(150)(6.0)=+900J

Friction (against the motion):

Wfrict=−(90)(6.0)=−540J

Fnorm and Fgrav are at 90° to the motion:

Wnorm=Wgrav=0J Wtotal=900−540=360J

Level floor and starts at rest, so KEi, PEi and PEf are all zero:

W=KEf→360=12(40)v2 v2=2(360)40=18 v=4.2m/s

Only forces along the motion do work. The push adds 900 J, friction takes 540 J back out as heat, and the 360 J left over becomes the crate's kinetic energy.

Power two ways

A tractor dragging a log

A tractor drags a log 30 m across a field at a steady speed in 20 s. Friction on the log is 2400 N. Find the work the tractor does on the log and its power, in watts and in horsepower. Then check the power with P = F·v.

Steady speed, so the tractor's pull equals friction: Fapp = 2400 N.

W=Fd=(2400)(30) W=72,000J P=Wt=72,00020 P=3600W 3600746≈4.8hp

Check with P = F·v. The speed is 30 m ÷ 20 s:

v=3020=1.5m/s P=Fv=(2400)(1.5)=3600W

Both power equations give the same answer. If the tractor dragged the log twice as fast, it would do the same work in half the time: twice the power.

Energy is conserved

A toy car on a smooth track

A 2.0 kg toy car is let go from rest at A, 1.8 m above the floor. The track is smooth, so ignore friction. Find its speed at the bottom (B) and at the top of the hump (C), 0.55 m high.

Only gravity does work, so W = 0 and TME stays the same:

TME=PEA=(2.0)(10)(1.8)=36J

At B (h = 0), all 36 J is KE:

36=12(2.0)v2→v2=36 vB=6.0m/s

At C, some of the energy is PE again:

PEC=(2.0)(10)(0.55)=11J KEC=36−11=25J 25=12(2.0)v2→v2=25 vC=5.0m/s

The car is fastest at the lowest point. It could not get over a hump taller than 1.8 m: that would need more than the 36 J it has.

Friction takes energy

A mountain biker coasting downhill

A mountain biker and bike (70 kg together) roll over the top of a 12 m tall hill at 3.0 m/s. She coasts to the bottom without pedaling and arrives at 14 m/s. How much work did friction and air resistance do? If the trail down is 60 m long, what was the average resistance force?

Energies, with h = 0 at the bottom:

KEi=12(70)(3.0)2=315J PEi=(70)(10)(12)=8400J KEf=12(70)(14)2=6860J

PEf = 0. Solve the work–energy equation for W:

315+8400+W=6860 W=−1855J

That work happened over 60 m, against the motion:

F=185560 F≈31N

The negative W bar shows the mechanical energy that was lost: about 1855 J turned into heat in the tires, brakes and air. Without friction she would have arrived even faster.

Work adds energy

Pedaling up a hill

A cyclist and bike (80 kg together) start up a hill at 4.0 m/s and reach the top, 15 m higher, at 6.0 m/s. Ignore friction and air resistance. How much work did the cyclist do? The climb took 100 s. What was her average power?

Set h = 0 at the bottom, so PEi = 0:

KEi=12(80)(4.0)2=640J KEf=12(80)(6.0)2=1440J PEf=(80)(10)(15)=12,000J 640+W=1440+12,000 W=12,800J P=Wt=12,800100 P=128W

Her positive work raised both her KE and her PE. Most of it went into height: 12,000 J of the 12,800 J.

The solar cycle

Reading a sunspot graph

The graph shows yearly sunspot counts for a stretch of years (simplified data). (a) How long is one solar cycle? (b) In which years was the Sun most active? (c) Why do sunspots look dark?

(a) The low points are at 1901, 1912, 1923, 1934 and 1945. Five low points make 4 gaps, so 4 cycles:

1945−1901=44years cycle=444 cycle=11years

(b) The peaks: 1905, 1916, 1927 and 1938. Check: 1938 − 1905 = 33 years for 3 cycles, 11 years each ✓

(c) A sunspot is a cooler spot on the photosphere, where a strong magnetic field blocks hot gas from rising. It still glows, but less than the hotter gas around it, so it looks dark by comparison.

Use as many cycles as the graph shows: one long span divided by the number of cycles is more accurate than one short gap. Notice the peaks are different heights, but the timing stays close to 11 years.

Fusion

Balancing the proton–proton chain

(a) Step 2 is written 2H + 1H → ? + γ. Use the top numbers and the charges to name the missing nucleus. (b) In step 1, show that the top numbers and the charges both balance. (c) Four hydrogen atoms have a total mass of 4 × 1.00783 u, and one helium-4 atom has 4.00260 u (u is a tiny unit of mass). What fraction of the mass turns into energy?

(a) Add the top numbers and the charges on the left. The γ ray has no top number and no charge, so the missing nucleus gets all of both:

top:2+1=3 charge:+1+(+1)=+2

Charge +2 means 2 protons, so it is helium; 3 particles means 2 protons + 1 neutron:

H2+H1→He3+γ

(b) Step 1: 1H + 1H → 2H + e+ + ν. The positron and the neutrino have top number 0.

top:1+1=2=2+0+0✓ charge:+1+(+1)=+1+(+1)+0✓

2H has only one + charge, so the positron is what carries the other one away.

(c) Mass before and after:

4×1.00783=4.03132u 4.03132−4.00260=0.02872u lost 0.028724.03132=0.0071 about0.7%of the mass

The missing 0.7% became energy (E = mc²): the gamma rays, and the energy of the particles flying apart. For every kilogram of hydrogen the Sun fuses, about 7 grams of mass turns into energy. The positrons and neutrinos are made in step 1; the neutrinos fly straight out of the Sun.

Stars

Reading the HR diagram

Star A has a surface temperature of 3500 K and gives off 100,000 times as much light as the Sun. Star B is 10,000 K and gives off 0.01 times as much light as the Sun. (The Sun is about 5800 K.) What kind of star is each one? What color does each look, and why is each one as bright as it is?

Star A: cooler than the Sun, so it is to the right of the Sun; much brighter, so it is near the top. Upper right: a red supergiant. It looks red because it is cool. It is still very bright because it is enormous: a huge surface gives off a lot of light even though each square meter is cool.

Star B: hotter than the Sun, so it is to the left; much dimmer, so it is near the bottom. Lower left: a white dwarf. It looks blue-white because it is hot. It is dim because it is tiny, about the size of Earth.

Brightness depends on temperature and size. Star A is what a very massive star becomes near the end of its life, before it explodes as a supernova. Star B is what is left after a Sun-like star sheds its outer layers.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. Is the work done by the named force positive, negative or zero? (a) A crossing guard holds a stop sign still for two minutes (her force on the sign). (b) A goalie's hands stop a fast shot (hands on ball). (c) Gravity on a falling acorn. (d) A student walks down a level hallway at a steady speed carrying a backpack (his upward force on the backpack).
    Answer
    (a) Zero: the sign doesn't move. (b) Negative: the force points against the ball's motion and takes away its energy. (c) Positive: gravity points down and the acorn moves down. (d) Zero: the force is up but the motion is sideways, at 90°.
  2. A delivery drone lifts a 2.0 kg package 15 m straight up at a steady speed in 6.0 s. How much work does it do on the package? What is its power?
    Answer
    Steady speed, so the lifting force equals the weight: F = m·g = (2.0)(10) = 20 N. W = F·d = (20)(15) = 300 J. P = W/t = 300/6.0 = 50 W.
  3. A 250-W winch and a 750-W winch each drag the same log 12 m across flat ground with a steady 500 N pull. Which does more work? How long does each one take?
    Answer
    Same work: W = F·d = (500)(12) = 6000 J each. t = W ÷ P: 6000 ÷ 250 = 24 s and 6000 ÷ 750 = 8.0 s. Three times the power does the same job in a third of the time.
  4. A 0.40 kg soccer ball is kicked at 20 m/s. Find its KE. What would it be if it were kicked at 40 m/s?
    Answer
    KE = ½(0.40)(20)² = 80 J. At twice the speed, KE is 2² = 4 times as much: 320 J.
  5. A 0.020 kg acorn falls 5.0 m from a branch to the ground. Ignore air resistance. How much PE does it lose? How fast is it going when it lands?
    Answer
    PE = m·g·h = (0.020)(10)(5.0) = 1.0 J. All of it becomes KE: 1.0 = ½(0.020)v², so v² = 100 and v = 10 m/s.
  6. A box slides across a level carpet and comes to a stop. What happens to its KE, its PE and its TME? Where does the energy go?
    Answer
    KE decreases to zero. PE stays the same (the height doesn't change). TME decreases, because friction does negative work. The energy becomes heat in the box and the carpet.
  7. Friends push a 1200 kg stalled car from rest along a level road. After subtracting the work done by friction, their pushes do 2400 J of work. Which terms of KEi + PEi + W = KEf + PEf cancel? How fast is the car going?
    Answer
    KEi = 0 (starts at rest), and PEi and PEf cancel (level road). That leaves W = KEf: 2400 = ½(1200)v², so v² = 4 and v = 2.0 m/s.
  8. A cyclist rides at a steady 8.0 m/s on level ground. Friction and air resistance together push back on her with 25 N. What is her power?
    Answer
    Steady speed, so her forward force equals 25 N. P = F·v = (25)(8.0) = 200 W.
  9. A cart coasts on a frictionless track from rest at 3 m high. Can it get over a hill 4 m high? What about a hill exactly 3 m high?
    Answer
    Not the 4 m hill: its TME is only enough for PE at 3 m, so it can never get higher than its start. A 3 m hill is the most it could just reach, arriving with zero speed at the top.
  10. A photo of the Sun shows dark sunspots. Are sunspots colder than the rest of the photosphere? Are they cold? What makes them different?
    Answer
    They are cooler than the photosphere around them (about 4000 K instead of about 5800 K), so they look dark next to it. But they are not cold: on their own they would glow brightly. Strong magnetic fields there block hot gas from rising up from below.
  11. (a) In which step of the proton–proton chain is a neutrino made, and what happens to it? (b) Why does it take such a hot, dense core to fuse hydrogen at all?
    Answer
    (a) Step 1, when a proton turns into a neutron (along with a positron). Neutrinos barely interact with anything, so they fly straight out of the Sun. (b) Protons are all positive, so they repel each other. Only at a huge temperature (fast-moving protons) and pressure (protons crowded together) do they get close enough to fuse.
  12. Stars Q and R look equally bright in the night sky, but Q is 10 times farther away. Which one really gives off more light? Q looks blue-white and R looks red: which is hotter, and which one sits farther left on an HR diagram?
    Answer
    Q. It looks just as bright from much farther away, so it must give off much more light (a greater luminosity). Q is also hotter, because blue-white is a hotter glow than red, so Q sits farther left on the HR diagram.
  13. All the stars in a cluster formed from the same cloud at the same time. Today the cluster has plenty of main-sequence stars between 1 and 3 times the Sun's mass, but no main-sequence stars heavier than that. Why are the heavier ones missing, and what did they leave behind?
    Answer
    Heavier stars have stronger gravity and hotter cores, so they burn through their fuel much faster. They ran out of core hydrogen first and left the main sequence, while the lighter stars are still fusing hydrogen. The heaviest ones (more than about 8 times the Sun's mass) became red supergiants, exploded as supernovae and left neutron stars or black holes. The ones in between swelled into red giants and ended as white dwarfs. It also tells you the cluster is old: at least a few hundred million years.
  14. An HR diagram is a snapshot: thousands of stars plotted as dots at one moment. How can astronomers use it to work out how one star changes over billions of years? Why are most of the dots on the main sequence?
    Answer
    Each dot is a star at a different stage of its life, like a photo of a crowd of people of all ages: no one watched one person grow up, but the photo still shows every age. Most dots are on the main sequence because a star spends most of its shining life there (about 90%), fusing hydrogen in its core. The giant stages are short, so at any moment only a few stars are caught in them.
  15. Light from almost every distant galaxy is red-shifted, and the farther away the galaxy is, the bigger the shift. What does that tell you about the galaxies, and about the universe?
    Answer
    A red shift means the galaxy is moving away from us. Galaxies in every direction moving away, with the farther ones moving faster, means the whole universe is expanding. Run that backward and everything was once packed together, which is the Big Bang idea.
