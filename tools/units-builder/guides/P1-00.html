Study guide

Unit 0 · Measurement and uncertainties (IB)

How to use this: read the key ideas, then cover each worked example and try it yourself before reading the solution. Do the self-check questions on paper before you open the answers, mark the ones you miss, and come back to them in a day or two without looking. Rereading won't do much; answering questions will.

Key ideas

  • SI base units: metre (m), kilogram (kg), second (s), ampere (A), kelvin (K), mole (mol). Every other unit is built from these: a newton is kg m s−2, a joule is kg m2 s−2.
  • Prefixes: n (10−9), µ (10−6), m (10−3), c (10−2), k (103), M (106), G (109). Write large and small numbers in scientific notation, e.g. 3.0 × 108 m s−1.
  • Order of magnitude: the power of ten closest to a value. An estimate only needs to be right to within a factor of about ten.
  • Every measurement has an uncertainty, written (value ± uncertainty) unit, e.g. (2.34 ± 0.01) s.
  • Reading uncertainty (class rules): analogue scale, ± half the smallest division. A length on a ruler, ± one division, because you read both ends. Digital display, ± 1 in the last decimal place.
  • Repeated readings: report the mean ± half the range. If half the range is smaller than the reading uncertainty, use the reading uncertainty.
  • The value and its uncertainty end at the same decimal place. (2.34 ± 0.01) s, not (2.34 ± 0.005) s or (2.3 ± 0.01) s.
  • Random error scatters readings both ways; repeating and averaging reduces its effect. Systematic error shifts every reading the same way (a zero error, a miscalibrated scale); repeats don't fix it. On a graph a zero error shows up as an unexpected intercept.
  • Precise means readings agree with each other (small spread). Accurate means close to the true value. A result can be precise but not accurate.
  • Vectors (displacement, velocity, force) have direction; scalars (mass, time, energy) don't. Add vectors tip to tail, or by components.

Common mistakes

  • Rounding the uncertainty to one significant figure and then cutting the value to match. In this class the value and uncertainty simply end at the same decimal place.
  • Using ± half a division for a ruler length. You read two ends, so it's ± one division.
  • Adding absolute uncertainties when you multiply or divide. For products and quotients, add the percentage uncertainties.
  • Forgetting to double the percentage uncertainty for a squared quantity (T2, r2, v2).
  • Thinking more repeats fix a systematic error. They only reduce random error.
  • Forcing a best-fit line through the origin when the data and error bars don't support it.
  • Adding vector magnitudes directly. 3 N east plus 4 N north is 5 N, not 7 N.

How to report a result with its uncertainty

  1. Find each reading uncertainty from the instrument: half a division (analogue), one division (ruler length), or the last digit (digital).
  2. If you repeated a reading, take the mean and half the range; use the reading uncertainty instead if it is bigger.
  3. Timing n cycles? Divide the time and its uncertainty by n.
  4. Work out the result with the equation, keeping extra digits.
  5. Propagate: add absolute uncertainties for + and −; add percentage uncertainties for × and ÷; multiply by the power for a power.
  6. Turn the percentage back into an absolute uncertainty, then round so the value and the uncertainty end at the same decimal place. Add the unit.

Graphs and gradients

  • Plot whatever makes the relationship a straight line (for a pendulum, T2 against L).
  • Draw error bars, then the best-fit line, then the steepest and shallowest lines that still pass through every error bar.
  • Gradient uncertainty = (steepest − shallowest)/2. The same works for the intercept.
  • An intercept where the physics says there should be none points to a systematic error.

Resolving vectors

  • Draw the vector and the angle. The side next to the angle uses cos; the side opposite uses sin.
  • Add all the x-components, then all the y-components.
  • Rebuild the resultant with Pythagoras, and find its direction with tan θ = Ry/Rx.
Repeated readings

Timing a pendulum

A student times 10 swings of a pendulum three times: 15.6 s, 15.9 s and 15.7 s. Find the period with its uncertainty.

Mean of the three timings for 10 swings:

t¯=15.6+15.9+15.73=15.73s

Half the range (bigger than the ±0.1 s reading uncertainty):

Δt=15.9−15.62=0.15s t10=(15.7±0.2)s

Divide the time and its uncertainty by 10:

T=(1.57±0.02)s

Half the range (0.15 s) is bigger than the stopwatch's ±0.1 s, so it sets the uncertainty. Timing 10 swings makes the uncertainty in one period 10 times smaller.

Sums and products

A sheet of paper

A sheet is measured with a ruler marked in millimetres: length (29.7 ± 0.1) cm and width (21.0 ± 0.1) cm. Find the perimeter and the area with their uncertainties.

Perimeter: a sum, so add absolute uncertainties:

P=2(29.7+21.0)=101.4cm ΔP=2(0.1+0.1)=0.4cm P=(101.4±0.4)cm

Area: a product, so add percentage uncertainties:

A=(29.7)(21.0)=623.7cm2 ΔAA=0.129.7+0.121.0 ΔAA=0.34%+0.48%=0.81% ΔA=0.0081×623.7=5cm2 A=(624±5)cm2

For a sum, absolute uncertainties add. For a product, percentage uncertainties add. Both ruler readings are ± one division because each length needs two readings.

Powers

g from a pendulum

A pendulum has length L = (0.800 ± 0.001) m and period T = (1.79 ± 0.02) s. Find g = 4π2L/T2 with its uncertainty.

g=4π2LT2=4π2(0.800)(1.79)2=9.86m s−2

Percentage uncertainties (T is squared, so its share doubles):

ΔLL=0.0010.800=0.13% ΔTT=0.021.79=1.12% Δgg=0.13%+2(1.12%)=2.4% Δg=0.024×9.86=0.2m s−2 g=(9.9±0.2)m s−2

T is squared, so its 1.1% counts twice. The period is by far the biggest source of uncertainty, so that's the measurement to improve (time more swings).

Gradient uncertainty

A spring constant from a graph

A student plots force F against extension x for a spring, with error bars. The best-fit line has gradient 4.0 N cm−1. The steepest and shallowest lines through all the error bars have gradients 4.4 and 3.6 N cm−1. Find k with its uncertainty, and its percentage uncertainty.

The gradient of F against x is k:

Δk=ksteep−kshallow2 Δk=4.4−3.62=0.4N cm−1 k=(4.0±0.4)N cm−1 Δkk=0.44.0=10%

F = kx, so the gradient is k. If the best-fit line had missed the origin, that would suggest a zero error in the extension readings, which a straight-line graph lets you spot.

Vectors

Two forces on a crate

Two ropes pull a crate along the floor: 20 N at 30° above the horizontal, and 10 N horizontally in the same direction. Find the size and direction of the resultant.

Components of the 20 N force:

Fx=20cos30∘=17.3N Fy=20sin30∘=10.0N

Add the components:

Rx=17.3+10=27.3N Ry=10.0N R=27.32+10.02 R=29N θ=tan−1(10.027.3) θ=20∘ above the horizontal

Split each force into components, add the components, then rebuild the resultant. Adding the magnitudes (30 N) would be wrong because the forces point in different directions.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. A thermometer is marked every 1 °C. A reading is about 21.5 °C. How should you write it?
    Answer
    (21.5 ± 0.5) °C. Analogue scale: ± half the smallest division, and the value ends at the same decimal place as the uncertainty.
  2. A ruler marked in millimetres measures a pencil as 14.3 cm. What is the uncertainty, and why?
    Answer
    ± 0.1 cm (one division). You read both ends of the pencil, each ± half a division, so the length is ± one division: (14.3 ± 0.1) cm.
  3. A digital balance reads 52.36 g. Write the reading with its uncertainty.
    Answer
    (52.36 ± 0.01) g. A digital reading is ± 1 in the last decimal place.
  4. Three timings are 2.31 s, 2.40 s and 2.35 s, on a stopwatch reading to 0.01 s. What do you report?
    Answer
    Mean 2.353 s, half the range (2.40 − 2.31)/2 ≈ 0.05 s, which is bigger than ±0.01 s. Report (2.35 ± 0.05) s.
  5. A = (4.0 ± 0.2) m and B = (2.0 ± 0.1) m. Find the uncertainty in A − B and in A × B.
    Answer
    A − B = (2.0 ± 0.3) m: absolute uncertainties add, even when you subtract. A × B: 5% + 5% = 10%, so (8.0 ± 0.8) m2.
  6. A radius has a 2% uncertainty. What is the percentage uncertainty in the area of the circle, πr2? In the volume of the sphere, (4/3)πr3?
    Answer
    Area: 2 × 2% = 4%. Volume: 3 × 2% = 6%. Constants like π and 4/3 have no uncertainty.
  7. An ammeter reads 0.02 A when no current flows, and every reading in the experiment includes this. What kind of error is it, and does repeating help?
    Answer
    A systematic (zero) error. Repeating doesn't help, because every reading is shifted the same way. Subtract 0.02 A from each reading. On a graph it shows up as an unexpected intercept.
  8. Results of g: 9.62, 9.63, 9.61 m s−2. Are they precise? Accurate?
    Answer
    Precise: they agree closely with each other. Not accurate: they're all about 0.2 m s−2 under 9.81, which suggests a systematic error.
  9. Estimate the order of magnitude of the number of heartbeats in a human lifetime.
    Answer
    About 70 beats per minute × 60 × 24 × 365 × 80 years ≈ 3 × 109, so the order of magnitude is 109.
  10. A walker goes 3.0 km east and then 4.0 km north. What is the displacement?
    Answer
    5.0 km at 53° north of east. √(3.02 + 4.02) = 5.0 km, and tan θ = 4.0/3.0 gives θ = 53°. The distance walked is 7.0 km.
