Key ideas: atoms and light

  • The nucleus: Rutherford's gold-foil experiment found that most alpha particles pass straight through, but a few bounce back. So an atom is mostly empty space, with its positive charge and almost all its mass in a tiny nucleus.
  • Nuclear notation AZX: Z = protons, A = protons + neutrons (nucleons). Isotopes have the same Z but different numbers of neutrons.
  • Photons: light comes in packets of energy E = hf = hc/λ. Higher frequency means more energy per photon. A handy value: hc = 1240 eV·nm.
  • Photoelectric effect: light knocks electrons out of a metal only if each photon has at least the work function φ. Then Kmax = hf − φ. Brighter light means more photons, so more electrons, but not faster ones. This only makes sense if light is made of photons.
  • Energy levels: electrons in an atom have only certain energies. Dropping between levels emits a photon with exactly the energy difference, ΔE = hf; absorbing a photon of exactly that energy lifts it up. That's why each element has its own line spectrum.
  • An emission spectrum is bright lines from a hot gas; an absorption spectrum is dark lines where a cool gas in front of a light source removes those same wavelengths.
  • Matter waves: any moving particle has a de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p. Electrons diffracting through crystals show it. Particles and light both show wave–particle duality.
  • Compton scattering: an X-ray photon that bounces off an electron comes out with a longer wavelength (less energy), having given the electron energy and momentum. That shows photons carry momentum, p = h/λ.
  • Black-body radiation: a hot object gives off a continuous spectrum that depends only on its temperature. Hotter objects give off much more power (P = σAT⁴) and peak at shorter wavelengths (λmaxT = 2.9 × 10⁻³ m·K).

Key ideas: the nucleus

  • The strong force holds nucleons together. It's attractive, very strong and very short-range (about the size of a nucleus), so it can beat the protons' electric repulsion.
  • Mass–energy equivalence: E = mc². In nuclear reactions, mass can turn into energy and back again. 1 u of mass is equivalent to 931.5 MeV.
  • Binding energy is the energy needed to pull a nucleus apart into separate nucleons. A nucleus with more binding energy per nucleon is more stable.
  • IB A nucleus has less mass than its separate nucleons; that mass defect Δm gives the binding energy, E = Δmc². Binding energy per nucleon peaks near iron (A ≈ 56).
  • Fission (a heavy nucleus splitting) and fusion (light nuclei joining) both release energy, because the products are more tightly bound (more binding energy per nucleon) than what went in. The lost mass appears as kinetic energy of the products and as photons.
  • Radioactive decay is random and spontaneous. Alpha (α, a helium nucleus): A drops by 4, Z by 2. Beta-minus (β⁻): a neutron becomes a proton plus an electron and an antineutrino, so Z goes up by 1. Beta-plus (β⁺): a proton becomes a neutron plus a positron and a neutrino. Gamma (γ): a photon, with no change to A or Z.
  • Every reaction conserves charge, nucleon number, energy and momentum. Use those to balance decay equations.
  • IB Penetration and ionization: alpha is stopped by paper but ionizes most strongly; beta is stopped by a few mm of aluminium; gamma is only reduced by thick lead or concrete and ionizes least. Measured count rates include background radiation, which has to be subtracted.
  • Half-life t½ is the time for half the nuclei (and so half the activity) to decay. After n half-lives, (½)ⁿ is left. The decay constant is λ = ln 2/t½.
  • IB Reactors: a moderator slows neutrons so they cause more fission; control rods absorb neutrons to control the chain reaction; a heat exchanger carries the energy away; shielding stops radiation. The waste stays radioactive for a very long time and must be stored safely.

Key ideas: stars IB

  • A star is stable when the outward pressure of radiation and hot gas balances the inward pull of gravity. Fusion in its core needs very high temperature and density.
  • Luminosity L is the total power a star gives off: L = σAT⁴ = 4πR²σT⁴. Apparent brightness b = L/(4πd²) is what reaches us, and it falls with the square of the distance.
  • The HR diagram plots luminosity (up) against surface temperature (decreasing to the right). Main-sequence stars fuse hydrogen; red giants and supergiants are cool but huge and bright; white dwarfs are hot but tiny and dim.
  • A star's mass decides its life: low-mass stars end as white dwarfs; high-mass stars become supergiants, then neutron stars or black holes.
  • Parallax: d (parsecs) = 1/p (arcseconds) for nearby stars.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking brighter light gives photoelectrons more energy. Only the frequency does; brightness only changes how many come out.
  • Mixing up eV and J. Convert with 1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, or use hc = 1240 eV·nm and stay in eV.
  • Forgetting that energy levels are negative: a drop from −1.5 eV to −3.4 eV releases 1.9 eV.
  • Losing the electron or positron when balancing a beta decay, so Z doesn't balance.
  • Thinking a half-life means everything is gone after two half-lives. After two, a quarter is still left.
  • Thinking a nucleus weighs the same as its parts. The bound nucleus has less mass; that missing mass is the binding energy.

How to solve a photon or energy-level problem

  1. Work in eV where you can, using hc = 1240 eV·nm.
  2. Photon energy: E = 1240/λ(nm) eV.
  3. Energy levels: ΔE = Eupper − Elower, then λ = 1240/ΔE.
  4. Photoelectric: Kmax = Ephoton − φ. If it comes out negative, no electrons are emitted.
  5. Need joules? Multiply eV by 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹.
  6. Check the wavelength: 400–700 nm is visible, shorter is ultraviolet, longer is infrared.

Decay equations

  • Write the parent nucleus, an arrow, the daughter and the particle.
  • Top numbers (A) must add up on both sides; bottom numbers (Z) too. An electron counts as Z = −1, a positron as +1, an alpha as 42He.
  • Find the daughter element from its new Z.

Half-life problems

  • Count the half-lives: n = t/t½.
  • Fraction left = (½)ⁿ. For whole numbers, just halve n times.
  • Activity and count rate halve too (after subtracting background).
Photoelectric effect

Ultraviolet light on a metal

Light of wavelength 250 nm shines on a metal with work function 2.3 eV. Find the photon energy, the maximum kinetic energy of the electrons, and the longest wavelength that will release any electrons.

E = 1240/250 = 4.96 eV
Kmax = 4.96 − 2.3 = 2.7 eV (a stopping voltage of 2.7 V)
Threshold: E = φ → λ = 1240/2.3 = 540 nm

Any light redder than 540 nm releases nothing, however bright it is, because each photon has too little energy.

Energy levels

Hydrogen's red line

A hydrogen electron drops from the n = 3 level (−1.51 eV) to the n = 2 level (−3.40 eV). Find the photon's energy and wavelength.

ΔE = −1.51 − (−3.40) = 1.89 eV
λ = 1240/1.89 = 656 nm (red)

This is the bright red line in hydrogen's spectrum. A cool hydrogen gas absorbs exactly the same 656 nm light, making a dark line in an absorption spectrum.

de Broglie wavelength

An electron as a wave

Find the de Broglie wavelength of an electron moving at 1.0 × 10⁶ m/s. Why does a crystal diffract it?

λ = h/(mv) = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴)/[(9.11 × 10⁻³¹)(1.0 × 10⁶)]
  = 7.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ m

That's about the spacing between atoms in a crystal, so the crystal acts like a diffraction grating. A thrown ball's wavelength is far too tiny to ever notice.

Half-life

A medical isotope

A sample contains 80 mg of iodine-131, which has a half-life of 8.0 days. How much is left after 24 days?

n = 24/8.0 = 3 half-lives
80 → 40 → 20 → 10 mg (that's (½)³ = ⅛ of it)

The other 70 mg hasn't vanished; it has decayed into a different element (xenon). The activity has also dropped to ⅛.

Decay equations

Balancing alpha and beta decay

Write the decay equations for uranium-238 (alpha decay) and carbon-14 (beta-minus decay).

23892U → 23490Th + 42He
146C → 147N + 0−1e + antineutrino

Check the top and bottom numbers: 238 = 234 + 4 and 92 = 90 + 2; 14 = 14 + 0 and 6 = 7 − 1. Losing Z changes the element.

Binding energy IB

The mass defect of helium-4

Two protons (1.007276 u each) and two neutrons (1.008665 u each) make a helium-4 nucleus of mass 4.001506 u. Find the binding energy and the binding energy per nucleon.

Separate nucleons: 2(1.007276) + 2(1.008665) = 4.031882 u
Δm = 4.031882 − 4.001506 = 0.030376 u
Binding energy = (0.030376)(931.5) = 28.3 MeV; per nucleon: 7.07 MeV

About 0.75% of the mass disappears when the nucleus forms. That same energy is what's released when hydrogen fuses into helium in stars.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. How many protons and neutrons are in carbon-12 and in carbon-14? Why are they the same element?
    Answer
    Both have 6 protons. Carbon-12 has 6 neutrons and carbon-14 has 8. Same number of protons, so the same element: they're isotopes.
  2. Red light shines on a metal and no electrons come off. You make the red light much brighter. What happens? What would happen with dim ultraviolet light?
    Answer
    Still no electrons: each red photon has too little energy, however many there are. Dim UV light releases electrons right away, because each UV photon has enough energy (just fewer electrons than bright UV).
  3. What is the difference between an emission spectrum and an absorption spectrum of the same gas?
    Answer
    Emission: bright lines on a dark background, from the hot gas itself. Absorption: dark lines in a continuous spectrum, where the cooler gas absorbs light passing through it. The lines are at the same wavelengths in both.
  4. An electron and a proton have the same speed. Which has the longer de Broglie wavelength?
    Answer
    The electron. λ = h/(mv), and its mass (and so momentum) is much smaller.
  5. In Compton scattering, how does the scattered X-ray differ from the incoming one? What does that show?
    Answer
    It has a longer wavelength, so less energy and less momentum; the electron took the difference. It shows photons carry momentum, like particles in a collision.
  6. Radium-226 (Z = 88) undergoes alpha decay. What is the daughter nucleus?
    Answer
    A drops by 4 and Z by 2: 22286Rn, radon-222.
  7. What fraction of a radioactive sample is left after 4 half-lives? How many half-lives until the activity is ⅛ of the start?
    Answer
    (½)⁴ = 1/16. Activity drops to ⅛ after 3 half-lives.
  8. IB Which type of radiation can be stopped by a sheet of paper? Which ionizes most strongly? Why do Geiger counters read a little even with no source nearby?
    Answer
    Alpha is stopped by paper, and it ionizes most strongly. Gamma needs thick lead or concrete to reduce it. The counter still reads background radiation from rocks, cosmic rays and the air, which must be subtracted from measurements.
  9. IB Both splitting uranium and fusing hydrogen release energy. How can that be?
    Answer
    Both produce nuclei with more binding energy per nucleon, closer to the iron peak on the binding-energy curve. Uranium is on the heavy side and hydrogen on the light side, so moving toward iron from either side releases energy.
  10. Star A has a surface temperature of 6000 K, and star B 3000 K. Which peaks at the shorter wavelength? What colour does each look?
    Answer
    Star A: λmax ∝ 1/T, so it peaks at half star B's wavelength (about 480 nm, so white to yellow-white). Star B peaks in the infrared, about 970 nm, and looks red.
  11. IB A star's parallax is 0.25 arcseconds. How far away is it?
    Answer
    d = 1/p = 1/0.25 = 4.0 parsecs.
  12. IB A star is 4 times as luminous as the Sun but has only half the Sun's surface temperature. How does its radius compare?
    Answer
    L = 4πR²σT⁴, so R² ∝ L/T⁴ = 4/(½)⁴ = 64, and R = 8 times the Sun's radius. Cool but bright means big: a giant.
  13. IB In a nuclear reactor, what do the moderator and the control rods each do?
    Answer
    The moderator slows fast neutrons down so they're more likely to cause another fission. The control rods absorb neutrons, so pushing them in slows the chain reaction and pulling them out speeds it up.
