Study guide

Unit 1 · Thermodynamics

How to use this: read the key ideas, then cover each worked example and try it yourself before reading the solution. Do the self-check questions on paper before you open the answers, mark the ones you miss, and come back to them in a day or two without looking. Rereading won't do much; answering questions will.

Key ideas

  • Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles: Kavg = 3/2kBT, with T in kelvin. T(K) = T(°C) + 273. Doubling the kelvin temperature doubles the average kinetic energy.
  • Particles in a gas have a spread of speeds. A hotter gas's speed distribution is wider and peaks at a higher speed.
  • Pressure comes from particles hitting the walls. Hotter (faster) particles, or more of them in the same space, mean more force on each area of wall.
  • Ideal gas law: PV = nRT = NkBT. For a fixed amount of gas, PV/T stays constant, so you can compare two states. An ideal gas has particles with no size and no forces between them except in collisions; real gases behave this way at low pressure and high temperature.
  • Internal energy U is the total kinetic energy of the particles plus the potential energy of the forces between them. For an ideal monatomic gas it's all kinetic: U = 3/2nRT.
  • Heat Q is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. It always flows on its own from hot to cold, until the objects reach thermal equilibrium (the same temperature).
  • Energy is transferred by conduction (particle collisions, through contact), convection (a warm fluid rising because it's less dense) and radiation (electromagnetic waves).
  • Conduction rate Q/Δt = kAΔT/L is faster through a good conductor (big k), a bigger area, a bigger temperature difference, or a thinner layer.
  • Specific heat c is the energy needed to warm 1 kg by 1 K: Q = mcΔT. Water's is large (4186 J/(kg·K)).
  • IB Phase changes happen at constant temperature. The energy changes the particles' potential energy (breaking or forming bonds), not their kinetic energy: Q = mL, with L the specific latent heat of fusion or vaporization.
  • First law (AP form): ΔU = Q + W, where Q is heat added to the gas and W is work done on the gas. Compressing a gas does positive work on it; when it expands, W is negative.
  • PV diagrams: the work's size is the area under the path. Isobaric = constant pressure; isochoric (isovolumetric) = constant volume, so W = 0; isothermal = constant T, so ΔU = 0; adiabatic = no heat, Q = 0.
  • Second law: the total entropy of an isolated system never decreases. Entropy measures how spread out energy is. That's why heat flows from hot to cold on its own and never the other way.

Watch the sign of W

  • AP Physics 2 exam: ΔU = Q + W, with W = work done on the gas. Expanding gas: W < 0.
  • Tsokos and the IB data booklet: Q = ΔU + W, with W = work done by the gas. Expanding gas: W > 0.
  • Both say the same physics: the two W's are equal and opposite. Pick one convention per problem, and use the AP one on the AP exam. (The first law is HL-only in IB, so IB SL exams won't ask for it.)

Common mistakes

  • Using °C in the gas law or in Kavg = 3/2kBT. Gas-law temperatures must be in kelvin.
  • Saying 40 °C is “twice as hot” as 20 °C. In kelvin it's 313 K versus 293 K, only 7% more.
  • Calling heat and temperature the same thing. Temperature is a property of an object; heat is energy moving between objects.
  • Mixing up the two first-law sign conventions (see the box).
  • Thinking isothermal means no heat. Isothermal means no temperature change; it's adiabatic that means Q = 0.
  • Saying a metal feels colder because it's at a lower temperature. It's at the same temperature; it just conducts heat out of your hand faster.

How to solve a first-law problem

  1. Identify the process: constant P, V or T, or adiabatic? Sketch it on a PV diagram.
  2. Find W on the gas: −(area under the path). Expanding gives W < 0; compressing gives W > 0; constant volume gives W = 0.
  3. Find ΔU from the temperature change: ΔU = 3/2nRΔT for a monatomic ideal gas; isothermal means ΔU = 0.
  4. Use ΔU = Q + W to find whatever is left.
  5. For a full cycle, ΔU = 0, so Q = −W, and the net work's size is the area enclosed by the loop.
  6. Check signs: did heat go in or out? Does that make sense for the process?

Gas-law problems

  • Convert every temperature to kelvin first.
  • Same gas in two states: P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂, and cross out anything that stays the same.
  • One state, and you want n or N: PV = nRT (R = 8.31 J/(mol·K)) or PV = NkBT.

Mixing (calorimetry)

  • Energy lost by the hot objects = energy gained by the cold ones (with no losses to the room).
  • Write mcΔT for each object, with ΔT always positive, and set lost = gained.
  • IB If something melts or boils, add mL for that step.
Ideal gas law

A gas heated in a rigid tank

2.0 mol of gas at 27 °C fills a rigid 0.050 m³ tank. Find its pressure. It's then heated to 177 °C. What is the new pressure?

T₁ = 27 + 273 = 300 K
P₁ = nRT/V = (2.0)(8.31)(300)/0.050 = 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa
Constant V: P₂/T₂ = P₁/T₁, with T₂ = 450 K
  P₂ = (1.0 × 10⁵)(450/300) = 1.5 × 10⁵ Pa

The Celsius temperature went up more than 6 times, but the pressure only rose by half: the kelvin temperature went from 300 K to 450 K.

Calorimetry

Mixing hot and cold water

0.20 kg of water at 90 °C is mixed with 0.30 kg of water at 20 °C in an insulated cup. What is the final temperature?

Heat lost by hot = heat gained by cold:
  (0.20)c(90 − T) = (0.30)c(T − 20)
  18 − 0.20T = 0.30T − 6.0 → T = 48 °C

Same substance, so c cancels and the answer is a mass-weighted average. It lands closer to 20 °C because there's more cold water.

Latent heat IB

Melting ice, then warming the water

How much energy turns 0.50 kg of ice at 0 °C into water at 20 °C? (Lf = 3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg, cwater = 4186 J/(kg·K).)

Melt (0 °C stays 0 °C): Q₁ = mL = (0.50)(3.34 × 10⁵) = 1.67 × 10⁵ J
Warm: Q₂ = mcΔT = (0.50)(4186)(20) = 4.19 × 10⁴ J
Total = 2.1 × 10⁵ J

Melting takes four times as much energy as the warming after it, and the temperature doesn't change at all while it melts.

First law

A gas expands at constant pressure

A gas at a constant 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa expands from 0.010 m³ to 0.030 m³ while 10 kJ of heat flows in. Find the work done on the gas and its change in internal energy.

W on gas = −PΔV = −(2.0 × 10⁵)(0.020) = −4.0 kJ
ΔU = Q + W = 10 − 4.0 = +6.0 kJ (the gas gets hotter)

Of the 10 kJ that went in, 4 kJ left again as work pushing the piston out. In the IB form, W by the gas = +4.0 kJ and Q = ΔU + W gives the same 6.0 kJ.

Conduction

Heat through a window

A glass window is 1.0 m² in area and 4.0 mm thick, with k = 0.80 W/(m·K). The inside surface is at 20 °C and the outside at 0 °C. At what rate does heat flow out?

Q/Δt = kAΔT/L = (0.80)(1.0)(20)/(0.0040) = 4.0 × 10³ W

Halve the thickness and the rate doubles. Double glazing works by adding a layer of gas, which has a much smaller k than glass.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. A gas is heated from 300 K to 600 K. What happens to the average kinetic energy of its particles? What if it's heated from 20 °C to 40 °C instead?
    Answer
    300 K to 600 K doubles the kelvin temperature, so it doubles the average kinetic energy. 20 °C to 40 °C is 293 K to 313 K, only about a 7% increase.
  2. Use the particle model to explain why the pressure in a sealed can rises when it's heated.
    Answer
    The particles move faster, so they hit the walls more often and harder (with more momentum change each time). More force on the same area means more pressure.
  3. A fixed amount of gas is squeezed to half its volume at constant temperature. What happens to its pressure?
    Answer
    PV stays constant at constant T, so the pressure doubles.
  4. In a lab, you measure a sealed gas's pressure at several temperatures and plot P against T in °C. Where does the straight line cross the temperature axis, and what does that mean?
    Answer
    At about −273 °C. That's absolute zero (0 K), where the ideal-gas pressure would drop to zero because the particles would have no kinetic energy.
  5. 0.40 kg of a metal at 100 °C is dropped into 1.0 kg of water at 20 °C, and they settle at 23 °C. What is the metal's specific heat? (cwater = 4186 J/(kg·K))
    Answer
    Heat gained by water: (1.0)(4186)(3) = 12 558 J. Heat lost by metal: (0.40)c(77) = 12 558, so c ≈ 410 J/(kg·K).
  6. A gas is heated at constant volume. How much work is done on it? How does ΔU compare with Q?
    Answer
    No work: the volume doesn't change, so W = 0. Then ΔU = Q: all the heat goes into internal energy.
  7. When you pump up a bike tire quickly, the pump gets warm. Use the first law to explain why.
    Answer
    The compression is fast, so there's almost no time for heat to flow (nearly adiabatic, Q ≈ 0). Work is done on the air (W > 0), so ΔU = W > 0 and its temperature rises.
  8. A gas is taken once around a closed loop on a PV diagram. What is ΔU for the whole cycle? How can you find the net work?
    Answer
    ΔU = 0, because the gas ends in the same state. The net work's size is the area enclosed by the loop; then Q = −W for the cycle.
  9. An ice cube melts in a warm room. What happens to the total entropy of the ice and the room? Why can't the room spontaneously refreeze it?
    Answer
    Total entropy increases: the room's energy spreads into the ice. Refreezing would mean heat flowing from the cold ice into the warm room on its own, which would decrease total entropy, and the second law forbids that.
  10. IB Water boils at 100 °C on a stove. The burner keeps supplying energy, but the temperature stays at 100 °C. Where does the energy go?
    Answer
    Into the particles' potential energy: it breaks the bonds holding the liquid together and turns it into vapour (Q = mLv). Their average kinetic energy, and so the temperature, stays the same until all the water has boiled.
  11. Under what conditions does a real gas stop behaving like an ideal gas? Why?
    Answer
    At high pressure and low temperature, close to condensing. The particles are then close together and slow, so their own volume and the forces between them matter, and the ideal-gas assumptions fail.
