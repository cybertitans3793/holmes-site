Study guide

Unit 1 · Newton’s First Law

How to use this: read the key ideas, then cover each worked example and try it yourself before reading the solution. Do the self-check questions on paper before you open the answers, mark the ones you miss, and come back to them in a day or two without looking. Rereading won't do much; answering questions will.

Key ideas

  • Position is where something is relative to a chosen origin. Displacement Δx = xf − xi is the change in position: it has a sign (direction). Distance is the total path length, and it's never negative.
  • Velocity is the rate of change of position, v = Δx/Δt, and has a direction. Speed is how fast, with no direction. Average speed = distance ÷ time, which isn't always the size of the average velocity.
  • Position–time graph: the slope is the velocity. A straight line means constant velocity; a steeper line means faster; a flat line means at rest; a downward slope means moving in the negative direction.
  • Velocity–time graph: constant velocity is a horizontal line. The area between the line and the time axis is the displacement (area under the time axis, where v is negative, counts as negative).
  • Motion diagrams show the object at equal time intervals. Evenly spaced dots mean constant velocity.
  • Frames of reference: velocity depends on who is watching. In one dimension, add the velocities: your velocity relative to the ground = your velocity relative to the train + the train's velocity relative to the ground.
  • A force is a push or pull from one object on another. Contact forces need touching (normal, tension, friction, spring); gravity acts at a distance.
  • Common forces: weight Fg = mg points down. A normal force pushes perpendicular to a surface, as hard as it needs to. Tension pulls along a rope or string. Friction acts along a surface, against sliding (or against the way it would slide). A spring pushes or pulls with Fs = kx.
  • Free-body diagram: one dot for the object, an arrow for each real force on it, each labelled by what exerts it.
  • Newton's 1st law: if the net force on an object is zero, its velocity stays constant: at rest stays at rest, moving keeps moving in a straight line at the same speed. No force is needed to keep something moving.
  • Inertia is an object's resistance to a change in its motion. Mass measures it. Mass (kg) is the same everywhere; weight (N) depends on g.
  • Equilibrium means net force zero, so constant velocity, including at rest. The forces in each direction add to zero.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a position–time graph as a picture of the path. A line going up means moving in the + direction, not climbing a hill.
  • Mixing up distance and displacement. A full lap of a track has a big distance and zero displacement.
  • Thinking something needs a force to keep moving. Moving things slow down because of friction or air resistance, not because a “force of motion” runs out.
  • Drawing a “force of motion” or “force of the throw” on a free-body diagram after the object has left your hand. Only draw forces from things touching it, plus gravity.
  • Assuming the normal force always equals mg. It equals mg only when nothing else pushes or pulls vertically and the object isn't accelerating up or down.
  • Calling mass and weight the same thing. A 2 kg book weighs 20 N.
  • Thinking friction always points backward, against the motion. It opposes sliding between the surfaces: friction on your shoes and on a car's tires is what pushes you forward.
  • Averaging the speeds to get the average speed. Use total distance ÷ total time. The plain average of the speeds only works when equal times are spent at each speed.
  • Adding air resistance when the problem doesn't mention it. Leave it out unless the problem says it matters.
  • Thinking two objects side by side are going the same speed. Same position (crossing x–t lines) is not same velocity (equal slopes).
  • Using the average speed for a whole trip as the speed at one instant when the speed changes. For the speed at an instant, use a short stretch where the motion is steady, or the slope of the x–t graph right there.
  • Writing x = x0 + vt for a leg that starts later. Use x = x0 + v(t − t0), where t − t0 is the time since that leg began.

How to solve an equilibrium problem

  1. Pick the object you care about. Draw it as a dot.
  2. Find every force on it: gravity, plus one force for each thing touching it. Ask “what exerts this force?” for each one.
  3. Draw the free-body diagram with arrows roughly to scale. Label each arrow (Fg, FN, FT, f).
  4. Check the motion: at rest or constant velocity means ΣF = 0.
  5. Write ΣF = 0 for each direction (up = down, left = right).
  6. Solve, then check: is every force a real push or pull from something?

Reading motion graphs

  • Read the axes first. The same shape means different things on a position graph and a velocity graph.
  • Position–time: the value is where it is; the slope is the velocity.
  • Velocity–time: the value is the velocity; the area is the displacement.
  • Where two position–time lines cross, the objects are at the same place at the same time.

When do they meet?

  • Write x = x0 + vt for each object, using the same origin and the same + direction.
  • Something moving in the − direction gets a negative v.
  • Set the two positions equal and solve for t. Put t back in to find where.
Position–time graph

A walk there and back

A student walks from x = 0 to x = 12 m in 4 s, stands still for 2 s, then walks back to x = 0 in 3 s. Find the velocity in each part, the displacement, the distance, the average velocity and the average speed.

Velocity is the slope of each straight piece:

v=ΔxΔt v0–4=12−04=+3m/s v4–6=0 v6–9=0−123=−4m/s

Displacement and distance for the whole walk:

Δx=0−0=0 d=12+12=24m vavg=ΔxΔt=09=0 speedavg=dΔt=249=2.7m/s

The slopes give the velocities. Average velocity only cares where you start and finish, so a round trip has zero average velocity even though you were moving most of the time.

When do they meet?

Two cars heading toward each other

Car A starts at x = 0 moving at +20 m/s. At the same moment, car B is at x = 300 m moving at −10 m/s. When and where do they meet?

Write each car's position with the same origin and + direction:

xA=0+20t xB=300−10t

They meet where the positions are equal:

20t=300−10t →30t=300 t=10s x=20(10)=200m

The closing speed is 20 + 10 = 30 m/s, so they close the 300 m gap in 10 s. Car B's minus sign is what makes the two speeds add.

Frames of reference

Walking on a moving walkway

An airport walkway moves at 1.0 m/s. You walk along it at 1.5 m/s relative to the walkway. How fast are you moving relative to the floor? What if you turn around and walk the other way at 1.5 m/s?

Take the walkway's direction as +, and add the velocities:

vyou, floor=vyou, walkway+vwalkway, floor

Walking the same way:

v=1.5+1.0=+2.5m/s

Turned around:

v=−1.5+1.0=−0.5m/s

That's 0.5 m/s, backward relative to the floor.

Your speed depends on the frame. To someone riding the walkway you're always going 1.5 m/s; to someone on the floor you're going 2.5 m/s or 0.5 m/s.

Equilibrium

The normal force isn't always mg

A 2.0 kg book sits on a table. You press down on it with your hand with 8.0 N. How big is the normal force from the table?

Forces on the book: weight down, your hand down, normal force up. At rest, so ΣF = 0:

FN=mg+Fhand FN=(2.0)(10)+8.0 FN=28N

The table pushes as hard as it needs to so the book doesn't sink into it. Pull up on the book instead and the normal force gets smaller.

Average speed

Average speed isn't the average of the speeds

A cyclist rides 12 km out at 24 km/h, then 12 km back home against the wind at 8 km/h. What is her average speed for the whole ride?

Find the time for each half:

t1=12km24km/h=0.50h t2=12km8km/h=1.5h

Total distance over total time:

speedavg=dΔt=24km2.0h speedavg=12km/h

Averaging 24 and 8 gives 16 km/h, which is wrong. She spends three times as long at the slow speed, so the average is pulled toward 8 km/h. The plain average of the speeds works only when equal times (not equal distances) are spent at each speed.

Catching up

A car chasing a bus

A bus passes a gas station at a steady 15 m/s. Four seconds later, a car passes the same station at a steady 25 m/s, going the same way. When and where does the car catch the bus?

Method 1: one equation per vehicle. Start the clock (t = 0) when the bus passes the station, with x = 0 at the station. The car starts 4.0 s late, so its clock is t − 4.0:

xbus=15t xcar=25(t−4.0)

Set the positions equal:

15t=25t−100→10t=100 t=10s x=15(10)=150m

Method 2: ride along with the bus. When the car passes the station, the bus is 15 × 4.0 = 60 m ahead. In the bus's frame the car closes that gap at 25 − 15 = 10 m/s:

Δt=60m10m/s=6.0s

That's 6.0 s after the car passes the station, which is t = 10 s, and the car has gone 25 × 6.0 = 150 m.

Chasing in the same direction, the gap closes at the difference of the speeds; heading toward each other, it closes at the sum. Both methods give the same answer; the second is quicker once you see the head start.

Equilibrium · friction

Leaning against a wall

A 70 kg person stands with her feet out from a wall and leans on it with her hands. The wall pushes straight out on her hands with 150 N, and she doesn't move. Find the floor's normal force on her and the friction force from the floor, with its direction.

Forces on her: weight down, floor's normal force up, the wall's push away from the wall, and friction from the floor along the floor. At rest, so ΣF = 0 in each direction.

Vertical:

FN,floor=mg=(70)(10) FN,floor=700N

Horizontal:

f=Fwall f=150N,toward the wall

Nothing is moving, yet there's friction. The wall's push would make her feet slide away from the wall, so friction points toward the wall to stop that. Friction opposes sliding (or the way things would slide), not “the motion.” On a slick floor that can't supply 150 N, her feet slide out.

Motion diagram

Side by side isn't the same speed

A strobe picture shows two carts on side-by-side tracks, with a dot every 0.50 s. Both are at the 0 mark at t = 0. Cart A's dots are 1.0 m apart. Cart B's dots are at 0, 0.2, 0.8, 1.8, 3.2 and 5.0 m. Find A's velocity. When are the carts side by side again? During which interval do they have the same average velocity? Which is faster when they're side by side?

A's dots are evenly spaced, so it has constant velocity:

vA=1.0m0.50s=2.0m/s

B's gaps are 0.2, 0.6, 1.0, 1.4 and 1.8 m: they grow, so B is speeding up. Both carts' last dots are at 5.0 m, five intervals in:

t=5×0.50s=2.5s,x=5.0m

Same average velocity means the same gap. B's third gap (t = 1.0 s to 1.5 s) is 1.0 m, the same as A's:

vB,avg=1.0m0.50s=2.0m/s

In the last interval, B's gap is 1.8 m:

vB,avg=1.8m0.50s=3.6m/s>2.0m/s

Same place (dots lined up, x–t lines crossing) is not the same speed (equal gaps, equal slopes). When B matches A's speed, A is as far ahead as it ever gets (1.2 m); B only draws level later, when it's already going faster.

Data · average vs instantaneous

Timers along a bike path

Students with stopwatches stand at every 10 m mark along a straight bike path. They all start their watches when a cyclist starts from rest at the 0 m mark, and each stops their watch as she passes.

x (m)0102030405060
t (s)02.84.05.16.07.08.0

Find her average speed over the 60 m and her speed as she passes the 50 m mark. Why are they different?

Average speed uses the whole run:

speedavg=60m8.0s=7.5m/s

Time for each 10 m: 2.8, 1.2, 1.1, 0.9, 1.0, 1.0 s. It stops shrinking after about 20 m, so from there on she rides at a steady speed; the small ups and downs are reaction-time scatter. Use the widest steady stretch, 20 m to 60 m:

v=60−208.0−4.0=10m/s

The first 10 m took 2.8 s (an average of only 3.6 m/s) because she started from rest. That slow start drags the whole-run average below her steady speed.

The average speed for a trip is not the speed at a given instant when the speed changes. For the speed at an instant, use data from where the motion is steady (or the slope of the x–t graph right there), and spread the points out so timing errors matter less.

Distance vs displacement

A robot's path on a grid

A delivery robot starts at the origin. It drives 40 m east in 8.0 s, then 30 m north in 6.0 s, waits 16 s, then drives 80 m west in 20 s. Find its distance, displacement, average speed and average velocity. Where is it at t = 40 s?

Distance adds every leg:

d=40+30+80=150m

It ends at x = 40 − 80 = −40 m, y = 30 m. The displacement is the straight arrow from start to end:

displacement=402+302=50m

That's 50 m toward the northwest (up and to the left on the grid).

Total time: 8.0 + 6.0 + 16 + 20 = 50 s (the wait counts).

speedavg=150m50s=3.0m/s vavg=50m50s=1.0m/s

The average velocity points the same way as the displacement: northwest.

At t = 40 s it has been on the west leg (which began at t = 30 s) for 10 s, at 80 m ÷ 20 s = 4.0 m/s:

x=40−(4.0)(10)=0,y=30m

Distance counts every meter driven; displacement only cares where it started and ended. Legs at right angles combine with the Pythagorean theorem, not by adding. Waiting adds time but no distance, so it lowers both averages.

Position equations

One equation per leg of a trip

A student bikes to school along a straight road. She rides at 4.0 m/s for 150 s, waits 30 s at a light, then rides downhill at 7.5 m/s until she reaches school, 1500 m from home. Write her position equation for each leg. When does she arrive, and where is she at t = 240 s?

Each leg has the form x = x0 + v(t − t0), where x0 is where that leg starts and t0 is when. Home is x = 0.

Leg 1 (0 to 150 s), ending at 4.0 × 150 = 600 m:

x=4.0t

Leg 2 (150 to 180 s), stopped:

x=600m

Leg 3 (from 180 s):

x=600+7.5(t−180)

Arrival: set x = 1500 m in leg 3.

1500=600+7.5(t−180)→t−180=120 t=300s

At t = 240 s she's on leg 3:

x=600+7.5(60)=1050m

Check with the velocity–time graph: the areas are 4.0 × 150 = 600 m and 7.5 × 120 = 900 m, which add to 1500 m. Her average speed is 1500 m ÷ 300 s = 5.0 m/s.

The t − t0 is the time since that leg began. Writing 600 + 7.5t instead would put her at 2400 m at t = 240 s, past the school. The area under the v–t graph is a quick check on the equations.

Equilibrium · spring and string

Two blocks hanging from a spring

A spring (k = 150 N/m) hangs from the ceiling. Block A (2.0 kg) hangs from the spring, and block B (1.0 kg) hangs from A by a light string. Everything is at rest. Find the tension in the string and how far the spring is stretched.

Start with B: it has only two forces, the string pulling up and its weight. At rest, so they balance:

FT=mBg=(1.0)(10)=10N

Now A: the spring pulls up; its weight and the string pull down.

Fs=mAg+FT=20+10=30N

(Or treat A and B as one object: the string is inside it, so Fs = (3.0)(10) = 30 N straight away.) Then the stretch:

x=Fsk=30N150N/m=0.20m

Start with the object that has the fewest unknown forces. The string pulls down on A with the same 10 N it pulls up on B, and the spring holds up everything below it, so it stretches more than A alone would make it.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. A runner does one lap of a 400 m track in 80 s and finishes where she started. What are her average speed and her average velocity?
    Answer
    Average speed = 400/80 = 5.0 m/s. Average velocity = 0, because her displacement is zero.
  2. On a position–time graph, a straight line slopes downward. What is the object doing?
    Answer
    Moving in the negative direction at a constant speed. The slope is the velocity, and a constant negative slope means constant negative velocity. It doesn't mean it's slowing down or going downhill.
  3. A velocity–time graph is a horizontal line at 4.0 m/s from t = 0 to t = 6.0 s. What is the displacement?
    Answer
    The area under the line: (4.0)(6.0) = 24 m in the positive direction.
  4. A hockey puck slides across smooth ice at a steady speed. What force keeps it moving?
    Answer
    None. With friction and air resistance negligible, the net force is zero, so by Newton's 1st law its velocity just stays the same. The forces on it are its weight and the ice's normal force, and they balance.
  5. An elevator moves upward at constant speed. Compare the cable's tension with the elevator's weight.
    Answer
    They're equal. Constant velocity means zero net force, whether it's moving up, moving down or at rest.
  6. You hang a 0.50 kg mass from a spring and it stretches 0.10 m. What is the spring constant? How far would a 1.0 kg mass stretch it?
    Answer
    At rest, kx = mg: k = 5.0/0.10 = 50 N/m. Twice the weight gives twice the stretch: 0.20 m.
  7. You push on a heavy crate with 50 N, but it doesn't move. How big is the friction force on it?
    Answer
    50 N, opposite your push. The crate is at rest, so the forces balance. Static friction only pushes as hard as it needs to, up to its maximum.
  8. An astronaut's mass is 70 kg on Earth. What are her mass and weight on the Moon, where g = 1.6 N/kg?
    Answer
    Her mass is still 70 kg: the amount of matter (and inertia) doesn't change. Her weight is mg = 70 × 1.6 = 112 N, about ⅙ of her 700 N weight on Earth.
  9. Car A drives east at 25 m/s. Car B drives west at 20 m/s. How fast is car B moving as seen from car A?
    Answer
    45 m/s, toward the west. Take east as +: vB,A = vB − vA = −20 − 25 = −45 m/s.
  10. A passenger on a train moving at constant velocity tosses a ball straight up. Where does it land? What path does someone standing beside the track see?
    Answer
    Back in the passenger's hand. The ball keeps the train's forward velocity, so it moves along with the train. Someone beside the track sees it follow a curved path (a parabola), moving forward the whole time.
  11. A ball is thrown upward. Draw the free-body diagram while it's on its way up, ignoring air resistance.
    Answer
    Just one arrow: its weight, straight down. The “force of the throw” ended when it left your hand. It keeps going up because of its velocity (inertia), not because of a force pushing it up.
  12. In a motion diagram, the dots for a cart moving to the right get closer and closer together. Describe its motion.
    Answer
    It's moving in the + direction and slowing down. Each gap is the distance covered in the same time, so shrinking gaps mean a shrinking speed; the velocity is positive but getting smaller.
  13. A velocity–time graph is +3.0 m/s from t = 0 to 4.0 s, then −2.0 m/s from 4.0 s to 7.0 s. Find the displacement and the distance.
    Answer
    Areas: (3.0)(4.0) = +12 m and (−2.0)(3.0) = −6.0 m. Displacement = 12 − 6.0 = +6.0 m. Distance counts both as positive: 12 + 6.0 = 18 m.
  14. In a timing lab, every student starts their stopwatch 0.5 s late, so every time in the table is 0.5 s too small. Does this change the speed you get from the slope of the x–t graph?
    Answer
    No. Every point shifts left by the same 0.5 s, so the line moves but keeps the same slope. Only the intercept is off. That's one reason to take the speed from the slope of a best-fit line rather than from a single x ÷ t.
  15. Two runners race 30 m out and 30 m back, each running 6.0 m/s relative to the surface under their feet. One runs on the floor; the other runs on a moving walkway that carries her 2.0 m/s in the “out” direction. Who wins?
    Answer
    The one on the floor. Floor: 60 ÷ 6.0 = 10 s. Walkway: out at 6.0 + 2.0 = 8.0 m/s takes 30 ÷ 8.0 = 3.75 s, back at 6.0 − 2.0 = 4.0 m/s takes 30 ÷ 4.0 = 7.5 s, for 11.25 s in all. Her speeds 8.0 and 4.0 average to 6.0, but she spends twice as long at the slow speed.
  16. Who's right? You drag a box across the floor at a steady speed with a rope. Maya says, “To keep it moving, you have to pull harder than friction, or the box would stop.” Theo says, “Your pull is equal to friction.” Who's right?
    Answer
    Theo. Steady speed in a straight line means the net force is zero (Newton's 1st law), so the rope's pull and friction are equal and opposite. If you pulled harder than friction, the box would speed up. You only need extra pull to get it going faster, not to keep it moving.
