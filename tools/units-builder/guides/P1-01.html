Study guide

Unit 1 · Newton’s First Law

How to use this: read the key ideas, then cover each worked example and try it yourself before reading the solution. Do the self-check questions on paper before you open the answers, mark the ones you miss, and come back to them in a day or two without looking. Rereading won't do much; answering questions will.

Key ideas

  • Position is where something is relative to a chosen origin. Displacement Δx = xf − xi is the change in position: it has a sign (direction). Distance is the total path length, and it's never negative.
  • Velocity is the rate of change of position, v = Δx/Δt, and has a direction. Speed is how fast, with no direction. Average speed = distance ÷ time, which isn't always the size of the average velocity.
  • Position–time graph: the slope is the velocity. A straight line means constant velocity; a steeper line means faster; a flat line means at rest; a downward slope means moving in the negative direction.
  • Velocity–time graph: constant velocity is a horizontal line. The area between the line and the time axis is the displacement (area under the time axis, where v is negative, counts as negative).
  • Motion diagrams show the object at equal time intervals. Evenly spaced dots mean constant velocity.
  • Frames of reference: velocity depends on who is watching. In one dimension, add the velocities: your velocity relative to the ground = your velocity relative to the train + the train's velocity relative to the ground.
  • A force is a push or pull from one object on another. Contact forces need touching (normal, tension, friction, spring); gravity acts at a distance.
  • Common forces: weight Fg = mg points down. A normal force pushes perpendicular to a surface, as hard as it needs to. Tension pulls along a rope or string. Friction acts along a surface, against sliding (or against the way it would slide). A spring pushes or pulls with Fs = kx.
  • Free-body diagram: one dot for the object, an arrow for each real force on it, each labelled by what exerts it.
  • Newton's 1st law: if the net force on an object is zero, its velocity stays constant: at rest stays at rest, moving keeps moving in a straight line at the same speed. No force is needed to keep something moving.
  • Inertia is an object's resistance to a change in its motion. Mass measures it. Mass (kg) is the same everywhere; weight (N) depends on g.
  • Equilibrium means net force zero, so constant velocity, including at rest. The forces in each direction add to zero.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a position–time graph as a picture of the path. A line going up means moving in the + direction, not climbing a hill.
  • Mixing up distance and displacement. A full lap of a track has a big distance and zero displacement.
  • Thinking something needs a force to keep moving. Moving things slow down because of friction or air resistance, not because a “force of motion” runs out.
  • Drawing a “force of motion” or “force of the throw” on a free-body diagram after the object has left your hand. Only draw forces from things touching it, plus gravity.
  • Assuming the normal force always equals mg. It equals mg only when nothing else pushes or pulls vertically and the object isn't accelerating up or down.
  • Calling mass and weight the same thing. A 2 kg book weighs 20 N.

How to solve an equilibrium problem

  1. Pick the object you care about. Draw it as a dot.
  2. Find every force on it: gravity, plus one force for each thing touching it. Ask “what exerts this force?” for each one.
  3. Draw the free-body diagram with arrows roughly to scale. Label each arrow (Fg, FN, FT, f).
  4. Check the motion: at rest or constant velocity means ΣF = 0.
  5. Write ΣF = 0 for each direction (up = down, left = right).
  6. Solve, then check: is every force a real push or pull from something?

Reading motion graphs

  • Read the axes first. The same shape means different things on a position graph and a velocity graph.
  • Position–time: the value is where it is; the slope is the velocity.
  • Velocity–time: the value is the velocity; the area is the displacement.
  • Where two position–time lines cross, the objects are at the same place at the same time.

When do they meet?

  • Write x = x0 + vt for each object, using the same origin and the same + direction.
  • Something moving in the − direction gets a negative v.
  • Set the two positions equal and solve for t. Put t back in to find where.
Position–time graph

A walk there and back

A student walks from x = 0 to x = 12 m in 4 s, stands still for 2 s, then walks back to x = 0 in 3 s. Find the velocity in each part, the displacement, the distance, the average velocity and the average speed.

Velocity is the slope of each straight piece:

v=ΔxΔt v0–4=12−04=+3m/s v4–6=0 v6–9=0−123=−4m/s

Displacement and distance for the whole walk:

Δx=0−0=0 d=12+12=24m vavg=ΔxΔt=09=0 speedavg=dΔt=249=2.7m/s

The slopes give the velocities. Average velocity only cares where you start and finish, so a round trip has zero average velocity even though you were moving most of the time.

When do they meet?

Two cars heading toward each other

Car A starts at x = 0 moving at +20 m/s. At the same moment, car B is at x = 300 m moving at −10 m/s. When and where do they meet?

Write each car's position with the same origin and + direction:

xA=0+20t xB=300−10t

They meet where the positions are equal:

20t=300−10t →30t=300 t=10s x=20(10)=200m

The closing speed is 20 + 10 = 30 m/s, so they close the 300 m gap in 10 s. Car B's minus sign is what makes the two speeds add.

Frames of reference

Walking on a moving walkway

An airport walkway moves at 1.0 m/s. You walk along it at 1.5 m/s relative to the walkway. How fast are you moving relative to the floor? What if you turn around and walk the other way at 1.5 m/s?

Take the walkway's direction as +, and add the velocities:

vyou, floor=vyou, walkway+vwalkway, floor

Walking the same way:

v=1.5+1.0=+2.5m/s

Turned around:

v=−1.5+1.0=−0.5m/s

That's 0.5 m/s, backward relative to the floor.

Your speed depends on the frame. To someone riding the walkway you're always going 1.5 m/s; to someone on the floor you're going 2.5 m/s or 0.5 m/s.

Equilibrium

The normal force isn't always mg

A 2.0 kg book sits on a table. You press down on it with your hand with 8.0 N. How big is the normal force from the table?

Forces on the book: weight down, your hand down, normal force up. At rest, so ΣF = 0:

FN=mg+Fhand FN=(2.0)(10)+8.0 FN=28N

The table pushes as hard as it needs to so the book doesn't sink into it. Pull up on the book instead and the normal force gets smaller.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. A runner does one lap of a 400 m track in 80 s and finishes where she started. What are her average speed and her average velocity?
    Answer
    Average speed = 400/80 = 5.0 m/s. Average velocity = 0, because her displacement is zero.
  2. On a position–time graph, a straight line slopes downward. What is the object doing?
    Answer
    Moving in the negative direction at a constant speed. The slope is the velocity, and a constant negative slope means constant negative velocity. It doesn't mean it's slowing down or going downhill.
  3. A velocity–time graph is a horizontal line at 4.0 m/s from t = 0 to t = 6.0 s. What is the displacement?
    Answer
    The area under the line: (4.0)(6.0) = 24 m in the positive direction.
  4. A hockey puck slides across smooth ice at a steady speed. What force keeps it moving?
    Answer
    None. With friction and air resistance negligible, the net force is zero, so by Newton's 1st law its velocity just stays the same. The forces on it are its weight and the ice's normal force, and they balance.
  5. An elevator moves upward at constant speed. Compare the cable's tension with the elevator's weight.
    Answer
    They're equal. Constant velocity means zero net force, whether it's moving up, moving down or at rest.
  6. You hang a 0.50 kg mass from a spring and it stretches 0.10 m. What is the spring constant? How far would a 1.0 kg mass stretch it?
    Answer
    At rest, kx = mg: k = 5.0/0.10 = 50 N/m. Twice the weight gives twice the stretch: 0.20 m.
  7. You push on a heavy crate with 50 N, but it doesn't move. How big is the friction force on it?
    Answer
    50 N, opposite your push. The crate is at rest, so the forces balance. Static friction only pushes as hard as it needs to, up to its maximum.
  8. An astronaut's mass is 70 kg on Earth. What are her mass and weight on the Moon, where g = 1.6 N/kg?
    Answer
    Her mass is still 70 kg: the amount of matter (and inertia) doesn't change. Her weight is mg = 70 × 1.6 = 112 N, about ⅙ of her 700 N weight on Earth.
  9. Car A drives east at 25 m/s. Car B drives west at 20 m/s. How fast is car B moving as seen from car A?
    Answer
    45 m/s, toward the west. Take east as +: vB,A = vB − vA = −20 − 25 = −45 m/s.
  10. A passenger on a train moving at constant velocity tosses a ball straight up. Where does it land? What path does someone standing beside the track see?
    Answer
    Back in the passenger's hand. The ball keeps the train's forward velocity, so it moves along with the train. Someone beside the track sees it follow a curved path (a parabola), moving forward the whole time.
  11. A ball is thrown upward. Draw the free-body diagram while it's on its way up, ignoring air resistance.
    Answer
    Just one arrow: its weight, straight down. The “force of the throw” ended when it left your hand. It keeps going up because of its velocity (inertia), not because of a force pushing it up.
