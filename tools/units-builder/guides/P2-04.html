Unit 4 · Waves and Optics

How to use this: read the key ideas, then cover each worked example and try it yourself before reading the solution. Do the self-check questions on paper before you open the answers, mark the ones you miss, and come back to them in a day or two without looking. Rereading won't do much; answering questions will.

Key ideas: waves

  • A wave carries energy from place to place without carrying the medium along. In a transverse wave the medium moves perpendicular to the wave's travel; in a longitudinal wave (like sound) it moves back and forth along it.
  • v = fλ. The medium sets the speed; the source sets the frequency. Crossing into a new medium, the frequency stays the same, so the wavelength changes with the speed.
  • At a boundary, part of a wave reflects and part transmits. The reflected pulse is upside down when the wave hits a medium where it travels slower (like a fixed end), and upright when it hits a faster medium (a free end).
  • Electromagnetic waves are oscillating electric and magnetic fields, perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel. They need no medium and travel at c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum. In order of increasing frequency: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma.
  • Polarization: a polarizing filter lets through only one direction of the electric field's oscillation. Only transverse waves can be polarized, so light can and sound can't.
  • Refraction: light changes speed in a material, v = c/n, and bends at a boundary: n₁ sinθ₁ = n₂ sinθ₂ (angles from the normal). Going into a higher n, it bends toward the normal.
  • Total internal reflection happens going into a lower n, when the angle of incidence is bigger than the critical angle: sinθc = n₂/n₁. Optical fibres work this way.
  • Superposition: overlapping waves add their displacements. Two coherent sources (same frequency, steady phase) interfere constructively where the path difference is mλ and destructively where it's (m + ½)λ.
  • Beats: two sounds of slightly different frequency make a loudness that pulses at fbeat = |f₁ − f₂|.
  • Diffraction: waves spread out through a gap or around an edge, most when the gap is about the size of the wavelength. That's why you can hear around a corner but not see around it.
  • Double slits and gratings: bright fringes where d sinθ = mλ. For small angles the fringe spacing is Δy = λL/d. A grating (many slits) gives the same angles but sharper, brighter maxima.
  • Doppler effect: a source moving toward you is heard (or seen) at a higher frequency; moving away, lower. The wave's speed doesn't change, only the spacing of the wavefronts.
  • IB For light, when v is much less than c: Δf/f ≈ Δλ/λ ≈ v/c. A redshift (longer λ) means the source is moving away; it's how we measure the motion of stars and galaxies.
  • Standing waves form when two identical waves travel opposite ways in a confined space, making fixed nodes (no motion) and antinodes (biggest motion). Only certain wavelengths fit. The lowest frequency is the first harmonic (AP also calls it the fundamental).
  • IB Resonance: driving a system at its natural frequency makes its amplitude grow large. Damping (light, critical or heavy) lowers and widens the resonance peak and shifts it slightly lower in frequency.

Key ideas: optics

  • Reflection: the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, both measured from the normal.
  • A plane mirror makes a virtual, upright image the same size as the object, as far behind the mirror as the object is in front.
  • Curved mirrors: a concave mirror has focal length f = R/2 and can make real or virtual images; a convex mirror always makes small, upright, virtual images.
  • Lenses: a converging lens (f > 0) brings parallel rays to a focus; a diverging lens (f < 0) spreads them out. Mirrors and lenses use the same equation: 1/f = 1/so + 1/si.
  • Real images form where light actually meets (si > 0, upside down, can go on a screen). Virtual images are where light only seems to come from (si < 0, upright).
  • An object inside the focal length of a converging lens gives an enlarged, upright, virtual image: a magnifying glass. A diverging lens always gives a smaller, upright, virtual image.
  • Ray diagrams use three easy rays: one parallel to the axis (through, or as if from, the focus), one through the focus (comes out parallel), and one through the centre of a lens (straight on).
  • Thin films: light reflects from both surfaces of a thin film and the two reflections interfere. A reflection off a material with higher n flips the wave by half a wavelength. With one flip, the reflection is brightest when 2nt = (m + ½)λ; with zero or two flips, when 2nt = mλ.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the frequency changes when a wave enters a new medium. It's the speed and wavelength that change.
  • Thinking a bigger amplitude makes a wave go faster. Speed depends only on the medium.
  • Looking for total internal reflection going into a denser medium. It only happens going toward lower n.
  • Thinking a moving source changes the speed of its sound. Only the frequency you hear changes.
  • Giving a closed pipe even harmonics. A pipe closed at one end only has the odd ones.
  • Dropping the minus sign for a virtual image or a diverging lens's focal length.
  • Forgetting the half-wavelength flip when light reflects off a higher-index material in a thin film.

How to solve a lens or mirror problem

  1. Sketch a ray diagram first with two or three easy rays, so you know roughly where the image is and whether it's real or virtual.
  2. Assign signs: converging lens or concave mirror f > 0; diverging lens or convex mirror f < 0.
  3. Solve 1/f = 1/so + 1/si for si.
  4. Interpret the sign: si > 0 means a real image; si < 0 means a virtual one.
  5. Find the magnification M = −si/so: negative means upside down; |M| > 1 means enlarged.
  6. Check against your ray diagram. They should agree.

Standing waves

  • Draw the first harmonic: a fixed end or closed end is a node; a free or open end is an antinode.
  • Count how many half-wavelengths (or quarter-wavelengths, for a closed pipe) fit in the length L. That gives λ.
  • Then f = v/λ. Higher harmonics are whole-number multiples of the first (odd multiples only for a closed pipe).

Interference

  • Find the path difference from each source to the point.
  • Divide by λ: a whole number means constructive (bright, loud); a half-number means destructive.
  • For slits and gratings, use d sinθ = mλ, with d the distance between slits (for a grating, d = 1/(lines per metre)).

Leaving water, light bends away from the normal. Beyond 49°, none escapes: it all reflects back into the water, which is why a pool's surface looks like a mirror from underneath.

Double slit

Fringe spacing

Light of wavelength 600 nm passes through two slits 0.20 mm apart onto a screen 2.0 m away. How far apart are the bright fringes?

Δy=λLd=(600×10−9)(2.0)0.20×10−3 Δy=6.0×10−3m(6.0mm)

Move the slits closer together, or use longer-wavelength (redder) light, and the fringes spread out.

Standing waves

Open pipe vs closed pipe

A pipe is 0.85 m long and sound travels at 340 m/s. Find the first three harmonics if the pipe is open at both ends, and if it's closed at one end.

Open at both ends, the first harmonic is half a wavelength:

λ1=2L=1.7m →f1=vλ1=3401.7 f1=200Hz, then 400 and 600Hz

Closed at one end, the first harmonic is a quarter wavelength:

λ1=4L=3.4m →f1=3403.4 f1=100Hz, then 300 and 500Hz

Closing one end halves the first harmonic's frequency and removes the even harmonics, which is why open and closed pipes sound so different.

Thin film

The thinnest bright soap film

A soap film (n = 1.33) has air on both sides. What is the thinnest film that strongly reflects green light of wavelength 532 nm?

The air→soap reflection flips (soap has the higher n); the soap→air one doesn't. One flip, so the reflection is bright when:

2nt=(m+12)λ

Thinnest film (m = 0):

t=λ4n=532nm4(1.33) t=100nm

Inside the film the wavelength is λ/n, which is why n appears in 2nt; the λ in the formula is the wavelength in air, the colour you actually see. Different thicknesses reflect different colours, which is where the swirling colours on a soap bubble come from.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. A wave on a light rope passes into a heavier rope, where it travels slower. What happens to its frequency, speed and wavelength? Is the reflected pulse upright or inverted?
    Answer
    The frequency stays the same; the speed and wavelength both decrease. The reflected pulse is inverted, because it reflects off a slower medium.
  2. A 440 Hz note travels through air at 343 m/s. What is its wavelength?
    Answer
    λ = v/f = 343/440 = 0.78 m.
  3. Why can light be polarized, but sound can't?
    Answer
    Polarization picks out one direction of oscillation perpendicular to the wave's travel, so it only works for transverse waves. Sound in air is longitudinal.
  4. Two speakers play the same note in phase. At a certain point, one is 1.5 wavelengths farther away than the other. Is the sound loud or quiet there?
    Answer
    Quiet. A path difference of 1.5λ is a half-number of wavelengths, so the waves arrive out of phase and interfere destructively.
  5. A 440 Hz tuning fork and a guitar string sound together, and you hear 4 beats per second. What are the possible frequencies of the string?
    Answer
    436 Hz or 444 Hz. The beat frequency is the difference, so you'd need another test (like tightening the string) to tell which.
  6. Why can you hear someone talking around a corner but not see them?
    Answer
    Sound wavelengths (about 1 m) are similar to the size of a doorway, so sound diffracts a lot. Light's wavelength (about 500 nm) is far smaller, so it barely diffracts around everyday objects.
  7. You swap a diffraction grating for one with more lines per millimetre. What happens to the bright maxima on the screen?
    Answer
    They spread farther apart. More lines per millimetre means a smaller d, and d sinθ = mλ gives a bigger θ.
  8. An ambulance with its siren on drives toward you, passes, and drives away. Describe what you hear. Does the speed of the sound change?
    Answer
    The pitch is higher than normal as it approaches and lower as it moves away. The speed of the sound in the air doesn't change; only the wavefront spacing (wavelength) does.
  9. IB A hydrogen line emitted at 656.3 nm is observed in a galaxy at 662.9 nm. Is the galaxy moving toward or away from us, and how fast?
    Answer
    Away (redshift). Δλ/λ = 6.6/656.3 ≈ 0.010, so v ≈ 0.010c ≈ 3.0 × 10⁶ m/s.
  10. A string 0.60 m long, fixed at both ends, vibrates in its third harmonic. What is the wavelength, and how many nodes are there?
    Answer
    λ₃ = 2L/3 = 0.40 m. There are 4 nodes: one at each end and two in between.
  11. Light travels down a glass optical fibre for kilometres without escaping through the sides. Explain why.
    Answer
    It hits the inside wall at an angle bigger than the critical angle, going from glass into lower-index material, so it's totally internally reflected again and again.
  12. What kind of image does a diverging lens make of a real object, wherever the object is?
    Answer
    Always virtual, upright and smaller than the object, on the same side of the lens as the object.
  13. IB A singer shatters a wine glass by holding one note. Explain using resonance. How would damping change things?
    Answer
    The note matches the glass's natural frequency, so each push adds energy in step with the vibration and the amplitude grows until the glass breaks. Damping removes energy every cycle, which lowers and widens the resonance peak, so the amplitude stays smaller.
