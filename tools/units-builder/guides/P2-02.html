Unit 2 · Electrostatics

How to use this: read the key ideas, then cover each worked example and try it yourself before reading the solution. Do the self-check questions on paper before you open the answers, mark the ones you miss, and come back to them in a day or two without looking. Rereading won't do much; answering questions will.

Key ideas

  • Charge comes in two kinds. Like charges repel, opposites attract. Charge is conserved (never made or destroyed, only moved) and quantized: every charge is a whole number of e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.
  • Charging moves electrons. By friction, electrons rub off one object onto another. By contact, a charged object shares charge with a neutral one (same sign). By induction, a nearby charge pushes electrons around, and grounding lets some escape, leaving the opposite sign.
  • A charged object attracts a neutral one by polarizing it: the near side gets the opposite charge, and it's closer, so attraction wins.
  • Coulomb's law: F = k|q₁q₂|/r². It's an inverse-square law like gravity, but much stronger, and it can repel. Forces from several charges add as vectors.
  • Electric field E = F/q is the force per coulomb on a small positive test charge. It points away from + and toward −. A point charge makes E = kQ/r².
  • Field lines start on + and end on −, never cross, and are closer together where the field is stronger. Between two parallel plates the field is uniform: E = ΔV/d.
  • A charge in a uniform field feels a constant force, so it moves like a projectile in gravity: a straight-line acceleration along the field, or a parabola if it enters sideways.
  • Inside a conductor in equilibrium the field is zero, and any extra charge sits on the outer surface.
  • Electric potential energy of two charges: U = kq₁q₂/r (negative for opposite charges). Electric potential V = U/q (volts = J/C) is a scalar: for point charges, add V = kQ/r with signs.
  • Equipotentials are surfaces of equal V. They're always perpendicular to the field lines, and moving a charge along one takes no work. Field lines point from high V to low V.
  • Energy conservation in fields: a charge moving through a potential difference ΔV changes its potential energy by qΔV, and its kinetic energy changes by the opposite amount. An electronvolt (1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J) is the energy one e gains across 1 V.
  • Capacitors store charge and energy. For parallel plates, C = κε₀A/d. Q = CV and the stored energy is U = ½QV = ½CV². A dielectric (κ > 1) between the plates increases C.
  • IB Millikan's oil-drop experiment balanced charged drops between plates (qE = mg) and found every charge was a whole-number multiple of e, showing charge is quantized.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking positive charge moves when objects are charged. In solids it's electrons that move.
  • Forgetting to square r: 3 times the distance gives 1/9 the force.
  • Adding field magnitudes from two charges without thinking about direction. Field is a vector; potential is a scalar.
  • Assuming zero field means zero potential (or the other way round). Halfway between equal and opposite charges, V = 0 but E isn't.
  • Treating force and field as the same thing. The field exists even with no charge there; the force is qE on whatever charge you put there.
  • Expecting an electron to accelerate along the field lines. Negative charges are pushed the opposite way, toward higher potential.

How to solve a field or force problem

  1. Draw the charges with their signs, and mark the point you care about.
  2. Find each charge's contribution at that point: its size (kQ/r² for E, kq₁q₂/r² for F) and its direction from the signs.
  3. Add as vectors. On a line, use + and − for direction; in 2D, split into components.
  4. For potential, add scalars instead: V = Σ kQ/r with the signs of the charges.
  5. For motion, use F = qE and Newton's 2nd law, or energy: ½mv² changes by −qΔV.
  6. Check the direction makes sense: like charges push apart, and E points away from +.

Coulomb's law

Force between two charges

A +2.0 µC charge and a −3.0 µC charge are 0.30 m apart. Find the force between them.

F=k|q1q2|r2 F=(9.0×109)(2.0×10−6)(3.0×10−6)(0.30)2 F=0.60N

It's attractive, because the charges have opposite signs.

Each charge pulls on the other with the same 0.60 N (Newton's 3rd law), even though one charge is bigger.

Adding fields

Where is the field zero?

A +4.0 µC charge sits at x = 0 and a +1.0 µC charge at x = 0.30 m. Where between them is the electric field zero?

Between the charges the two fields point opposite ways. The field is zero where they're equal:

k(4.0μC)x2=k(1.0μC)(0.30−x)2

Take the square root of both sides:

2x=10.30−x →0.60−2x=x x=0.20m

The zero point is closer to the smaller charge, which makes sense: being close makes up for having less charge.

Charge between plates

An electron accelerated across a gap

Two parallel plates 2.0 cm apart have a 400 V potential difference. An electron starts at rest at the negative plate. Find the field, the force on the electron, and its speed when it reaches the positive plate.

E=ΔVd=4000.020 E=2.0×104V/m F=eE=(1.6×10−19)(2.0×104)=3.2×10−15N

Energy: the electron gains eΔV = 400 eV:

12mv2=eΔV=(1.6×10−19)(400)=6.4×10−17J v=2(6.4×10−17)9.11×10−31 v=1.2×107m/s

The electron gains exactly 400 eV, whatever the gap. Energy is the fastest route; using a = F/m and kinematics gives the same speed.

Potential vs field

Halfway between a + and a − charge

A +2.0 µC and a −2.0 µC charge are 0.40 m apart. Find the electric potential and the electric field at the midpoint.

Potential is a scalar, so add with signs:

V=k(+2.0μC)0.20+k(−2.0μC)0.20 V=0

The fields both point toward the − charge, so they add:

E=2kqr2=2(9.0×109)(2.0×10−6)(0.20)2 E=9.0×105N/C toward the − charge

Zero potential doesn't mean zero field. Potentials add as numbers and cancel; fields add as arrows and here they point the same way.

Capacitor

A parallel-plate capacitor

Two plates of area 0.020 m² are 1.0 mm apart in air and connected to a 12 V battery. Find the capacitance, the charge and the stored energy. What changes if a dielectric with κ = 3.0 fills the gap while the battery stays connected?

C=ε0Ad=(8.85×10−12)(0.020)0.0010 C=1.8×10−10F Q=CV=(1.77×10−10)(12)=2.1×10−9C U=12CV2=12(1.77×10−10)(12)2=1.3×10−8J

With κ = 3.0 and V fixed by the battery, C = κε₀A/d triples, so Q = CV and U = ½CV² triple too.

With the battery connected, V can't change, so a bigger C pulls more charge onto the plates. If the battery had been disconnected first, Q would stay fixed and V would drop to a third.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. You rub a balloon on your hair and the balloon becomes negative. What moved, and what charge does your hair end up with?
    Answer
    Electrons moved from your hair to the balloon. Your hair is left positive, with exactly as much charge as the balloon gained, since charge is conserved.
  2. A negatively charged rod is brought near a neutral, empty soda can on a table. The can rolls toward the rod. Why?
    Answer
    The rod pushes electrons in the can to the far side, so the near side is positive (the can is polarized). The positive side is closer, so its attraction beats the repulsion of the far side.
  3. Describe how to give a metal sphere a positive charge using only a negatively charged rod, without touching the rod to the sphere.
    Answer
    Bring the rod near the sphere, pushing its electrons to the far side. Touch the far side to ground so those electrons escape. Remove the ground, then the rod. The sphere is left positive, the opposite sign to the rod (charging by induction).
  4. Two charges attract with force F. What is the force if the distance between them triples? What if instead both charges double?
    Answer
    Tripling the distance gives F/9. Doubling both charges gives 4F.
  5. A proton and an electron are released in the same uniform electric field. Compare the forces on them and their accelerations.
    Answer
    The forces are equal in size and opposite in direction (same size of charge). The electron's mass is about 1836 times smaller, so its acceleration is about 1836 times bigger.
  6. How much work does it take to move a charge along an equipotential surface? Why?
    Answer
    None. The potential doesn't change, so qΔV = 0. Equivalently, the field is perpendicular to an equipotential, so the electric force is perpendicular to the motion.
  7. A proton starts at rest and moves through a 1000 V potential difference. How much kinetic energy does it gain, in eV and in joules?
    Answer
    1000 eV, which is (1000)(1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁶ J.
  8. You place extra charge on a hollow metal sphere. Where does the charge end up, and what is the field inside?
    Answer
    On the outer surface. The charges push each other as far apart as they can. Inside the conductor (and in the hollow) the field is zero.
  9. A capacitor is charged, disconnected from the battery, and then its plates are pulled farther apart. What happens to Q, C, V and the stored energy?
    Answer
    Q stays the same (it can't go anywhere). C = ε₀A/d decreases, so V = Q/C increases. U = ½QV increases; the extra energy comes from the work you do pulling the attracting plates apart.
  10. IB In a Millikan-type experiment, an oil drop of mass 4.9 × 10⁻¹⁵ kg hangs still between plates with E = 1.5 × 10⁵ N/C. What is its charge? How many extra electrons is that?
    Answer
    At rest, qE = mg: q = (4.9 × 10⁻¹⁵)(9.8)/(1.5 × 10⁵) = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, which is exactly 2e, so two extra electrons.
  11. Sketch the field lines between a positive point charge and a large negative plate. Where is the field strongest?
    Answer
    Lines leave the positive charge, spread out, and end perpendicular to the plate. They're most crowded right next to the point charge, so the field is strongest there.
