Unit 10 · Simple Harmonic Motion

How to use this: read the key ideas, then cover each worked example and try it yourself before reading the solution. Do the self-check questions on paper before you open the answers, mark the ones you miss, and come back to them in a day or two without looking. Rereading won't do much; answering questions will.

Key ideas

  • An oscillation is a back-and-forth motion about an equilibrium position, where the net force is zero. Pull it away and a restoring force pushes it back toward equilibrium.
  • Simple harmonic motion (SHM) happens when the restoring force is proportional to the displacement: F = −kx. Then a = −(k/m)x: the acceleration always points back toward equilibrium and grows with distance from it.
  • Amplitude A is the farthest distance from equilibrium (not end to end). Period T is the time for one full cycle; frequency f = 1/T is cycles per second (Hz). One full cycle covers 4A of distance.
  • Mass on a spring: T = 2π√(m/k). More mass or a softer spring means a longer period. It doesn't depend on the amplitude or on g, so a vertical spring has the same period as a horizontal one.
  • A vertical spring oscillates about a new equilibrium, where kΔy = mg. Measure x from there and everything else works the same.
  • Pendulum (small swings): T = 2π√(L/g). It doesn't depend on the mass or (for small angles) the amplitude, but it does depend on g.
  • At equilibrium: speed is greatest, and the net force and acceleration are zero. At the turning points (±A): speed is zero, and the net force and acceleration are greatest.
  • Graphs: position is a sine or cosine curve, x = A cos(2πft) if it starts at +A. The velocity is biggest where the position graph crosses zero, and the acceleration graph is the position graph flipped upside down.
  • Energy swaps between elastic (or gravitational) potential and kinetic. For a spring, E = ½kA² = ½mvmax², and at any point ½kx² + ½mv² = E.
  • Inertial and gravitational mass: a spring's period measures inertial mass (it works even in orbit), while a scale measures gravitational mass. They're always equal, which is why a pendulum's period doesn't depend on its mass.
  • IB Damping (friction or drag) makes the amplitude shrink over time. Pushing an oscillator at its own natural frequency makes the amplitude grow large: resonance. More damping means a smaller, broader resonance peak.

Equations

f = 1/Tfrequency and period

a = −(k/m)xSHM acceleration
T = 2π√(m/k)mass on a spring
T = 2π√(L/g)simple pendulum (small angles)
x = A cos(2πft)position, starting at +A

vmax = 2πfAmaximum speed (at equilibrium)

Common mistakes

  • Thinking a bigger amplitude means a longer period. It goes farther, but faster, and the period stays the same.
  • Thinking a heavier pendulum bob swings with a different period. Mass doesn't appear in T = 2π√(L/g).
  • Putting the maximum speed at the ends. The ends are where it stops and turns around.
  • Saying the acceleration is zero at the ends because v = 0. That's where the acceleration is biggest.
  • Calling the end-to-end distance the amplitude. That's 2A.
  • Thinking a vertical spring has a different period from a horizontal one. Gravity only moves the equilibrium point.
  • Measuring a pendulum's L only to where the string meets the bob. L runs from the pivot to the bob's center of mass.
  • Calling any repeating motion SHM. A bouncing ball repeats, but its force isn't proportional to displacement, so its period changes with height.
  • Using T = 2π√(L/g) for wide swings. It only works for small angles; big swings take a little longer.
  • Taking a vertical spring's amplitude from its natural length. A is measured from the new equilibrium, where the block hangs at rest.
  • Plotting T against m and expecting a straight line. T ∝ √m, so plot T² against m; the slope is 4π²/k.
  • Giving the K and U graphs the same period as the motion. They peak twice per cycle (period T/2) and never go below zero.

How to solve an SHM problem

  1. What is oscillating? A spring (period depends on m and k) or a pendulum (period depends on L and g)?
  2. Find the equilibrium position, and measure x from there.
  3. Read off A and T (or f) from the words or the graph.
  4. For timing questions, use the period formula. A quarter period takes it from an end to equilibrium.
  5. For speeds at a position, use energy: ½kA² = ½kx² + ½mv².
  6. Check with ratios: 4 times the mass gives 2 times the period; 4 times the length gives 2 times the period.

Reading SHM graphs

  • The amplitude is the height of a peak above the center line; the period is the time from one peak to the next.
  • On a position graph, the slope is the velocity: steepest at the center line, flat at the peaks.
  • Where x is positive, a is negative, and the other way round.

Mass on a spring

Period, top speed and top acceleration

A 0.50 kg cart on a spring with k = 200 N/m is pulled 0.10 m from equilibrium and released. Find the period, the maximum speed and the maximum acceleration.

T=2πmk=2π0.50200 T=0.31s

Energy: all the spring energy at the end becomes kinetic energy at equilibrium:

12kA2=12mvmax2 →vmax=Akm=(0.10)2000.50 vmax=2.0m/s amax=kAm=(200)(0.10)0.50 amax=40m/s2

Pull it back 0.20 m instead and the period stays 0.31 s, but the top speed and acceleration both double.

Energy at a point

How fast at x = 0.060 m?

For the same cart (A = 0.10 m), how fast is it moving when it is 0.060 m from equilibrium?

E=12kA2=12(200)(0.10)2=1.0J U=12kx2=12(200)(0.060)2=0.36J K=E−U=1.0−0.36=0.64J v=2Km=2(0.64)0.50 v=1.6m/s

More than half the way out, it still has most of its top speed: 64% of the energy is still kinetic.

Pendulum

A pendulum clock, here and on the Moon

A clock's pendulum has a period of 2.0 s. How long is it? What would its period be on the Moon, where g is ⅙ of Earth's?

T=2πLg →L=gT24π2=(10)(2.0)24π2 L=1.0m

On the Moon, g is ⅙ as big and T ∝ 1/√g:

TMoon=2.06=4.9s

The clock would run slow on the Moon. A mass on a spring would keep the same period there, because its period doesn't depend on g.

Reading a graph

What does the graph tell you?

An oscillator's position graph starts at its highest point, 0.040 m, and next reaches that peak at 0.80 s. Find its amplitude, period and frequency, where it is at t = 0.20 s, and its maximum speed.

Read the graph: A = 0.040 m and T = 0.80 s.

f=1T=10.80 f=1.25Hz

t = 0.20 s is a quarter period: it's at equilibrium, moving fastest, heading toward −x.

vmax=2πfA=2π(1.25)(0.040) vmax=0.31m/s

A quarter period always takes an oscillator from an end to the middle. At 0.40 s it is at the other end, x = −0.040 m.

Collision + SHM

Clay lands on an oscillating block

A 0.90 kg block oscillates on a spring (k = 90 N/m) and passes equilibrium at 1.0 m/s. At that moment a 0.10 kg lump of clay dropped from above lands on it and sticks. Find the new speed at equilibrium, the new amplitude, and what happens to the period.

The clay drops vertically, so horizontal momentum is conserved:

(0.90)(1.0)=(1.0)v v=0.90m/s

At equilibrium all the energy is kinetic, so ½kA² = ½mv², giving A = v√(m/k):

Aold=(1.0)0.9090=0.10m Anew=(0.90)1.090=0.095m Tnew=2π1.090=0.66s (up from 0.63 s)

The period is longer.

The sticky collision loses kinetic energy, so the amplitude shrinks; the extra mass makes the period longer. Try the same thing with clay landing at an end, where v = 0: the amplitude wouldn't change at all.

Reading an equation

What does x = 0.080 cos(5πt) tell you?

A glider on a spring moves along a track with position x = 0.080 cos(5πt), x in meters and t in seconds. Find its amplitude, frequency, period, maximum speed and maximum acceleration. Where is it at t = 0.050 s and at t = 0.20 s?

Match it piece by piece to x = A cos(2πft). The number in front of t is 2πf, not f:

A=0.080m2πf=5π→f=5π2π f=2.5HzT=1f=0.40s vmax=2πfA=(5π)(0.080) vmax=1.3m/s

Since T = 2π√(m/k), the ratio k/m equals (2πf)², so amax = (k/m)A becomes:

amax=(2πf)2A=(5π)2(0.080) amax=20m/s2

For the positions, put t into the equation. The angle 5πt is in radians, so set your calculator to radian mode:

x(0.050)=0.080cos(0.25π)=0.080(0.707) x=0.057m x(0.20)=0.080cos(π) x=−0.080m

Reading f = 5π ≈ 16 Hz is the classic slip; divide by 2π first. At 0.20 s (half a period) it's at the opposite end. In its first eighth of a period it moves only 0.023 m, because it starts from rest at the end. In degree mode the calculator gives 0.080 m at 0.050 s, as if it hadn't moved at all.

Inertial vs gravitational mass

One block, two ways to find its mass

A block of unknown mass hangs on a spring with k = 50 N/m. Pulled down a little and released, it makes 10 full oscillations in 6.3 s. (a) Find its mass from the timing. (b) Before it was set bouncing it hung at rest. How far below the spring's natural length should it have hung?

(a) Time 10 cycles and divide: your reaction-time error is spread over 10 periods instead of one.

T=6.3s10=0.63s T=2πmk→m=kT24π2=(50)(0.63)24π2 m=0.50kg

(b) Hanging at rest, the forces balance: the spring force holds up the weight.

kΔy=mg→Δy=mgk=(0.50)(10)50 Δy=0.10m

(a) measures inertial mass: how hard the block is to speed up and slow down. (b) measures gravitational mass: how hard Earth pulls on it. Every experiment gives the same number for both. On the Moon the timing in (a) wouldn't change, but the stretch in (b) would be ⅙ as much (about 0.017 m): the mass is the same, g is smaller.

Is it SHM?

A ball bouncing on the floor

A ball bounces on a hard floor with no energy loss, so it rises back to the same height every time. Is this simple harmonic motion? Compare the time for one bounce when it's dropped from 1.25 m and from 0.80 m.

System: the ball. Each bounce is a fall from rest plus an identical rise, each taking √(2h/g):

T=22hg h=1.25m:T=22(1.25)10=2(0.50) T=1.0s h=0.80m:T=22(0.80)10=2(0.40) T=0.80s

Now the SHM test: in the air the only force is mg, the same size and direction at every height. It isn't a restoring force that grows with distance from a center, and the period changes with the height.

It repeats, but it isn't SHM: its graph is a chain of parabolas with sharp corners at the floor, not a smooth cosine, and a higher drop takes longer. SHM needs F = −kx (a straight-line F vs. x graph through the origin), and only then is the period independent of the amplitude. A cart rolling back and forth between two bumpers fails the same test.

Six graphs from one motion

A hanging block: x, v, a, F, K and U against time

A 0.80 kg block hangs at rest from a spring with k = 80 N/m. It is pulled 0.050 m below that resting point and released at t = 0. Take up as positive. How far is the spring stretched at equilibrium? Find the period and the biggest values of v, a, the net force F, K and U, then sketch all six against time for one period.

Equilibrium is where the spring holds up the weight:

kΔy=mg→Δy=(0.80)(10)80 Δy=0.10m

Measure x from there, so A = 0.050 m (not 0.15 m). The period:

T=2π0.8080 T=0.63s

The peaks:

vmax=Akm=(0.050)100=0.50m/s Fmax=kA=4.0Namax=Fmaxm=5.0m/s2 Kmax=Umax=12kA2=0.10J

U is the potential energy of the spring–block–Earth system, counted from its value at equilibrium. With x measured from equilibrium it works out to ½kx², the same as for a horizontal spring.

The shapes: x starts at −A (the bottom). v starts at 0 and goes positive first (it moves up), peaking at T/4 as it passes equilibrium. a and F start at their positive peak (the net force points up, back toward equilibrium): they are the x graph flipped. K and U are never negative, and each peaks twice per period.

Learn one shape and read the rest off it: v is the slope of x; a, and F = ma, are x upside down; K is big wherever |v| is, U wherever |x| is. K and U repeat every T/2 = 0.31 s, and at every instant they add to the same 0.10 J.

Linearizing data

k and an unknown mass from a T² vs. m graph

A student hangs different masses on one spring and times 10 full oscillations for each:

m (kg)0.10 · 0.20 · 0.30 · 0.40 · 0.50
10 T (s)4.5 · 6.2 · 7.8 · 8.8 · 10.0

She then hangs an object of unknown mass on the same spring, and it makes 10 oscillations in 8.4 s. Use a graph to find the spring constant and the unknown mass.

T ∝ √m, so T against m is a curve. Square both sides of T = 2π√(m/k) to get a straight line through the origin:

T2=(4π2k)mslope=4π2k

Divide each time by 10, then square: T² = 0.20, 0.38, 0.61, 0.77, 1.00 s². Plot T² (y) against m (x) and draw the best-fit line. Take the slope from two points far apart on the line, (0, 0) and (0.50 kg, 1.00 s²):

slope=1.00s20.50kg=2.0s2/kg k=4π2slope=4π22.0 k=20N/m

The unknown: T = 0.84 s, so T² = 0.71 s². Read across to the line and down, or use the slope:

m=T2slope=0.712.0 m=0.35kg

Rearrange so the things you measured go on the axes and the thing you want sits in the slope. Use the best-fit line, not one data point: the line averages out the scatter in all five timings. Sanity check: 8.4 s falls between the 7.8 s and 8.8 s trials, and 0.35 kg falls between 0.30 and 0.40 kg.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. You double the amplitude of a mass on a spring. What happens to the period, the total energy and the maximum speed?
    Answer
    The period stays the same. The energy (½kA²) becomes 4 times as big, and the maximum speed doubles.
  2. You replace the mass on a spring with one 4 times as heavy. What happens to the period?
    Answer
    T ∝ √m, so the period doubles.
  3. A pendulum's string is made 4 times as long, and its bob is replaced with one twice as heavy. What happens to the period?
    Answer
    T ∝ √L, so the longer string doubles the period. The heavier bob changes nothing.
  4. A mass on a spring makes 20 full oscillations in 10 s. What are its period and frequency?
    Answer
    T = 10/20 = 0.50 s; f = 1/T = 2.0 Hz.
  5. A 0.20 kg mass hung on a spring stretches it 0.050 m. What is k, and what is the period when it bounces?
    Answer
    kΔy = mg: k = 2.0/0.050 = 40 N/m. T = 2π√(0.20/40) = 2π(0.071) = 0.44 s.
  6. Where in its motion does an oscillating mass on a spring have its greatest speed? Its greatest acceleration?
    Answer
    Greatest speed at equilibrium (all the energy is kinetic). Greatest acceleration at the two ends, where the spring force is biggest.
  7. A spring with k = 200 N/m oscillates with A = 0.10 m. Where are its kinetic and potential energies equal?
    Answer
    E = 1.0 J. Equal halves means ½kx² = 0.50 J, so x = √(1.0/200) ≈ 0.071 m from equilibrium (that's A/√2), on either side.
  8. Astronauts on the space station measure their mass by bouncing on a spring-mounted chair. Why does that work in orbit when a bathroom scale doesn't?
    Answer
    The chair's period depends on inertial mass (T = 2π√(m/k)), and inertia doesn't need gravity. A scale reads the normal force, which is zero when everything is in free fall together.
  9. A pendulum hangs in an elevator. What happens to its period while the elevator accelerates upward?
    Answer
    The period gets shorter. Accelerating upward acts like a stronger gravity (g + a), and T = 2π√(L/g) decreases as g increases.
  10. On a position–time graph of SHM, x is at its most positive value. What is the sign of the acceleration there?
    Answer
    Negative. The restoring force, and so the acceleration, always points back toward equilibrium: a = −(k/m)x.
  11. A student measures 0.80 m from a pendulum's pivot to the center of its bob, then times 20 full small swings in 36 s. What value of g does she get? Why time 20 swings instead of one?
    Answer
    T = 36/20 = 1.8 s. From T = 2π√(L/g), g = 4π²L/T² = 4π²(0.80)/(1.8)² ≈ 9.7 m/s². Her reaction-time error at the start and stop is the same whether she times 1 swing or 20, so dividing by 20 makes the error in T 20 times smaller.
  12. Who's right? A box full of water swings as a pendulum on a long string, while the water slowly drains out of a hole in the bottom. Ana: “The period can't change. A pendulum's period doesn't depend on its mass.” Ben: “If the box is very light compared with the water, the period gets longer, then shorter again, as it drains.”
    Answer
    Ben. L runs from the pivot to the center of mass of box + water. Full, the center of mass is at the middle of the box. As it drains, the water left sits low in the box, so the center of mass drops: L grows and the period gets longer. Once it's nearly empty, the center of mass rises back to the middle of the light box and the period shortens again. Ana is right that the mass itself doesn't matter, but L is changing. (With a heavy steel box, the box sets the center of mass and the period barely changes.)
  13. An oscillating mass on a spring is at x = A/2, halfway out. What fraction of its energy is kinetic, and how does its speed compare with its maximum speed?
    Answer
    U = ½k(A/2)² = ¼ of ½kA², so U is ¼ of the energy and K is ¾. Since K ∝ v², v = √(3/4) vmax ≈ 0.87 vmax. Halfway out, it still has 87% of its top speed.
  14. A block on a spring hangs straight down and stretches it 0.12 m. The same spring and block are set on a frictionless 30° ramp, with the spring along the slope. How do the resting stretch and the period compare with hanging?
    Answer
    Along the ramp only mg sin30° = ½mg pulls on the spring, so the stretch is half: 0.060 m. The period is the same, because T = 2π√(m/k) has neither g nor the angle in it.
  15. A pendulum clock and a clock run by a mass on a spring both keep perfect time on Earth. Both are taken to the Moon (g is ⅙ of Earth's). After one real hour there, how many minutes does each show?
    Answer
    The spring clock shows 60 min: its period doesn't depend on g. The pendulum's period is √6 ≈ 2.45 times longer, so it ticks 2.45 times less often and shows 60/2.45 ≈ 24.5 min.
  16. Who's right? A cart on a horizontal spring oscillates with amplitude A. At the instant it's at x = +A (momentarily at rest), a block with 3 times the cart's mass is dropped straight down onto it and sticks. Cam: “The period doubles, so the top speed halves, so the cart's biggest momentum halves too.” Dee: “The period doubles and the top speed halves, but the biggest momentum doubles.”
    Answer
    Dee. The mass is 4 times as big, so T = 2π√(m/k) doubles. The cart was at rest at the end, so the block takes no energy from it: E = ½kA² and A stay the same. vmax = 2πA/T halves. But momentum is (4m)(vmax/2) = 2 times the old value. Cam forgot the mass went up too.
  17. IB A child on a swing is pushed once per swing, at just the right moment. Why does the amplitude build up? What would friction (damping) do?
    Answer
    The pushes come at the swing's natural frequency (resonance), so each one adds energy in step with the motion and the amplitude grows. Damping removes energy every cycle, so the amplitude levels off at a smaller value, and without pushes it would die away.
