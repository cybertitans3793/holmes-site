Study guide

Unit 2 · Newton’s Laws

How to use this: read the key ideas, then cover each worked example and try it yourself before reading the solution. Do the self-check questions on paper before you open the answers, mark the ones you miss, and come back to them in a day or two without looking. Rereading won't do much; answering questions will.

Key ideas

  • A force is a push or a pull that comes from an interaction between two objects. It has a size (in newtons, N) and a direction.
  • Contact forces need touching: Fnorm, Ffrict, Ftens, Fair, Fapp, Fspring. Field forces act from a distance: gravity (Fgrav), electric and magnetic forces.
  • A free-body diagram shows every force on one object. Each arrow is labeled, points the way the force acts, and is longer for a bigger force.
  • The net force (Fnet) is all the forces added up, with directions. Think tug of war: Fnet tells you who wins, and by how much.
  • If every force is cancelled by an equal, opposite force, the forces are balanced and Fnet = 0.
  • Newton's 1st law: with balanced forces, an object at rest stays at rest, and a moving object keeps the same speed and direction. No force is needed to keep something moving.
  • Inertia is how much an object resists a change in its motion. It depends only on mass: more mass, more inertia.
  • Mass (kg) is the amount of stuff in an object. It is the same everywhere. Weight is the force of gravity on it, W = Fgrav = m·g, in newtons. Weight changes from planet to planet.
  • Newton's 2nd law: a = Fnet ÷ m. Double the net force, double the acceleration. Double the mass, half the acceleration.
  • The acceleration points the same way as the net force. The object does not have to move that way: if Fnet points backward, it slows down.
  • Friction opposes sliding. Ffrict = μ·Fnorm, where μ (“mu”) is the coefficient of friction. It depends on the two surfaces.
  • Static friction holds a resting object still, up to a limit. Once it slides, kinetic friction takes over, and it is usually smaller.
  • Newton's 3rd law: forces come in pairs. If A pushes on B, B pushes back on A just as hard, in the opposite direction, at the same time.
  • The two forces in a pair are equal, but their effects are not. The object with less mass gets the bigger acceleration.

Equations

Fnet = sum of forcesadd forces that point the same way; subtract forces that point opposite ways
a = Fnet ÷ mNewton's 2nd law. a in m/s/s, Fnet in N, m in kg.
Fnet = m · asame law, rearranged to find a force
W = Fgrav = m · gweight. On Earth g = 10 N/kg (9.8 is also fine).
Ffrict = μ · Fnormfriction. μ has no units.
Fnorm = Fgravfor an object on a level floor with no other up or down forces
a = Δv ÷ tfrom Unit 1, to link the acceleration to a change in speed

Common mistakes

  • Thinking a moving object needs a forward force to keep going. At constant velocity the forces are balanced.
  • Putting a weight in newtons where a = Fnet ÷ m needs a mass in kilograms.
  • Using just one force, or the biggest force, in place of Fnet.
  • Using the mass for Fnorm in Ffrict = μ·Fnorm. Fnorm is a force: on a level floor, it equals the weight, m·g.
  • Saying the two forces in a 3rd-law pair cancel. They act on different objects, so they never cancel each other.
  • Thinking the bigger or faster object pushes harder in a collision. The forces are always equal.

How to solve a Newton's 2nd law problem

  1. Draw a free-body diagram of the one object. Label every force.
  2. Find the weight: Fgrav = m·g. If you are given the weight, find the mass: m = Fgrav ÷ g.
  3. Up and down: if the object doesn't speed up or slow down vertically, Fnorm = Fgrav.
  4. Friction: Ffrict = μ·Fnorm. It points opposite the sliding.
  5. Net force: bigger force minus the smaller one in that direction. Give the direction.
  6. Acceleration: a = Fnet ÷ m, in the direction of Fnet.

Finding a missing force

  • Find Fnet = m·a. It points the same way as a.
  • The force on the side of the acceleration is bigger than the opposite force by Fnet.
  • Constant velocity or at rest? Then a = 0, Fnet = 0, and opposite forces are equal.

Which forces go on the diagram?

  • Is a planet nearby? Then draw Fgrav, down.
  • What is touching the object right now? A surface (Fnorm, maybe Ffrict), a rope (Ftens), air (Fair), a hand (Fapp).
  • Nothing touching? No contact force. A thrown ball has no “force of the throw” once it leaves your hand.

Spotting 3rd-law pairs

  • Write it as “A pushes B …” then swap the names: “B pushes A …” in the opposite direction.
  • Both forces are the same type and the same size.
  • Each object gets one force. To compare effects, use a = F ÷ m for each object.
Balanced forces

Pulling a sled at a steady speed

A child pulls a 45 kg sled across level snow with a horizontal rope. The rope pulls with 60 N, and the sled moves at a constant speed. Draw its free-body diagram. Find Fgrav, Fnorm, Ffrict and the coefficient of friction μ.

Weight:

Fgrav=m·g=(45)(10) Fgrav=450N

No up or down acceleration, so up = down:

Fnorm=450N

Constant speed means balanced forces, so left = right:

Ffrict=Ftens=60N

Now solve Ffrict = μ·Fnorm for μ:

μ=FfrictFnorm=60450 μ≈0.13

The sled keeps moving even though Fnet = 0. That's Newton's 1st law: balanced forces mean no change in motion, not no motion.

Finding acceleration

Shoving a bin of gym mats

You push a 50 kg bin of gym mats across a level floor with a 260 N horizontal force. The coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.30. Find the bin's acceleration. If it started from rest, how fast is it moving after 3.0 s?

Fgrav=(50)(10)=500N Fnorm=Fgrav=500N Ffrict=μ·Fnorm=(0.30)(500)=150N

Net force: right minus left.

Fnet=260−150=110N, right a=Fnetm=11050 a=2.2m/s/s, right

Speed after 3.0 s, from a = Δv ÷ t:

Δv=a·t=(2.2)(3.0) v=6.6m/s

Only the 110 N left over after friction speeds the bin up. Push with exactly 150 N and it would slide at a constant speed.

Finding a missing force

How hard do the drone's propellers push?

A 1.5 kg camera drone lifts straight up off the ground, speeding up at 2.0 m/s/s. Ignore air resistance. How big is the upward force from the propellers (Fapp)? What force would make it hover?

Fgrav=(1.5)(10)=15N, down

The net force points the same way as a (up):

Fnet=m·a=(1.5)(2.0)=3.0N, up

The up force must beat the down force by 3.0 N:

Fapp=Fgrav+Fnet=15+3.0 Fapp=18N

Hovering means a = 0, so the forces balance:

Fapp=Fgrav=15N

To speed up going up, the up force has to be bigger than the weight. To rise at a steady speed, the propellers only need 15 N, the same as hovering.

Mass vs. weight

A rover on Earth and on Mars

A 200 kg rover is tested on Earth (g = 10 N/kg), then sent to Mars (g = 3.7 N/kg). Find its mass and weight in each place. On both planets, its motor gives it a net force of 100 N on flat ground. Find its acceleration on each planet.

Mass does not change with location:

m=200kg on both planets WEarth=m·g=(200)(10)=2000N WMars=(200)(3.7)=740N

Newton's 2nd law uses the mass:

a=Fnetm=100200 a=0.50m/s/s on both planets

On Mars the rover weighs much less, but it is just as hard to speed up. Inertia depends on mass, not weight.

Newton's 3rd law

Two skaters push apart

Two ice skaters stand face to face and push off each other. Jamal (60 kg) pushes Priya (40 kg) with a 120 N force. Ignore friction. How hard does Priya push Jamal? Find each skater's acceleration.

3rd law: the pair of forces is equal and opposite.

Fon Jamal=120N, backward

2nd law for each skater on their own:

aJamal=12060 aJamal=2.0m/s/s aPriya=12040 aPriya=3.0m/s/s

They feel the same size force, in opposite directions. Priya has less mass (less inertia), so she gets the bigger acceleration. It doesn't matter who “does the pushing.”

Ratios

Changing the force and the mass

A cart on a track accelerates at 3.0 m/s/s. Then the net force on it is made 4 times bigger, and a load is added that doubles its mass. What is the new acceleration?

a goes up with Fnet and down with m:

anew=a×42=3.0×2 anew=6.0m/s/s

Multiply by the force factor, divide by the mass factor. You don't need the actual force or mass.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. A marble rolls onto a long, level floor at 2 m/s. Pretend there is no friction and no air. What will it do next? Which law tells you?
    Answer
    Keep rolling at 2 m/s in a straight line (Newton's 1st law). Nothing needs to push it to keep it going.
  2. Which has more inertia: 2 kg of feathers or 2 kg of lead?
    Answer
    The same. Inertia depends only on mass, and both are 2 kg.
  3. A 40 kg rover weighs 400 N on Earth. On Mars it weighs 150 N. What is its mass on Mars? What is g there?
    Answer
    Mass is still 40 kg: moving to another planet doesn't change how much stuff it has. g = W ÷ m = 150 ÷ 40 = 3.75, about 3.8 N/kg.
  4. A curling stone slides across the ice and slowly slows down. List the forces on its free-body diagram.
    Answer
    Fgrav down, Fnorm up (equal), and Ffrict backward, opposite the motion. There is no forward force: nothing is pushing it anymore.
  5. A box has three horizontal forces on it: 50 N right, 20 N left and 30 N left. What is the net force? What could the box be doing?
    Answer
    Right 50 N, left 20 + 30 = 50 N, so Fnet = 0. The forces are balanced: the box is either at rest or moving at a constant velocity.
  6. A 4.0 kg cart has a net force of 10 N on it. What is its acceleration?
    Answer
    a = Fnet ÷ m = 10 ÷ 4.0 = 2.5 m/s/s, in the direction of the net force.
  7. A wagon accelerates at 6.0 m/s/s. The net force is doubled and the mass is tripled. What is the new acceleration?
    Answer
    a = 6.0 × 2 ÷ 3 = 4.0 m/s/s.
  8. A 25 kg box sits on a floor. The coefficient of static friction is 0.50. You push it sideways with 100 N. Does it move? How big is the friction force?
    Answer
    Fnorm = 250 N, so the most static friction can hold is 0.50 × 250 = 125 N. Your 100 N is less than that, so the box stays put. Static friction matches your push: 100 N.
  9. A 2.0 kg backpack slides across a hallway floor with nothing pushing it. The coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.30. Find the friction force and the backpack's acceleration.
    Answer
    Fnorm = Fgrav = 20 N. Ffrict = 0.30 × 20 = 6.0 N, backward. Friction is the net force, so a = 6.0 ÷ 2.0 = 3.0 m/s/s, backward (it slows down).
  10. A ball has left your hand and is still rising. Ignoring air resistance, what forces act on it? Which way is the net force?
    Answer
    Only Fgrav, down. The net force is down even though the ball moves up, so the ball slows down. The push from your hand stopped when the ball left it.
  11. A soccer player heads a ball. Compare the force of her head on the ball with the force of the ball on her head. Why does the ball reverse direction while her head barely moves?
    Answer
    The forces are equal in size and opposite in direction (Newton's 3rd law). The player has much more mass than the ball, so the same force gives her a much smaller acceleration.
  12. A rower pulls the oars so the blades push water backward. What pushes the boat forward?
    Answer
    The water pushes the oars forward, just as hard as the oars push the water backward. That forward push on the oars moves the boat.
