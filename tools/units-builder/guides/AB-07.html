Circuits

Key ideas

  • A circuit needs two things: an energy source (a battery or an outlet) and a closed, conducting loop from the + terminal back to the − terminal. Any break stops the flow.
  • Current (I) is how much charge (Q) passes a point each second. It is measured in amps (A). 1 A = 1 coulomb per second.
  • The charge is already in the wires. The battery does not supply it, and the bulb does not use it up. The same amount of charge comes out of a bulb as goes in.
  • What the battery gives is energy. It pumps charge “uphill” from the − terminal to the + terminal. The charge loses that energy in the bulbs, heaters and motors.
  • Voltage (ΔV, in volts) is the energy each coulomb of charge gains in the battery or loses in a device. The + terminal is the highest-voltage spot in the circuit.
  • Charges drift very slowly, but they all start moving at the same moment. That is why a light comes on as soon as you flip the switch.
  • Conventional current goes the way + charges would move: out of the + terminal, through the circuit, and back into the − terminal.
  • Resistance (R, in ohms, Ω) slows the flow of charge. Charges bump into atoms in the wire. A longer wire has more resistance. A thicker wire has less. The material matters too: copper and silver are good conductors.
  • Ohm's law: ΔV = I·R. More voltage means more current. More resistance means less current.
  • Power (P, in watts) is how fast a device uses energy. 1 W = 1 joule per second. A 100-W bulb uses 100 J every second.
  • Your electric company charges for energy in kilowatt-hours (kWh), not for charge or electrons.
  • Wall outlets in the US are 120 V. They are wired in parallel, so every appliance gets the full 120 V and you can switch each one on or off by itself.

Common mistakes

  • Saying a dead battery is “out of charge.” It is out of energy: the chemicals inside are used up.
  • Thinking current gets smaller after it goes through a bulb. In one loop the current is the same everywhere.
  • Forgetting to flip at the end of a parallel problem. 1/Req = 0.5 means Req = 2 Ω, not 0.5 Ω.
  • Thinking more bulbs always means more resistance. In parallel, each new branch is another path, so the total resistance goes down.
  • Using minutes in E = P·t when you want joules. Change to seconds first. For kWh, use kilowatts and hours.
  • Using 1.5 V or 9 V for something plugged into the wall. Outlets are 120 V.

Magnetism (ideas only, no math)

Magnets and fields

  • Every magnet has two poles, north (N) and south (S). The pull is strongest at the poles.
  • Like poles repel. Unlike poles attract. Only the two ends facing each other matter.
  • Cut a magnet in half and you get two smaller magnets, each with an N and an S. You can never get a lone N or S pole.
  • Only iron, nickel and cobalt (and things made of them, like steel) stick to magnets. Copper, aluminum, gold and silver do not.
  • Every electron acts like a tiny magnet because of its spin. In most atoms the electrons pair up and cancel. In iron they don't, and neighboring atoms line up.
  • Iron has domains: small regions where the atoms all point the same way. In plain iron the domains point every which way. In a magnet most of them line up.
  • Stroking a nail with a magnet lines up its domains. Dropping, hammering or heating a magnet scrambles them and weakens it.
  • The magnetic field is the space around a magnet where another magnet feels a force. Field lines go from N to S outside the magnet, they never cross, and they are closest together where the field is strongest.
  • A compass is a small magnet. Its N end points along the field line.
  • Earth is a giant magnet. A compass's N end points north, so there must be a magnetic south pole near the geographic North Pole.

Electricity ⇄ magnetism

  • A current makes a magnetic field. A compass near a wire swings when the current turns on.
  • Around a straight wire, the field lines are circles. Right-hand rule: point your right thumb along the current, and your fingers curl the way the field goes. Reverse the current and the field reverses.
  • Wind the wire into a coil and you get an electromagnet. It acts like a bar magnet with an N and S end, and you can switch it off.
  • Make an electromagnet stronger with more current, more turns of wire, or an iron core inside the coil.
  • A changing magnetic field makes a current (induction). Push a magnet into a coil and current flows. Hold it still and the current stops. Pull it out and current flows the other way.
  • More current from induction: move the magnet faster, use a stronger magnet, or use more turns.
  • Generators spin coils and magnets to make electricity. Motors do the reverse: current near magnets makes them turn. Transformers use induction to change voltage, and only work with a changing (AC) current.

How to solve a series circuit

  1. Check it's series: one path, no branches.
  2. Add the resistors: Req = R₁ + R₂ + R₃.
  3. Find the current: I = ΔVbattery ÷ Req. This is the current in every resistor.
  4. Find each voltage drop: ΔV₁ = I·R₁, ΔV₂ = I·R₂, and so on. The biggest resistor gets the biggest drop.
  5. Check: the drops should add up to the battery voltage.

How to solve a parallel circuit

  1. Check it's parallel: the wire splits at a node into branches.
  2. Every branch gets the battery voltage: ΔV₁ = ΔV₂ = ΔVbattery.
  3. Find each branch current: I₁ = ΔV ÷ R₁, I₂ = ΔV ÷ R₂. The smallest resistor gets the biggest current.
  4. Add the branch currents to get the battery current.
  5. Find Req: add 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …, then flip the answer.
  6. Check: ΔVbattery ÷ Req should equal the battery current, and Req should be smaller than the smallest resistor.

How to solve a power, energy or cost problem

  1. List what you know: P (watts), ΔV (volts), I (amps), R (ohms), time.
  2. Pick the power equation that uses what you know: P = ΔV·I (most problems), P = I²·R, or P = ΔV²/R.
  3. For energy in joules: E = P·t with P in watts and t in seconds.
  4. For energy in kWh: change watts to kilowatts (÷ 1000) and time to hours, then multiply.
  5. For cost: multiply the kWh by the price per kWh.
Ohm's law

A resistor on a 9-volt battery

A resistor is connected to a 9.0-V battery, and an ammeter reads 0.30 A. (a) What is the resistance? (b) The battery is swapped for a 4.5-V battery. What does the ammeter read now?

(a) Solve ΔV = I·R for R:

R=ΔVI=9.00.30 R=30Ω

(b) The resistor doesn't change. Only the voltage does:

I=ΔVR=4.530 I=0.15A

Half the voltage gives half the current. You could have answered (b) without finding R: current is directly proportional to voltage.

Series circuit

Three resistors in a row

Resistors of 1.0 Ω, 3.0 Ω and 5.0 Ω are connected in series to an 18-V battery. Find the equivalent resistance, the current, and the voltage drop across each resistor.

Add the resistors:

Req=1.0+3.0+5.0 Req=9.0Ω

Current (the same in every resistor):

I=ΔVbatteryReq=189.0 I=2.0A

Voltage drops, ΔV = I·R for each one:

ΔV1=(2.0)(1.0)=2.0V ΔV2=(2.0)(3.0)=6.0V ΔV3=(2.0)(5.0)=10V

Check:

2.0+6.0+10=18V✓

Same current everywhere, so the biggest resistor gets the biggest share of the voltage.

Parallel circuit

Two branches on one battery

A 3.0-Ω resistor and a 6.0-Ω resistor are connected in parallel to a 9.0-V battery. Find the current in each resistor, the battery current, and the equivalent resistance.

Each branch gets the full 9.0 V:

I1=9.03.0=3.0A I2=9.06.0=1.5A

The branch currents add up:

Ibattery=3.0+1.5 Ibattery=4.5A

Equivalent resistance:

1Req=13.0+16.0=0.50 Req=10.50=2.0Ω

Check:

9.02.0=4.5A✓

Req (2.0 Ω) is smaller than either resistor. The smaller resistor carries more of the current.

Power at an outlet

Will the breaker trip?

A 1200-W space heater is plugged into a 120-V outlet. (a) What current does it draw? (b) What is its resistance? (c) A 900-W coffee maker is plugged into the same circuit, which has a 15-A breaker. Does the breaker trip?

(a) Solve P = ΔV·I for I:

I=PΔV=1200120 Iheater=10A

(b) Ohm's law:

R=ΔVI=12010 R=12Ω

(c) The coffee maker's current:

Icoffee=900120=7.5A

Outlets are in parallel, so the currents add:

Itotal=10+7.5=17.5A

17.5 A is more than 15 A, so the breaker trips. Every appliance you add in parallel adds its own current to the wires in the wall.

Energy and cost

What does a game console cost to run?

A game console uses 150 W. It is played 3.0 hours a day for 30 days. Electricity costs $0.14 per kWh. How much energy does it use, and what does it cost?

Change to kilowatts and total hours:

P=150W=0.150kW t=(3.0)(30)=90h

Energy:

E=P·t=(0.150)(90) E=13.5kWh

Cost:

13.5×0.14 cost=$1.89

The same energy in joules:

(13.5)(3.6×106)=4.86×107J

The company bills you for energy (kWh). A small power used for many hours can cost as much as a big power used for a few minutes.

Electromagnet (no math)

Changing an electromagnet

A coil of wire around an iron nail, connected to a battery, picks up 8 paper clips. Predict what happens to the number of clips, and explain, if you: (a) wrap more turns of wire, (b) pull the nail out of the coil, (c) flip the battery around, (d) disconnect the battery.

(a) More clips. Each turn adds its own field, and the turns add up.

(b) Fewer clips. The nail's domains were lining up with the coil's field and adding to it. Without the iron core, only the coil's field is left.

(c) About the same number. The current reverses, so the N and S ends swap. The clips are plain iron, so they are pulled toward either pole.

(d) The clips fall off (maybe one or two stay for a moment). No current, no field. The nail's domains mostly scramble again.

An electromagnet is a magnet you can control: change the current, the turns or the core, and you change its strength. That is why scrapyard cranes use one.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. Your phone dies and you plug it in to “recharge” it. Was the phone out of charge? What was it really out of, and what does plugging it in do?
    Answer
    No. The charge is still in the wires and the battery. The battery ran out of energy: the chemicals that supply energy were used up. Plugging it in uses energy from the outlet to run the chemical reaction backward, so the battery can supply energy again.
  2. 45 C of charge passes a point in a wire in 15 s. What is the current? How much charge passes that point in one minute?
    Answer
    I = Q/t = 45/15 = 3.0 A. In 60 s: Q = I·t = (3.0)(60) = 180 C.
  3. Heavy-duty extension cords have thick wires. Why? And why is a 15 m cord worse than a 3 m cord of the same thickness for a space heater?
    Answer
    Thicker wire has less resistance, and a longer wire has more. Less resistance means less energy wasted heating the cord, so the thick, short cord is best.
  4. A circuit has a current of 0.60 A. The battery voltage is tripled and the resistance is doubled. What is the new current?
    Answer
    Tripling ΔV triples the current; doubling R halves it. 0.60 × 3 ÷ 2 = 0.90 A.
  5. A 600-W blender is plugged into a 120-V outlet. Find the current through it and its resistance.
    Answer
    I = P ÷ ΔV = 600 ÷ 120 = 5.0 A. R = ΔV ÷ I = 120 ÷ 5.0 = 24 Ω.
  6. Three identical bulbs are in series with a 12-V battery. What is the voltage across each bulb? A fourth identical bulb is added in series. What happens to the voltage across each bulb, the current, and the brightness?
    Answer
    12 ÷ 3 = 4.0 V each. With four bulbs: 12 ÷ 4 = 3.0 V each. The total resistance goes up, so the current goes down, and every bulb gets dimmer.
  7. Resistors of 10 Ω, 20 Ω and 20 Ω are in parallel with a 10-V battery. Find each branch current, the battery current and Req. Then the 10-Ω resistor is removed. What happens to the other two branches?
    Answer
    I = 10/10 = 1.0 A, 10/20 = 0.50 A, 10/20 = 0.50 A. Battery current = 2.0 A. 1/Req = 0.10 + 0.05 + 0.05 = 0.20, so Req = 5.0 Ω (check: 10/5.0 = 2.0 A). Remove the 10-Ω resistor: the other two still get 10 V and still carry 0.50 A each. Only the battery current changes, down to 1.0 A.
  8. A 900-W coffee maker runs for 40 minutes. How much energy does it use in joules and in kWh? What does that cost at $0.15/kWh?
    Answer
    Joules: E = (900)(2400 s) = 2,160,000 J = 2.16 × 10⁶ J. kWh: (0.90 kW)(⅔ h) = 0.60 kWh. Cost: 0.60 × 0.15 = $0.09, about 9¢.
  9. A bar magnet is cut into three pieces. How many poles does the middle piece have? Explain using domains.
    Answer
    Two: an N and an S, pointing the same way as the original magnet. The domains inside the middle piece are still lined up, so it is a complete magnet. You can never get a single pole.
  10. A steel can sticks to a magnet but an aluminum can doesn't. Why?
    Answer
    Steel is mostly iron. Iron's atoms act like tiny magnets (unpaired electron spins) and form domains, which line up near the magnet, so the can becomes a temporary magnet and is pulled in. In aluminum the electron magnets cancel or don't line up, so there's nothing for the magnet to pull on.
  11. A wire runs straight up out of your desk with current going up. You place compasses around the wire. Describe the field. What happens if the current is reversed?
    Answer
    The field lines are circles around the wire. Right-hand rule: thumb up, fingers curl counterclockwise as seen from above, so the compass needles point around the wire counterclockwise. Reverse the current and every needle flips to point clockwise.
  12. A magnet is pushed into a coil connected to a meter, held still inside, and then pulled out. What does the meter show at each step? What could you change to get a bigger reading?
    Answer
    Pushed in: a current. Held still: no current, because the field isn't changing. Pulled out: a current the other way. For more current: move the magnet faster, use a stronger magnet, or use a coil with more turns.
