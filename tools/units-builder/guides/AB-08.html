Waves

Wave basics

  • A wave is a disturbance that carries energy from place to place. It does not carry the material along. Each bit of the medium just vibrates back and forth around one spot.
  • Every wave starts with a vibration.
  • Transverse wave: the medium moves perpendicular to the wave's direction (a rope shaken up and down). It has crests and troughs.
  • Longitudinal wave: the medium moves parallel to the wave's direction (a Slinky pushed and pulled). It has compressions (squeezed together) and rarefactions (spread apart). Sound is longitudinal.
  • Mechanical waves need a medium (rope, water, air, rock). Sound can't travel through empty space. Electromagnetic waves (light, radio, X-rays) don't need a medium and all travel at c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum.
  • Amplitude (m): how far the medium moves from its rest position. Crest to trough is two amplitudes.
  • Wavelength λ (m): the length of one full cycle, like crest to crest.
  • Frequency f (Hz): how many cycles each second. Period T (s): the time for one cycle. T = 1/f.
  • Wave speed depends on the medium only. Changing the frequency changes the wavelength, not the speed.

What waves do

  • At a boundary, part of a wave reflects and part goes through (is transmitted).
  • Fixed end (or light rope → heavy rope): the reflected pulse comes back upside down. Free end (or heavy → light): it comes back right side up.
  • The frequency never changes at a boundary. The speed and wavelength do. Waves on a rope are faster, with a longer wavelength, in the lighter (less dense) rope.
  • Interference: when two waves meet, their displacements add (superposition). Crest + crest = bigger: constructive. Crest + trough = smaller: destructive. Then they pass through each other unchanged.
  • Standing waves: a wave and its reflection interfere to make a pattern that seems to stand still. Nodes don't move. Antinodes move the most. Each loop is half a wavelength.

Sound

  • Sound is a longitudinal, mechanical wave. Air particles vibrate back and forth; they do not travel from the speaker to your ear.
  • Sound moves at about 340 m/s in air, and faster in water and solids.
  • Pitch = frequency. High pitch means high frequency and short wavelength.
  • Loudness = amplitude. Yelling makes the air vibrate farther, not faster or more often.
  • An echo is reflected sound. The sound goes there and back, so the distance to the wall is half of (speed × time).
  • Doppler effect: when a source moves toward you, the waves bunch up and you hear a higher pitch. Moving away, they spread out and the pitch is lower. The source itself doesn't change its frequency, and the waves don't travel faster.
  • Light does it too: light from a star or galaxy moving away is red-shifted (its spectrum lines shift toward longer wavelengths).

Light: reflection and refraction

  • The normal is a line drawn perpendicular (90°) to the surface where the ray hits. All angles are measured from the normal, not from the surface.
  • Law of reflection: angle of incidence = angle of reflection.
  • A flat mirror makes an image behind the mirror, as far behind as the object is in front.
  • Refraction is bending when light crosses into a new material, because its speed changes. A ray along the normal (0°) goes straight through without bending.
  • Index of refraction n = c/v. A bigger n means light is slower there (the material is more “optically dense”). Air ≈ 1.00, water 1.33, glass ≈ 1.5.
  • Into a slower material (bigger n), light bends toward the normal. Into a faster material (smaller n), it bends away from the normal.
  • Snell's law gives the angles: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂.

Common mistakes

  • Calling the crest-to-trough height the amplitude. Amplitude is from the middle (rest position) to a crest.
  • Thinking a higher frequency makes a wave faster. In the same medium, the speed stays the same and the wavelength gets shorter.
  • Forgetting to divide by 2 in an echo problem.
  • Thinking the Doppler effect is about loudness. It's about pitch (frequency).
  • Measuring angles from the surface instead of the normal.
  • Counting nodes wrong: the two fixed ends of a string are nodes too. A pattern with 3 loops has 4 nodes.
  • Calculator in radians. Snell's law needs degree mode.

Earth's interior and plates

Inside the Earth

  • Layers from the outside in: crust (thin, solid rock), mantle (solid rock that flows very slowly), outer core (liquid iron and nickel), inner core (solid iron and nickel).
  • The inner core is the hottest layer, but the huge pressure keeps it solid.
  • Nobody has drilled through the crust. We know what's inside from seismic waves: the waves an earthquake sends through the Earth.
  • P waves (primary) are push–pull (longitudinal). They are faster and arrive first. They go through solids and liquids.
  • S waves (secondary) are side to side (transverse). They are slower and arrive second. They can't go through liquids.
  • On the far side of the Earth from an earthquake there is a big zone where no S waves arrive (the S-wave shadow zone). S waves can't get through liquid, so something is blocking them: the outer core is liquid.
  • Going deeper, the temperature, pressure and density all increase. The densest material, iron and nickel, sank to the center long ago.

Earthquakes

  • The epicenter is the spot on Earth's surface right above where the rock breaks underground.
  • P and S waves leave the break at the same moment. P waves are faster, so they arrive first.
  • The farther away a station is, the more the S waves fall behind. So the lag (S arrival time − P arrival time) grows with distance.
  • A travel-time graph shows how long each wave takes to reach each distance. Find the distance where the S curve is exactly the lag above the P curve.
  • One station gives only a distance, not a direction: the quake could be anywhere on a circle around it. Two circles cross at two points. A third station picks the one spot where all three circles meet: the epicenter.

Why plates move: mantle convection

  • The crust and the stiff top of the mantle are broken into plates. They ride on softer mantle rock below that can flow very slowly.
  • Heat from the core warms the bottom of the mantle. Hot rock is less dense, so it rises. Near the top it spreads sideways under the plates, cools, becomes denser and sinks. This loop is a convection current, like hot water rising in a pot.
  • Where hot rock rises, plates are pulled apart: a mid-ocean ridge.
  • Where cooler rock sinks, a plate dives back down: a subduction zone with a trench. The sinking edge also pulls the rest of the plate along.
  • The flow is slow: a few centimeters a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow.

Plates and boundaries

  • Ocean crust is thin and dense (basalt). Continental crust is thick and less dense, so it floats higher.
  • Divergent boundary: plates move apart. Magma rises and makes new crust. Examples: the Mid-Atlantic Ridge (Iceland sits on it) and the East African Rift.
  • Convergent boundary: plates move together. Ocean crust meeting a continent sinks under it (subduction), making a deep trench, earthquakes and a chain of volcanoes: the Andes in South America. Two continents can't sink, so they crumple into mountains: India pushing into Asia made the Himalayas.
  • Transform boundary: plates slide past each other. Lots of earthquakes, few volcanoes. Example: the San Andreas Fault in California.
  • Most earthquakes and volcanoes happen along plate boundaries. That's why Japan has many and Maryland has very few.
  • Magnetic stripes: new sea floor at a ridge records Earth's magnetic field, which flips now and then. The stripes match on both sides, which shows the sea floor spreading.
  • Hot spots stay put in the mantle while the plate moves over them, making a chain of volcanoes that get older farther from the hot spot. Plate speed = distance ÷ age.

How to solve a wave problem

  1. Sort the numbers. Is each one a distance (λ, amplitude), a time (T), a count per second (f) or a speed (v)?
  2. Read the picture carefully. Count how many full waves fit in a length. Crest to trough is 2 amplitudes.
  3. Get f and T from counting: f = cycles ÷ seconds, T = 1/f.
  4. Use v = f·λ to find the one you're missing.
  5. Check units: change cm to m and ms to s if the question needs it.

How to solve a refraction problem

  1. Draw the normal where the ray hits the boundary. Measure every angle from it.
  2. Label each side with its n. Material 1 is where the light starts.
  3. Predict the bend: bigger n → toward the normal; smaller n → away from it.
  4. Write Snell's law, n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, and solve for the unknown.
  5. To find an angle, get its sine first, then use sin⁻¹ (calculator in degree mode).
  6. Check that your answer bends the way you predicted.

How to locate an earthquake

  1. Read the seismogram at one station: the time the P wave starts and the time the S wave starts.
  2. Find the lag, S − P. Change minutes to seconds if the graph is in seconds.
  3. Mark the lag on a strip of paper: hold its edge along the graph's time axis and make two marks the lag apart.
  4. Slide the strip to the right, keeping it straight up and down, until one mark sits on the P curve and the other on the S curve. Read the distance straight below.
  5. Draw a circle with that radius around the station. The quake is somewhere on that circle.
  6. Repeat for two more stations. The one spot where all three circles cross is the epicenter.
Reading a wave

A wave on a rope

A snapshot of a rope shows exactly two full waves along 3.0 m of rope. The rope is 0.40 m from the top of a crest to the bottom of a trough. A ribbon tied to the rope goes up and down 12 times in 4.0 s. Find the amplitude, wavelength, frequency, period and speed.

Amplitude is half of crest to trough:

A=0.402=0.20m

Two waves fit in 3.0 m:

λ=3.02=1.5m

Frequency and period:

f=12 cycles4.0 s=3.0Hz T=1f=13.0=0.33s

Speed:

v=fλ=(3.0)(1.5) v=4.5m/s

The ribbon only moves up and down. It's the energy (and the wave shape) that moves along the rope at 4.5 m/s.

Echo

How far away is the moth?

A bat sends out a click and hears the echo from a moth 0.020 s later. Sound travels at 340 m/s. How far away is the moth?

Distance the sound travels in 0.020 s (there and back):

dtotal=v·t=(340)(0.020)=6.8m

The moth is halfway:

d=6.82=3.4m

Bats find insects this way (echolocation), and ships find the depth of the ocean the same way (sonar).

Sound and v = fλ

A tuning fork in air and in water

A 512-Hz tuning fork makes sound in air (340 m/s). (a) What is the wavelength? (b) The same fork is struck underwater, where sound travels at 1480 m/s. What are the frequency and wavelength there?

(a) Solve v = fλ for λ:

λ=vf=340512 λair=0.66m

(b) The fork sets the frequency, so it stays 512 Hz. The medium sets the speed:

λ=1480512 λwater=2.9m

Same source, same frequency (same pitch). The faster medium stretches each wave out, so the wavelength is longer.

Standing wave

Counting loops and nodes

A 2.4-m rope tied at both ends vibrates in the pattern shown, with three loops, at 5.0 Hz. (a) How many nodes and antinodes are there? (b) What is the wavelength? (c) How fast do waves travel on this rope?

(a) Count the dots and the loops, including both ends:

4 nodes,3 antinodes

(b) Each loop is half a wavelength:

loop=2.43=0.80m λ=2(0.80)=1.6m

(c) Wave speed:

v=fλ=(5.0)(1.6) v=8.0m/s

The number of antinodes equals the number of loops. The number of nodes is always one more.

Refraction

Light leaving a glass tank wall into water

Light inside the glass wall of an aquarium (n = 1.50) hits the boundary with the water (n = 1.33) at 40° from the normal. (a) Does it bend toward or away from the normal? (b) Find the angle of refraction. (c) How fast does light travel in the glass?

(a) It goes from a bigger n to a smaller n (into a faster material), so it bends away from the normal.

Earth's interior

From lag time to distance

A seismograph station records the P wave, and the S wave arrives 2 min 30 s later. (a) Use the travel-time graph to find how far away the earthquake was. (b) A second station's lag is only 30 s. Which station is closer, and about how far is it? (c) Why can't these two stations alone tell where the quake was?

(a) Change the lag to seconds:

2min30s=120+30=150s

Find where the S curve is 150 s above the P curve. That happens at 1500 km (the black bar on the graph). Check by reading both curves there:

tS−tP=350−200=150s✓ d≈1500km

(b) A shorter lag means a shorter distance, so the second station is closer. The curves are 30 s apart at about 250 km:

d≈250km

(c) Each station gives a distance but not a direction. Draw a circle around each station with that radius: two circles cross at two points. A third station's circle crosses only one of them, which is the epicenter.

The gap between the curves keeps widening to the right: the farther the waves travel, the farther the slower S wave falls behind. That's why a bigger lag always means a more distant quake.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. Fans in a stadium do “the wave.” The wave goes all the way around the stadium. Does any fan go around the stadium? What does travel around?
    Answer
    No fan moves around; each one just stands up and sits down in place. What travels is the disturbance (the energy and the pattern). That's true of every wave: it carries energy, not the medium.
  2. Two astronauts stand 10 m apart on the Moon, and one claps. Does the other hear it? What if they press their helmets together and one taps her helmet? Would they see a flashlight blink?
    Answer
    No: the Moon has no air, and sound is a mechanical wave that needs a medium. With helmets touching, yes: the sound travels through the solid helmets. They would see the flashlight, because light is an electromagnetic wave and needs no medium.
  3. A buoy bobs up and down 15 times in 60 s. What are the frequency and the period?
    Answer
    f = 15 ÷ 60 = 0.25 Hz. T = 1/f = 1/0.25 = 4.0 s.
  4. You shake one end of a long rope, then shake it twice as often. What happens to the wave speed and the wavelength?
    Answer
    The speed stays the same, because the rope (the medium) hasn't changed. Since v = fλ, doubling f makes the wavelength half as long.
  5. A pulse on a thin, light rope reaches a knot where it joins a thick, heavy rope. Describe the reflected pulse and the transmitted pulse.
    Answer
    Going from light (less dense) to heavy (more dense), the reflected pulse comes back upside down. The transmitted pulse stays right side up, moves slower in the heavy rope and has a shorter wavelength. The frequency doesn't change.
  6. A pulse with an amplitude of +4 cm meets a pulse with an amplitude of −1 cm on the same rope. What is the displacement where they overlap? What kind of interference is this? What happens after they pass?
    Answer
    +4 + (−1) = +3 cm. One is up and one is down, so it's destructive interference (the result is smaller than the bigger pulse). Afterward both pulses keep going as if nothing happened.
  7. A train blows its horn as it comes toward you, passes, and moves away. The horn gets louder, then softer, and the pitch drops as it passes. Which wave property explains the loudness change? Which explains the pitch change? Does the engineer on the train hear the pitch change?
    Answer
    Loudness: amplitude (the sound is weaker farther away). Pitch: frequency. Coming toward you, the waves are bunched up, so you hear a higher frequency; going away, a lower one (Doppler effect). The engineer moves with the horn, so the waves reach the engineer evenly spaced and the pitch doesn't change.
  8. A light ray hits a flat mirror with an angle of incidence of 30°. What is the angle between the incident ray and the reflected ray? What angle does the reflected ray make with the mirror's surface?
    Answer
    The angle of reflection is also 30°, so the two rays are 30° + 30° = 60° apart. The reflected ray is 90° − 30° = 60° from the surface (angles are measured from the normal).
  9. Light goes from air into cooking oil (n = 1.47). Does it bend toward or away from the normal? How fast does light travel in the oil?
    Answer
    Toward the normal: the oil has a bigger n, so light slows down. v = c/n = (3.0 × 10⁸)/1.47 = 2.0 × 10⁸ m/s.
  10. S waves from an earthquake never arrive at stations on the far side of the Earth. What does this tell scientists, and why?
    Answer
    Part of the core is liquid (the outer core). S waves are side-to-side waves and can't travel through a liquid, so the core blocks them and leaves an S-wave shadow zone.
  11. Earth's radius is about 6400 km, and the crust under a continent is about 30 km thick. If Earth were an apple 4 cm in radius, how thick would its crust be? Is that thicker or thinner than apple skin?
    Answer
    30 ÷ 6400 × 4 cm ≈ 0.019 cm ≈ 0.2 mm, about as thin as apple skin. The crust is a very thin shell on a planet that is mostly mantle and core.
  12. Sea-floor rock is youngest right at a mid-ocean ridge and gets older toward the trenches. Explain this using mantle convection.
    Answer
    Hot, less dense mantle rock rises under the ridge and makes new crust there, so the youngest rock is at the ridge. The flow carries the sea floor sideways, away from the ridge, and it cools and gets older as it goes. At a trench the old, cold, dense plate sinks back into the mantle.
  13. Name the type of plate boundary: (a) the Red Sea, which is slowly getting wider as Africa and Arabia pull apart; (b) Japan, with a deep ocean trench offshore and many volcanoes; (c) the Alpine Fault in New Zealand, where streams that cross it have been shifted sideways.
    Answer
    (a) Divergent. (b) Convergent (subduction). (c) Transform.
  14. A volcanic island is 300 km from the hot spot that made it, and its rock is 6 million years old. How fast is the plate moving, in km per million years and in cm per year?
    Answer
    300 km ÷ 6 million years = 50 km per million years. 50 km = 5,000,000 cm, so that's 5,000,000 cm ÷ 1,000,000 years = 5 cm/year.
  15. Using the travel-time graph in the worked examples: a station's S wave arrives 3 min 6 s after its P wave. About how far away was the quake? Another station's lag is 1 min. Which station is closer?
    Answer
    3 min 6 s = 180 + 6 = 186 s. The curves are 186 s apart at about 2000 km (P at 250 s, S at about 436 s). A 1-min (60 s) lag is about 500 km, so the second station is much closer. A smaller lag always means a closer quake.
