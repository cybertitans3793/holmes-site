Study guide

Unit 4 · Motion and Forces in 2D

How to use this: read the key ideas, then cover each worked example and try it yourself before reading the solution. Do the self-check questions on paper before you open the answers, mark the ones you miss, and come back to them in a day or two without looking. Rereading won't do much; answering questions will.

Key ideas

  • Vectors have a size and a direction (displacement, velocity, acceleration, force). Scalars have only a size (distance, speed, mass, time).
  • Components: split a vector into x and y parts. For a vector A at angle θ above the +x axis, Ax = A cosθ and Ay = A sinθ. If the angle is measured from the y axis, the sin and cos swap.
  • Adding vectors: draw them tip to tail, or add the components separately: Rx = Ax + Bx, Ry = Ay + By. Then R = √(Rx² + Ry²).
  • Relative velocity in 2D: a boat's velocity relative to the ground = its velocity relative to the water + the water's velocity, added as vectors. The time to cross a river depends only on the part of the boat's velocity straight across.
  • Projectiles: once launched, the only force is gravity (ignoring air). Horizontal and vertical motion are independent: ax = 0, so vx stays constant; ay = g downward.
  • The time in the air is set by the vertical motion alone. A ball dropped and a ball launched sideways from the same height land at the same time.
  • At the top of the path, vy = 0, but the ball is still moving with vx, and its acceleration is still g downward.
  • On level ground, 45° gives the longest range. Angles that add to 90° (like 30° and 60°) give the same range.
  • IB With air resistance, a projectile's horizontal speed drops, it doesn't go as high or as far, and it comes down more steeply than it went up.
  • Equilibrium in 2D: ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0 separately. The flatter the ropes holding up a weight, the bigger their tension.
  • Inclines: tilt your axes so x runs along the slope. Gravity splits into mg sinθ down the slope and mg cosθ into the slope, so FN = mg cosθ (with no other forces perpendicular to the slope).
  • On a frictionless incline, a = g sinθ, whatever the mass. A block that slides at constant speed (or is just about to slip) has μ = tanθ.
  • Pulling at an angle on a level floor: the force's vertical part lifts some of the weight, so FN = mg − F sinθ and friction gets smaller.

Common mistakes

  • Using sin where you need cos. Check which axis the angle is measured from, and make sure the component next to the angle gets cos.
  • Mixing horizontal and vertical values, like putting vx into a vertical equation. Keep two separate lists.
  • Saying a projectile has zero velocity or zero acceleration at the top.
  • Giving a projectile a horizontal acceleration, or making vx change, when there's no air resistance.
  • Using FN = mg on a slope, or for an angled pull.
  • Adding the sizes of vectors that point different ways: 3 N east plus 4 N north is 5 N, not 7 N.
  • Aiming a boat straight across and expecting it to land straight across. The current carries it downstream.
  • Trusting tan⁻¹ when the x-component is negative. The calculator gives an angle in the wrong quadrant, so sketch the vector: (−3, 4) points at 127°, not −53°.
  • Getting the time to reach a wall (or any horizontal distance) from the vertical equations. It comes from the horizontal motion: t = x ÷ vx.
  • Thinking the longer rope pulls harder. Only the angles matter: the rope closer to vertical carries more tension.

How to solve a projectile problem

  1. Split the launch velocity into vx = v cosθ and v0y = v sinθ.
  2. Make two lists, one for x (ax = 0) and one for y (ay = −g, with up as +).
  3. Find the time from whichever list has enough information. Usually that's the vertical one.
  4. Time is the link: the same t goes in both lists.
  5. Solve for what's asked (range, height, landing speed).
  6. For a final speed, combine the parts: v = √(vx² + vy²).

Forces in 2D

  • Draw the free-body diagram first, then pick axes. On a slope, put x along the slope so the acceleration is along one axis.
  • Split any force that isn't along an axis into components. Mark which angle you used.
  • Write ΣFx and ΣFy separately. Get FN from the direction with no acceleration, then friction from μFN.

River crossings

  • Crossing time = river width ÷ the boat's speed straight across.
  • Downstream drift = current speed × crossing time.
  • To land directly opposite, aim upstream so that part of the boat's velocity exactly cancels the current.
Horizontal launch

A ball rolls off a table

A ball rolls off a table 1.25 m high at 3.0 m/s. How long is it in the air, how far from the table does it land, and how fast is it going when it lands?

Vertical (starts with v₀ᵧ = 0, falls 1.25 m):

Δy=12gt2 →t=2Δyg=2(1.25)10 t=0.50s

Horizontal (constant speed):

x=vxt=(3.0)(0.50)=1.5m

Landing: vₓ = 3.0 m/s and vᵧ = gt = 5.0 m/s, combined:

v=vx2+vy2=3.02+5.02 v=5.8m/s

The 0.50 s comes only from the height. Roll it off twice as fast and it lands twice as far away, in the same 0.50 s.

Launch at an angle

A kick at 30°

A ball is kicked from level ground at 20 m/s, 30° above the horizontal. Find its time in the air, its range and its maximum height.

Split the launch velocity:

vx=20cos30∘=17.3m/s v0y=20sin30∘=10m/s

Time to the top (vᵧ = 0), then double it:

tup=v0yg=1010=1.0s t=2.0s x=vxt=(17.3)(2.0)=35m vy2=v0y2−2gy →0=102−2(10)y y=5.0m

A kick at 60° with the same speed lands at the same 35 m, but goes three times as high and stays up longer.

Equilibrium in 2D

A sign held up by two ropes

A 10 kg sign hangs from two ropes, each making 30° with the horizontal. Find the tension in each rope.

Horizontal: the two horizontal parts, T cos 30°, balance, so the tensions are equal.

Vertical: the two vertical parts hold up the weight:

2Tsin30∘=mg T=mg2sin30∘=(10)(10)2(0.50) T=100N

Each rope pulls with 100 N, even though the sign only weighs 100 N in total. Only the vertical parts hold the sign up, so flatter ropes need more tension.

Incline with friction

A block sliding down a ramp

A 5.0 kg block slides down a 30° ramp. The coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.20. Find its acceleration.

Tilt the axes along the slope. Weight components:

mgsinθ=(50)(0.50)=25N (down the slope) FN=mgcosθ=(50)(0.866)=43.3N fk=μkFN=(0.20)(43.3)=8.7N (up the slope)

Along the slope:

a=mgsinθ−fkm=25−8.75.0 a=3.3m/s2

In symbols, a = g(sinθ − μkcosθ). The mass cancels, so a heavier block slides down at the same rate.

Relative velocity

Crossing a river

A river is 60 m wide and flows at 1.5 m/s. A boat heads straight across at 2.0 m/s relative to the water. How long does it take to cross, how far downstream does it land, and what is its speed relative to the shore?

Only the across part of the velocity gets the boat across:

t=widthvacross=602.0=30s drift=vcurrentt=(1.5)(30)=45m v=vacross2+vcurrent2=2.02+1.52 v=2.5m/s

The current doesn't change the crossing time, because it's perpendicular to the boat's heading. It only moves the landing point downstream.

Pulling at an angle · friction

Dragging a box with a slanted rope

An 8.0 kg box sits on a level floor (μk = 0.40). You pull it with a rope at 50 N, 37° above the horizontal. Find its acceleration. (cos37° = 0.80, sin37° = 0.60)

Axes: x along the floor, y straight up. Split the pull:

Fx=50(0.80)=40N Fy=50(0.60)=30N

Vertical first, because nothing accelerates up or down (ΣFy = 0):

FN+Fy=mg→FN=80−30=50N fk=μkFN=(0.40)(50)=20N

Horizontal (ΣFx = ma):

a=Fx−fkm=40−208.0 a=2.5m/s2

Get FN from the direction with no acceleration before you touch friction. Using μmg = 32 N for friction gives 1.0 m/s², less than half the right answer: the rope's upward part lifts some of the box's weight off the floor, so the floor pushes up less and friction is smaller.

Equilibrium · unequal ropes

A lantern held by a level rope and a slanted rope

A 9.0 kg lantern hangs from a knot. Rope 2 runs level from the knot to a wall. Rope 1 runs from the knot up to the ceiling at 37° above the horizontal. Find both tensions.

Forces on the knot: T1 up and to the right, T2 to the left, and the lantern's weight (90 N) down. The knot is at rest, so ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0.

Vertical first: only rope 1 has an upward part, so it holds up all the weight.

T1sin37∘=mg→T1=900.60 T1=150N

Horizontal: rope 2 cancels rope 1's sideways pull.

T2=T1cos37∘=(150)(0.80) T2=120N

With unequal ropes, solve the direction where only one unknown appears first. Both tensions are bigger than the 90 N weight. Lower rope 1's anchor so it slants at 30° and both grow: T1 = 180 N and T2 = 156 N.

Launch at an angle · different heights

A ball thrown upward off a cliff

From the edge of a 20 m cliff, a ball is thrown at 25 m/s, 37° above the horizontal. How long is it in the air, how far from the base of the cliff does it land, and how fast is it going when it lands? (cos37° = 0.80, sin37° = 0.60)

Split the launch velocity:

vx=25(0.80)=20m/s v0y=25(0.60)=15m/s

Vertical, with up as + and y = 0 at the ground. The ball starts at y = 20 m and lands at y = 0:

0=20+15t−12(10)t2→t2−3t−4=0

This factors as (t − 4)(t + 1) = 0. Throw away the negative root:

t=4.0s

No quadratic? Split at the top instead: 15/10 = 1.5 s up to a peak 11.25 m above the cliff (31.25 m above the ground), then a drop from rest: 31.25 = ½(10)t², so t = 2.5 s. Total 4.0 s.

Horizontal (constant speed):

x=vxt=(20)(4.0)=80m

Landing: vy = 15 − (10)(4.0) = −25 m/s, and vx is still 20 m/s:

v=202+252 v=32m/s,51° below the horizontal

“Double the time to the top” only works when the ball lands at the height it started from. Here that gives 3.0 s, which is when the ball is back at cliff height, still 20 m up. When the heights differ, use the full y equation or split the flight at the top.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. A 50 N force acts at 37° above the horizontal. Find its components. (cos37° = 0.80, sin37° = 0.60)
    Answer
    Horizontal: 50 × 0.80 = 40 N. Vertical: 50 × 0.60 = 30 N.
  2. Add a 3.0 N force east and a 4.0 N force north. What is the resultant?
    Answer
    5.0 N (√(9 + 16)), at tan⁻¹(4/3) = 53° north of east.
  3. One bullet is fired horizontally from a rifle, and at the same instant another is dropped from the same height. Ignoring air resistance, which hits the level ground first?
    Answer
    They hit at the same time. Both start with zero vertical velocity and have the same vertical acceleration; the fired bullet's horizontal speed doesn't affect its fall.
  4. At the highest point of a projectile's path, what are its velocity and acceleration?
    Answer
    Its velocity is horizontal, equal to vx (not zero). Its acceleration is g, straight down, the same as everywhere else on the path.
  5. On level ground, which launch angle gives the greatest range? Which angle gives the same range as 25°?
    Answer
    45°. 65° gives the same range as 25°, because the two angles add to 90°.
  6. A plane flying horizontally at 50 m/s, 80 m above the ground, drops a supply package. How long does it fall, and how far ahead of the drop point does it land? Where is the plane when it lands?
    Answer
    80 = ½(10)t², so t = 4.0 s. It lands (50)(4.0) = 200 m ahead. Ignoring air resistance, the plane is directly above it, because both keep the same 50 m/s forward.
  7. A boat that moves at 2.5 m/s relative to the water wants to land directly across a river flowing at 1.5 m/s. Which way should it aim, and what is its speed across?
    Answer
    Aim upstream, at an angle θ where 2.5 sinθ = 1.5, so θ = 37° upstream of straight across. Its speed across is √(2.5² − 1.5²) = 2.0 m/s.
  8. A block slides down a frictionless 30° incline. What is its acceleration? Would a heavier block go faster?
    Answer
    a = g sinθ = 10 × 0.50 = 5.0 m/s². No: the mass cancels, so every block has the same acceleration.
  9. A 4.0 kg block rests on a 30° incline without slipping. How big is the friction force, and which way does it point? Is the normal force more or less than mg?
    Answer
    Friction balances the weight's component down the slope: mg sinθ = 40 × 0.50 = 20 N, pointing up the slope. FN = mg cosθ ≈ 35 N, less than mg = 40 N.
  10. A heavy picture hangs from a wire. Why can't you pull the wire so tight that it's perfectly horizontal?
    Answer
    A horizontal wire has no vertical component to hold up the weight. As the wire gets flatter, the tension needed grows without limit, so it would snap first.
  11. You push a 20 kg lawn mower with 100 N along its handle, which points angled 30° down from the horizontal. What normal force does the ground exert on it?
    Answer
    Pushing down along the handle adds to the weight: FN = mg + F sin30° = 200 + 50 = 250 N. Pushing down at an angle increases friction; pulling up at an angle reduces it.
  12. A 2.0 kg block on a frictionless 30° incline is tied by a string over a pulley at the top to a hanging 3.0 kg mass. Which way does it accelerate, and how fast?
    Answer
    Along the string, the hanging weight (30 N) pulls one way and the block's weight component down the slope (2.0 × 10 × sin30° = 10 N) pulls the other. The hanging mass wins: a = (30 − 10)/(2.0 + 3.0) = 4.0 m/s², with the block moving up the slope.
  13. IB How does air resistance change a projectile's path compared with the no-air parabola?
    Answer
    It doesn't go as high or as far. Its horizontal speed decreases during the flight, and the path is no longer symmetric: it comes down more steeply than it went up.
  14. Who's right? A car speeds up from rest in a straight line. An ornament hanging from the mirror swings back and holds steady at 20° from vertical. Ana says: “The car's acceleration is g tan20°, and a heavier ornament would hang at the same 20°.” Ben says: “Gravity pulls a heavier ornament harder, so it would hang closer to vertical.” Who's right, and how fast is the car going after 5.0 s?
    Answer
    Ana. The ornament accelerates with the car, so the string's vertical part balances the weight (T cosθ = mg) and its horizontal part gives the acceleration (T sinθ = ma). Divide the two: tanθ = a/g, so a = g tan20° = 10 × 0.364 = 3.6 m/s². The mass cancels: a heavier ornament has more weight but needs more force to accelerate, by the same factor. After 5.0 s from rest, v = at = 18 m/s. (At a steady speed, the ornament would hang straight down.)
  15. In the box-dragging example on the Worked examples tab (8.0 kg, μk = 0.40, 50 N rope), you raise the rope to 53° and keep pulling with 50 N. Does the acceleration go up or down? (cos53° = 0.60, sin53° = 0.80)
    Answer
    Down. Now Fx = 30 N and Fy = 40 N, so FN = 80 − 40 = 40 N and friction = 0.40 × 40 = 16 N. a = (30 − 16)/8.0 ≈ 1.8 m/s², less than 2.5 m/s². A steeper rope cuts friction, but it also gives up forward pull, and here the lost pull (10 N) is more than the friction saved (4 N). Whether a steeper pull helps depends on μ: it helps only when the friction saved beats the forward pull lost.
