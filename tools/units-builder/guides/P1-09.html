Unit 9 · Fluids

How to use this: read the key ideas, then cover each worked example and try it yourself before reading the solution. Do the self-check questions on paper before you open the answers, mark the ones you miss, and come back to them in a day or two without looking. Rereading won't do much; answering questions will.

Key ideas

  • Density ρ = m/V is mass per volume. Water is 1000 kg/m³ (1.0 g/cm³). It's a property of the material, not of how much you have.
  • Pressure P = F/A is the force per area pushing perpendicular to a surface. It's a scalar, measured in pascals (1 Pa = 1 N/m²). Air pressure at sea level is about 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa.
  • Where pressure comes from: fluid particles constantly hit the walls. More particles in the same space, or faster particles, means more collisions and more pressure.
  • Pressure increases with depth, because of the weight of the fluid above: P = P₀ + ρgh. It depends only on the depth, not on the shape of the container.
  • Absolute pressure includes the air pushing on the surface; gauge pressure (what a tire gauge reads) is the extra above air pressure, ρgh.
  • At the same depth in the same connected fluid at rest, the pressure is the same. That's how barometers and manometers measure pressure: by the height of a liquid column.
  • Pascal's principle: pressure added to an enclosed fluid reaches every part of it. A hydraulic lift turns a small force on a small piston into a big force on a big one, but the small piston has to move much farther.
  • Buoyant force = the weight of the fluid pushed aside: Fb = ρfluidVsubg. It comes from the pressure on the bottom of an object being bigger than on its top.
  • Floating: the buoyant force equals the weight, and the fraction underwater equals ρobject/ρfluid. Anything denser than the fluid sinks.
  • Continuity: an incompressible fluid can't pile up, so the volume per second is the same everywhere along a pipe: A₁v₁ = A₂v₂. Narrower pipe, faster flow.
  • Bernoulli's equation is conservation of energy for a flowing fluid: P + ½ρv² + ρgy is the same at every point along the flow. At the same height, faster flow means lower pressure.
  • Torricelli: water leaving a hole a depth h under an open surface comes out at v = √(2gh), the same speed as something falling h.
  • The flow equations assume an ideal fluid: incompressible, no viscosity (internal friction) and smooth, steady (laminar) flow.
  • IB Viscous drag on a small sphere moving slowly through a fluid is Fd = 6πηrv (Stokes' law). It reaches terminal velocity when weight = buoyant force + drag.

Common mistakes

  • Using the object's density in Fb = ρVg. It's the density of the fluid, and the volume of fluid pushed aside.
  • Using the whole volume of a floating object instead of the part underwater.
  • Thinking a wider or oddly shaped container has more pressure at the bottom. Only the depth matters.
  • Forgetting P₀ (air pressure) when the question wants absolute pressure.
  • Thinking faster-moving fluid has higher pressure. At the same height it's the opposite.
  • Treating diameter like area. Half the diameter means a quarter of the area, so four times the speed.
  • Mixing g/cm³ and kg/m³. Convert to kg/m³ before using SI equations.

How to solve a buoyancy problem

  1. Draw the free-body diagram: weight down, buoyant force up, plus any string, spring scale or normal force.
  2. Find the submerged volume: all of it if fully underwater, part of it if floating.
  3. Write Fb = ρfluidVsubg.
  4. At rest or floating: ΣF = 0. Moving up or down: ΣF = ma.
  5. Solve for the unknown: a density, a volume, a tension or a scale reading.
  6. Check: if the object is denser than the fluid, it should need support to stay up.

Flow problems

  • Pick two points along the same flow, 1 and 2.
  • Use continuity (A₁v₁ = A₂v₂) to get the speeds.
  • Use Bernoulli to get a pressure or a height. A surface open to the air has P = Patm; the surface of a big tank has v ≈ 0.
  • If both points are at the same height, the ρgy terms cancel.

Scales and beakers

  • Hang an object in water from a spring scale: the scale reads mg − Fb.
  • The water pushes up on the object, so the object pushes down on the water just as hard (3rd law). A scale under the beaker reads more by exactly Fb.
Pressure at depth

A diver 20 m down

A diver swims 20 m under the surface of a freshwater lake. Find the absolute pressure and the gauge pressure on her. What force does the water put on her 0.040 m² face mask?

Gauge: ρgh = (1000)(10)(20) = 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa
Absolute: P₀ + ρgh = 1.0 × 10⁵ + 2.0 × 10⁵ = 3.0 × 10⁵ Pa (about 3 atm)
Force on the mask: F = PA = (3.0 × 10⁵)(0.040) = 1.2 × 10⁴ N

Every 10 m of water adds about one atmosphere. The air inside her mask pushes back out with nearly the same pressure, which is why it doesn't cave in.

Pascal's principle

A hydraulic car lift

A hydraulic lift has a small piston of area 0.010 m² and a large piston of area 0.50 m². What force on the small piston holds up a 10 000 N car? How far must the small piston move to raise the car 0.10 m?

Same pressure: F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂
  F₁ = (10 000)(0.010/0.50) = 200 N
Same volume moved: A₁d₁ = A₂d₂
  d₁ = (0.10)(0.50/0.010) = 5.0 m

The lift multiplies force by 50, but you push 50 times farther. The work in (200 N × 5.0 m) equals the work out (10 000 N × 0.10 m): no free energy.

Buoyancy

Weighing a block underwater

A 3.0 kg metal block with volume 1.0 × 10⁻³ m³ hangs from a spring scale, fully underwater. What does the scale read? What is the block's density?

Fb = ρwaterVg = (1000)(1.0 × 10⁻³)(10) = 10 N
At rest: T + Fb = mg → T = 30 − 10 = 20 N
Density: ρ = m/V = 3.0/(1.0 × 10⁻³) = 3000 kg/m³

The block “loses” 10 N underwater, exactly the weight of the 1 L of water it pushes aside. Weighing an object in air and in water is a way to measure its volume and density.

Continuity + Bernoulli

Water through a narrowing pipe

Water flows at 2.0 m/s through a horizontal pipe that narrows to one-quarter of its cross-sectional area. Find the speed in the narrow part and how much the pressure drops there.

Continuity: A v₁ = (A/4)v₂ → v₂ = 4(2.0) = 8.0 m/s
Bernoulli (same height): P₁ + ½ρv₁² = P₂ + ½ρv₂²
  P₁ − P₂ = ½(1000)(8.0² − 2.0²) = 3.0 × 10⁴ Pa

The water speeds up in the narrow part, so something must push it forward: the pressure behind it is higher than the pressure ahead. That's why the pressure is lower where it's faster.

Torricelli

A leaky tank

A large open tank has a small hole in its side 0.80 m under the water's surface. How fast does the water come out?

Bernoulli from the surface (v ≈ 0, P = Patm) to the hole (P = Patm):
  ρgh = ½ρv² → v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 10 × 0.80) = 4.0 m/s

Both points are open to the air, so the pressures cancel. The water leaves as fast as if it had fallen 0.80 m. A hole deeper down squirts faster.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. A 0.50 kg rock has a volume of 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m³. What is its density? Will it float in water?
    Answer
    ρ = 0.50/(2.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 2500 kg/m³. That's more than water's 1000 kg/m³, so it sinks.
  2. A 60 kg person stands on one high heel with an area of 1.0 cm². What pressure does it put on the floor?
    Answer
    F = mg = 600 N; A = 1.0 cm² = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m². P = 600/(1.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 6.0 × 10⁶ Pa, about 60 times air pressure. Same force, tiny area, huge pressure.
  3. A tall thin vase, a wide bowl and a cone are all filled with water to the same depth. Which has the greatest pressure at the bottom?
    Answer
    They're all the same. Pressure depends only on depth (P = P₀ + ρgh), not on the container's shape or how much water it holds.
  4. A block of wood with density 600 kg/m³ floats in water. What fraction of it is underwater?
    Answer
    Floating: Fb = mg, so ρwaterVsubg = ρwoodVg. Vsub/V = 600/1000 = 0.60, so 60% is underwater.
  5. A ship sails from a river (fresh water) into the ocean (salt water, denser). Does it float higher or lower?
    Answer
    Higher. Its weight hasn't changed, so it needs the same buoyant force. Denser water gives that force with less volume underwater.
  6. A stone sinks deeper and deeper in a lake. Does the buoyant force on it change?
    Answer
    No. The buoyant force depends on the volume of water pushed aside, which stays the same because water barely compresses. The pressure on the stone increases, but the difference between bottom and top doesn't.
  7. A beaker of water on a scale reads 20 N. You lower a block, hanging from a string, into the water without touching the bottom. The buoyant force on it is 3.0 N. What does the scale read now?
    Answer
    23 N. The water pushes up on the block with 3.0 N, so the block pushes down on the water with 3.0 N (3rd law), and that pushes down on the scale.
  8. You cover part of a garden hose's end with your thumb, leaving one-fifth of the opening. How does the water's speed change?
    Answer
    Continuity: A₁v₁ = A₂v₂, so one-fifth the area gives 5 times the speed.
  9. You blow between two sheets of paper hanging a few centimeters apart. Do they move apart or together? Why?
    Answer
    Together. The moving air between them is faster, so its pressure is lower than the still air outside, which pushes the sheets inward.
  10. Why would a barometer made with water need a tube about 10 m tall?
    Answer
    Air pressure holds up a column with ρgh = Patm: h = 1.0 × 10⁵/(1000 × 10) = 10 m. Mercury is 13.6 times denser, so a mercury barometer needs only about 0.76 m.
  11. You pump more air into a tire without changing its temperature. Use the particle model to explain why the pressure goes up.
    Answer
    More air particles in the same space means more collisions with the tire walls every second, so a bigger force on each area of wall.
  12. IB Two spheres of the same material fall slowly through oil at terminal velocity. One has twice the radius of the other. How do their terminal velocities compare?
    Answer
    Weight minus buoyancy goes as r³ (it's 8 times bigger), but Stokes drag 6πηrv goes only as r. At terminal velocity they balance, so v ∝ r²: the bigger sphere falls 4 times as fast.
