Unit 9 · Fluids

How to use this: read the key ideas, then cover each worked example and try it yourself before reading the solution. Do the self-check questions on paper before you open the answers, mark the ones you miss, and come back to them in a day or two without looking. Rereading won't do much; answering questions will.

Key ideas

  • Density ρ = m/V is mass per volume. Water is 1000 kg/m³ (1.0 g/cm³). It's a property of the material, not of how much you have.
  • Pressure P = F/A is the force per area pushing perpendicular to a surface. It's a scalar, measured in pascals (1 Pa = 1 N/m²). Air pressure at sea level is about 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa.
  • Where pressure comes from: fluid particles constantly hit the walls. More particles in the same space, or faster particles, means more collisions and more pressure.
  • Pressure increases with depth, because of the weight of the fluid above: P = P₀ + ρgh. It depends only on the depth, not on the shape of the container.
  • Absolute pressure includes the air pushing on the surface; gauge pressure (what a tire gauge reads) is the extra above air pressure, ρgh.
  • At the same depth in the same connected fluid at rest, the pressure is the same. That's how barometers and manometers measure pressure: by the height of a liquid column.
  • Pascal's principle: pressure added to an enclosed fluid reaches every part of it. A hydraulic lift turns a small force on a small piston into a big force on a big one, but the small piston has to move much farther.
  • Buoyant force = the weight of the fluid pushed aside: Fb = ρfluidVsubg. It comes from the pressure on the bottom of an object being bigger than on its top.
  • Floating: the buoyant force equals the weight, and the fraction underwater equals ρobject/ρfluid. Anything denser than the fluid sinks.
  • Continuity: an incompressible fluid can't pile up, so the volume per second is the same everywhere along a pipe: A₁v₁ = A₂v₂. Narrower pipe, faster flow.
  • Bernoulli's equation is conservation of energy for a flowing fluid: P + ½ρv² + ρgy is the same at every point along the flow. At the same height, faster flow means lower pressure.
  • Torricelli: water leaving a hole a depth h under an open surface comes out at v = √(2gh), the same speed as something falling h.
  • The flow equations assume an ideal fluid: incompressible, no viscosity (internal friction) and smooth, steady (laminar) flow.
  • IB Viscous drag on a small sphere moving slowly through a fluid is Fd = 6πηrv (Stokes' law). It reaches terminal velocity when weight = buoyant force + drag.

Common mistakes

  • Using the object's density in Fb = ρVg. It's the density of the fluid, and the volume of fluid pushed aside.
  • Using the whole volume of a floating object instead of the part underwater.
  • Thinking a wider or oddly shaped container has more pressure at the bottom. Only the depth matters.
  • Forgetting P₀ (air pressure) when the question wants absolute pressure.
  • Thinking faster-moving fluid has higher pressure. At the same height it's the opposite.
  • Treating diameter like area. Half the diameter means a quarter of the area, so four times the speed.
  • Mixing g/cm³ and kg/m³. Convert to kg/m³ before using SI equations.
  • Saying suction “pulls.” Removing air doesn't pull anything: the higher air pressure outside pushes.
  • Using one density for layered liquids. Add ρgh for each layer: P = P₀ + ρ₁gh₁ + ρ₂gh₂.
  • Thinking the buoyant force keeps growing as a fully underwater object goes deeper. It grows only while more of the object goes under.
  • Drawing pressure as one arrow, or only on the bottom of an object. The fluid pushes perpendicular on every face, inward, harder on the deeper faces.
  • Forgetting the floating object's own weight when finding how much load it can carry. Max load = ρfluidVtotalg − its own weight.
  • Using one Torricelli speed for a whole draining tank. As the level drops, h shrinks and the water comes out slower.

How to solve a buoyancy problem

  1. Draw the free-body diagram: weight down, buoyant force up, plus any string, spring scale or normal force.
  2. Find the submerged volume: all of it if fully underwater, part of it if floating.
  3. Write Fb = ρfluidVsubg.
  4. At rest or floating: ΣF = 0. Moving up or down: ΣF = ma.
  5. Solve for the unknown: a density, a volume, a tension or a scale reading.
  6. Check: if the object is denser than the fluid, it should need support to stay up.

Flow problems

  • Pick two points along the same flow, 1 and 2.
  • Use continuity (A₁v₁ = A₂v₂) to get the speeds.
  • Use Bernoulli to get a pressure or a height. A surface open to the air has P = Patm; the surface of a big tank has v ≈ 0.
  • If both points are at the same height, the ρgy terms cancel.

Scales and beakers

  • Hang an object in water from a spring scale: the scale reads mg − Fb.
  • The water pushes up on the object, so the object pushes down on the water just as hard (3rd law). A scale under the beaker reads more by exactly Fb.
Pressure at depth

A diver 20 m down

A diver swims 20 m under the surface of a freshwater lake. Find the absolute pressure and the gauge pressure on her. What force does the water put on her 0.040 m² face mask?

Pgauge=ρgh=(1000)(10)(20) Pgauge=2.0×105Pa P=P0+ρgh=1.0×105+2.0×105 P=3.0×105Pa(about 3 atm) F=PA=(3.0×105)(0.040) F=1.2×104N

Every 10 m of water adds about one atmosphere. The air inside her mask pushes back out with nearly the same pressure, which is why it doesn't cave in.

Pascal's principle

A hydraulic car lift

A hydraulic lift has a small piston of area 0.010 m² and a large piston of area 0.50 m². What force on the small piston holds up a 10 000 N car? How far must the small piston move to raise the car 0.10 m?

Same pressure on both pistons:

F1A1=F2A2 →F1=F2A1A2=(10000)0.0100.50 F1=200N

Same volume of fluid moves at each end:

A1d1=A2d2 →d1=d2A2A1=(0.10)0.500.010 d1=5.0m

The lift multiplies force by 50, but you push 50 times farther. The work in (200 N × 5.0 m) equals the work out (10 000 N × 0.10 m): no free energy.

Buoyancy

Weighing a block underwater

A 3.0 kg metal block with volume 1.0 × 10⁻³ m³ hangs from a spring scale, fully underwater. What does the scale read? What is the block's density?

Fb=ρwaterVg=(1000)(1.0×10−3)(10)=10N

At rest, the forces up balance the weight:

T+Fb=mg →T=30−10 T=20N ρ=mV=3.01.0×10−3 ρ=3000kg/m3

The block “loses” 10 N underwater, exactly the weight of the 1 L of water it pushes aside. Weighing an object in air and in water is a way to measure its volume and density.

Continuity + Bernoulli

Water through a narrowing pipe

Water flows at 2.0 m/s through a horizontal pipe that narrows to one-quarter of its cross-sectional area. Find the speed in the narrow part and how much the pressure drops there.

Continuity:

A1v1=A2v2 →v2=v1A1A2=(2.0)(4) v2=8.0m/s

Bernoulli at the same height:

P1+12ρv12=P2+12ρv22 P1−P2=12(1000)(8.02−2.02) P1−P2=3.0×104Pa

The water speeds up in the narrow part, so something must push it forward: the pressure behind it is higher than the pressure ahead. That's why the pressure is lower where it's faster.

Torricelli

A leaky tank

A large open tank has a small hole in its side 0.80 m under the water's surface. How fast does the water come out?

Bernoulli from the surface (v ≈ 0, P = Patm) to the hole (P = Patm). The pressures cancel:

ρgh=12ρv2 →v=2gh v=2(10)(0.80) v=4.0m/s

Both points are open to the air, so the pressures cancel. The water leaves as fast as if it had fallen 0.80 m. A hole deeper down squirts faster.

Buoyancy from a graph

Lowering a block into water

A metal block hangs from a force sensor on a string and is lowered slowly into a tank of water. The graph shows the tension T against the depth d of the block's bottom below the surface (negative d is still above the water). Find the block's mass, volume, density and height.

System: the block. Forces: T and Fb up, mg down. Lowered slowly, so ΣF = 0 and T = mg − Fb.

In the air (left plateau), Fb = 0:

mg=22N→m=2.2kg

Fully underwater (right plateau):

Fb=22−14=8.0N V=Fbρwaterg=8.0(1000)(10) V=8.0×10−4m3 ρ=mV=2.28.0×10−4 ρ=2750kg/m3

The tension falls only while more of the block is going under, from d = 0 to d = 0.16 m:

height=0.16m

Once the block is all the way under, the graph goes flat: the buoyant force doesn't change with depth. The straight slope means the block has the same cross-section all the way up (A = V/height = 5.0 × 10⁻³ m²); a ball would give a curve that's steepest halfway in.

Two liquids that don't mix

Oil floating on water

A tank holds a layer of oil floating on water. A pressure probe is lowered from the top surface to the bottom, and the graph shows the absolute pressure against depth (in meters). Find the oil's density, how thick each layer is, and the gauge pressure at the bottom.

In one liquid, P = P₀ + ρgh is a straight line with slope ρg. The kink at 0.50 m is the boundary: 0.50 m of oil, then 1.30 − 0.50 = 0.80 m of water.

slopeoil=104000−1000000.50=8000Pa/m ρoil=slopeg=800010 ρoil=800kg/m3

Check the lower part: (112 000 − 104 000)/0.80 = 10 000 Pa/m, so ρ = 1000 kg/m³. That's water. Gauge pressure at the bottom: each layer adds its own ρgh.

Pgauge=(800)(10)(0.50)+(1000)(10)(0.80) Pgauge=1.2×104Pa

The line is steeper in the denser liquid, and the denser liquid is always on the bottom. The intercept is the air pressure on the top surface. If the tank were all water, the bottom would read 13 kPa gauge: the lighter oil adds less pressure per meter.

Flow rate + projectile

How far does the hose reach?

A hose fills a 10 L bucket (0.010 m³) in 25 s. Its nozzle opening has an area of 0.80 cm² and the hose itself 2.0 cm². You hold the nozzle level, 0.80 m above the ground. How fast does the water leave, how far away does it land, and what is the gauge pressure in the hose just behind the nozzle?

Flow rate, the volume per second, is the same in the hose and the nozzle:

Q=Vt=0.01025=4.0×10−4m3/s vnozzle=QA=4.0×10−48.0×10−5 vnozzle=5.0m/s

After it leaves, each bit of water is a horizontal projectile. Fall time from 0.80 m:

0.80=12(10)t2→t=0.40s x=vt=(5.0)(0.40)=2.0m

In the hose: v = Q/A = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ / 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.0 m/s. Bernoulli from the hose (1) to just outside the nozzle (2), same height, P₂ = Patm:

P1−Patm=12ρ(v22−v12) P1−Patm=12(1000)(25−4.0) Pgauge=1.05×104Pa

A bucket and a clock give you the flow rate, and the flow rate plus the opening's area gives the speed. Once the water is out, it's just a projectile. The extra pressure in the hose is what speeds the water up as it squeezes into the nozzle.

Floating with a load

How many people can the raft carry?

A raft is a solid block of wood 2.0 m long, 1.5 m wide and 0.20 m thick, with density 600 kg/m³. How deep does it sit in fresh water when it's empty? What is the most load it can carry before its top goes under, and can it take three 70 kg people?

System: raft (plus its load). Floating means ΣF = 0, so Fb = total weight.

V=(2.0)(1.5)(0.20)=0.60m3 m=ρV=360kg

Empty, the raft pushes aside its own mass of water, 0.36 m³. Spread over its 3.0 m² bottom:

dempty=0.363.0 dempty=0.12m

The biggest buoyant force, ρwaterVg, comes when the whole raft is just under:

Fb,max=(1000)(0.60)(10)=6000N Fb,max=mg+Wload →Wload=6000−3600 Wload=2400N(240kg)

Three people are 210 kg, under 240 kg, so yes. Total mass 570 kg pushes aside 0.57 m³ of water: depth 0.57/3.0 = 0.19 m, leaving just 1 cm of raft above the water. A fourth person (280 kg in all) sinks it.

The raft's own weight uses up part of the buoyant force, so subtract it before counting people. A real raft can hold a little more than this: legs or gear hanging in the water push aside water too and add their own buoyant force.

Where buoyancy comes from

Fixing the pressure arrows on a block

A student draws the water's pressure on a submerged cube as a single arrow pushing up on its bottom face. Fix the diagram. Then, for a cube 0.10 m on each side whose top is 0.30 m under the surface of a lake, find the force of the water on its top and on its bottom, and the net force of the water on it.

Fluid pressure pushes perpendicular to every face, inward. The arrows on the two sides are at the same depths, so they're equal and cancel. The bottom is deeper than the top, so its arrow is longer.

Ptop=P0+ρg(0.30)=1.03×105Pa Pbot=P0+ρg(0.40)=1.04×105Pa

Each face has A = (0.10)² = 0.010 m², and F = PA:

Ftop=1030Ndown,Fbot=1040Nup Fnet=1040−1030=10Nup

Check with Archimedes: ρwaterVg = (1000)(1.0 × 10⁻³)(10) = 10 N. Same answer.

The buoyant force is the net force from the water's pressure: (ρgΔh)A = ρVg. Sink the cube 1 m deeper and both faces get 100 N more, but the difference stays 10 N. That's why the buoyant force doesn't depend on depth.

Continuity + free fall

Why a faucet stream gets thinner

A faucet fills a 0.50 L glass in 5.0 s, and the stream leaves the faucet's opening (area 1.0 cm²) moving straight down. Find the stream's speed and cross-sectional area 0.15 m below the faucet.

Flow rate, the same at every height (the water can't pile up):

Q=5.0×10−45.0=1.0×10−4m3/s v1=QA1=1.0m/s

The stream is open to the air the whole way, so the pressure is Patm everywhere and Bernoulli leaves only ½ρv² + ρgy: each bit of water just falls freely.

v22=v12+2gh=1.02+2(10)(0.15)=4.0 v2=2.0m/s

Continuity:

A2=Qv2=1.0×10−42.0 A2=5.0×10−5m2=0.50cm2

No water is lost on the way down: the stream thins because the water speeds up, and the same volume per second has to fit through a smaller area. Twice the speed means half the area, which is only about 0.71 times the width, since area goes as diameter squared.

Torricelli from data

A draining bottle: v² against h

A bottle with a small hole in its side stands so the hole is 0.45 m above the floor, and the water squirts out level. As the bottle drains, you mark where the stream lands each time the water's surface passes a height h above the hole. Show that the data fit v = √(2gh).

h (m)0.200.150.100.05
x (m)0.580.500.410.29

Each squirt is a level projectile. The fall time is the same every time:

0.45=12(10)t2→t=0.30s,v=x0.30

So v = 1.93, 1.67, 1.37, 0.97 m/s and v² = 3.74, 2.78, 1.87, 0.93 m²/s². Torricelli says v² = 2gh, so a graph of v² against h should be a straight line through the origin with slope 2g:

slope≈3.74−0.930.20−0.05 slope≈19m/s2(expected2g=20)

The points fall on a straight line through the origin, about 7% below 2g: the hole's edges and the water's friction take a little energy, so real streams are a bit slower. As the level drops the stream lands closer, and since x ∝ v ∝ √h, the range is halved only when h falls to a quarter.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. A 0.50 kg rock has a volume of 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m³. What is its density? Will it float in water?
    Answer
    ρ = 0.50/(2.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 2500 kg/m³. That's more than water's 1000 kg/m³, so it sinks.
  2. A 60 kg person stands on one high heel with an area of 1.0 cm². What pressure does it put on the floor?
    Answer
    F = mg = 600 N; A = 1.0 cm² = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m². P = 600/(1.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 6.0 × 10⁶ Pa, about 60 times air pressure. Same force, tiny area, huge pressure.
  3. A tall thin vase, a wide bowl and a cone are all filled with water to the same depth. Which has the greatest pressure at the bottom?
    Answer
    They're all the same. Pressure depends only on depth (P = P₀ + ρgh), not on the container's shape or how much water it holds.
  4. A block of wood with density 600 kg/m³ floats in water. What fraction of it is underwater?
    Answer
    Floating: Fb = mg, so ρwaterVsubg = ρwoodVg. Vsub/V = 600/1000 = 0.60, so 60% is underwater.
  5. A ship sails from a river (fresh water) into the ocean (salt water, denser). Does it float higher or lower?
    Answer
    Higher. Its weight hasn't changed, so it needs the same buoyant force. Denser water gives that force with less volume underwater.
  6. A stone sinks deeper and deeper in a lake. Does the buoyant force on it change?
    Answer
    No. The buoyant force depends on the volume of water pushed aside, which stays the same because water barely compresses. The pressure on the stone increases, but the difference between bottom and top doesn't.
  7. A beaker of water on a scale reads 20 N. You lower a block, hanging from a string, into the water without touching the bottom. The buoyant force on it is 3.0 N. What does the scale read now?
    Answer
    23 N. The water pushes up on the block with 3.0 N, so the block pushes down on the water with 3.0 N (3rd law), and that pushes down on the scale.
  8. You cover part of a garden hose's end with your thumb, leaving one-fifth of the opening. How does the water's speed change?
    Answer
    Continuity: A₁v₁ = A₂v₂, so one-fifth the area gives 5 times the speed.
  9. You blow between two sheets of paper hanging a few centimeters apart. Do they move apart or together? Why?
    Answer
    Together. The moving air between them is faster, so its pressure is lower than the still air outside, which pushes the sheets inward.
  10. Why would a barometer made with water need a tube about 10 m tall?
    Answer
    Air pressure holds up a column with ρgh = Patm: h = 1.0 × 10⁵/(1000 × 10) = 10 m. Mercury is 13.6 times denser, so a mercury barometer needs only about 0.76 m.
  11. You pump more air into a tire without changing its temperature. Use the particle model to explain why the pressure goes up.
    Answer
    More air particles in the same space means more collisions with the tire walls every second, so a bigger force on each area of wall.
  12. A suction-cup lifter for glass has a round cup of radius 4.0 cm. If all the air under the cup were removed, what is the heaviest sheet of glass it could hold up?
    Answer
    A = π(0.040)² = 5.0 × 10⁻³ m². With no air under the cup, only the outside air pushes, with F = PatmA = (1.0 × 10⁵)(5.0 × 10⁻³) = 5.0 × 10² N, enough for about 50 kg of glass. The cup doesn't pull: the air outside pushes the cup and glass together. A real lifter leaves some air under the cup, so it holds less.
  13. Who's right? A plastic bottle has a small hole in its side, and water squirts out from 0.20 m below the water's surface. You screw the cap on and the stream stops. Ana says: “It stops because the trapped air is now at the same pressure as the air outside.” Ben says: “It stops because the trapped air is now below air pressure, about 2 kPa below.” Who's right?
    Answer
    Ben. The water stops when the pressure at the hole equals the outside air pressure. The hole is 0.20 m below the surface, so the trapped air must be lower by ρgh = (1000)(10)(0.20) = 2.0 × 10³ Pa: a gauge pressure of −2.0 kPa. If Ana were right, the hole would be at Patm + 2.0 kPa and the water would keep squirting. A few drops leave after you cap it, so the trapped air spreads out and its pressure drops until the flow stops.
  14. An object weighs 5.0 N in air, 3.0 N hanging fully in water, and 3.4 N hanging fully in an unknown liquid. What is the liquid's density?
    Answer
    Buoyant force in water: 5.0 − 3.0 = 2.0 N, so V = 2.0/(1000 × 10) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m³. In the liquid: 5.0 − 3.4 = 1.6 N = ρliquidVg, so ρliquid = 1.6/(2.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 10) = 800 kg/m³. Shortcut: same volume both times, so ρliquid/ρwater = 1.6/2.0.
  15. A flat-sided barge sinks 5.0 cm deeper into a river when 6000 kg of cargo is loaded on it. What is the area of the barge at the waterline?
    Answer
    The extra buoyant force equals the cargo's weight: ρwater(AΔh)g = mg, so A = m/(ρΔh) = 6000/(1000 × 0.050) = 120 m². The barge's own weight doesn't matter: only the change is needed.
  16. A 30 m/s wind blows across a flat roof of area 150 m². The air in the attic below is still. Air's density is 1.2 kg/m³. Estimate the net force of the air on the roof, and its direction.
    Answer
    Bernoulli at the same height: the moving air above is at lower pressure by ½ρv² = ½(1.2)(30)² = 540 Pa. Net force = ΔP·A = (540)(150) = 8.1 × 10⁴ N, upward: the still air inside pushes the roof up. That's how storms lift roofs off.
  17. A helium balloon has a volume of 0.010 m³; the rubber and string have a mass of 4.0 g. Air is 1.2 kg/m³ and helium 0.18 kg/m³. What is the net force on the balloon when you let go?
    Answer
    Buoyant force from the air: (1.2)(0.010)(10) = 0.12 N up. Weight: helium (0.18)(0.010) = 1.8 g plus 4.0 g = 5.8 g, so 0.058 N down. Net: 0.062 N up, enough to lift about 6 g more. Air is a fluid too: Archimedes works the same way.
  18. Who's right? Two identical buckets are filled to the brim with water. A toy boat floats in one; the water it pushed aside spilled over and was wiped away. Maya says: “The bucket with the boat weighs more, because it has the boat in it.” Leo says: “They weigh the same.” Who's right?
    Answer
    Leo. A floating boat pushes aside water whose weight equals the buoyant force, which equals the boat's weight. That much water spilled out, so the boat exactly replaced the weight it removed. (If the boat sank, it would push aside only its own volume of water, less than its weight, and that bucket would weigh more.)
  19. IB Two spheres of the same material fall slowly through oil at terminal velocity. One has twice the radius of the other. How do their terminal velocities compare?
    Answer
    Weight minus buoyancy goes as r³ (it's 8 times bigger), but Stokes drag 6πηrv goes only as r. At terminal velocity they balance, so v ∝ r²: the bigger sphere falls 4 times as fast.
