Key ideas

  • Atoms have protons (+) and neutrons (no charge) locked in the nucleus, and electrons (−) on the outside.
  • Only electrons move. Protons never leave the nucleus. Every way of charging an object is really moving electrons.
  • Neutral: same number of protons and electrons. Negative: extra electrons. Positive: missing electrons.
  • Charge Q is measured in coulombs (C). One electron has a charge of −1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.
  • Three rules: opposites attract; likes repel; a charged object attracts a neutral one. Repelling is the only sure sign that both objects are charged.
  • Charge is conserved. It is never made or destroyed, only moved. Electrons that leave one object end up on another.
  • Rubbing (friction charging): electrons move to the material that holds them more tightly, the one higher on the triboelectric series. That one becomes −. The other becomes + by the same amount.
  • Conductors (metals, water, your body) let electrons move through them. Extra charge spreads out over a conductor. Insulators (plastic, rubber, glass, dry air) don't, so extra charge stays where it was put.
  • Polarization: a charged object near a neutral one pushes or pulls on that object's electrons, so one side becomes a little + and the other a little −. Nothing is added or removed, so the object is still neutral. This is why charged and neutral objects attract.
  • Conduction (touching): a charged conductor shares its charge with a neutral one. The neutral one gets the same sign. Two identical conductors split the charge evenly.
  • Induction (no touching): hold a charged object near, ground the neutral object, remove the ground, then the charged object. The object ends up with the opposite sign.
  • Grounding: touching a charged conductor to a big conductor (Earth, or you) lets electrons flow on or off until it is neutral.
  • Coulomb's law: the electric force Felect gets bigger with more charge and much smaller with distance. Double one charge → 2 × the force. Double the distance → ¼ of the force.
  • Electric field lines show the direction a + charge would be pushed: away from + and toward −. Where the lines are closer together the field is stronger. Lines never cross.

Common mistakes

  • Saying an object became positive by gaining protons. It lost electrons.
  • Thinking a negative object has only negative particles. It has protons too, just more electrons.
  • Thinking attraction proves both objects are charged. One of them could be neutral.
  • Mixing up conduction and induction. Touching gives the same sign; induction gives the opposite sign.
  • Calling polarization “charging.” A polarized object is still neutral overall.
  • Forgetting to square d, or forgetting to change µC to C and cm to m.
  • Putting the − sign of a charge into Coulomb's law. Use the sizes of the charges. The signs only tell you attract or repel.

How to explain a charging problem

  1. Find the electrons. Ask which way electrons move. Protons stay put.
  2. Rubbing? Find both materials on the series. The higher one takes electrons and becomes −.
  3. Touching (conduction)? Electrons flow until the charge is shared. The neutral object gets the same sign.
  4. Nearby but not touching? Polarization: electrons move away from a − object or toward a + object, inside the neutral object.
  5. Grounded? Electrons flow to or from the ground. With a charged object held nearby, that leaves the opposite sign (induction).
  6. Check the total. Unless something was grounded, the total charge before equals the total after.

How to solve a Coulomb's law problem

  1. “What if” question? Use ratios. Multiply the force by each charge's factor, then divide by the distance factor squared.
  2. Number question? List what you know: QA, QB, d, F.
  3. Convert: µC → × 10⁻⁶ C, nC → × 10⁻⁹ C, cm → ÷ 100 for m.
  4. Write the equation Felect = k·QA·QB ÷ d², and solve for the unknown first if it isn't F.
  5. Plug in sizes only (no − signs). Square d. Use powers of ten carefully on your calculator.
  6. State attract or repel from the signs: opposite signs attract, like signs repel.
Charging by rubbing

A PVC pipe rubbed with wool

Use the triboelectric series in the picture. You rub a neutral PVC pipe with a neutral wool cloth. 3.0 × 10¹² electrons move. Which way do they go? What is the charge on each object, in coulombs and in µC?

PVC is higher on the series, so it pulls electrons away from the wool. Electrons go wool → PVC.

Size of the charge moved:

Q=n×1.6×10−19C Q=(3.0×1012)(1.6×10−19) Q=4.8×10−7C Q=0.48×10−6C=0.48μC

PVC gained electrons; wool lost them:

QPVC=−0.48μC Qwool=+0.48μC

Check the total:

−0.48+0.48=0

Rubbing makes equal and opposite charges. The total is still zero, as it was before. Since they are opposite, the pipe and the cloth now attract.

Conduction and grounding

Sharing charge between metal spheres

Three identical metal spheres sit on plastic stands. Sphere A has −12 nC. B and C are neutral. A touches B, and they are pulled apart. Then B touches C, and they are pulled apart. Last, you touch A with your finger. What charge does each sphere end with?

A touches B. Identical spheres share the charge evenly:

QA=QB=−12+02 QA=QB=−6nC

Electrons moved from A to B. How many?

n=6×10−9C1.6×10−19C=3.75×1010

B touches C:

QB=QC=−6+02 QB=QC=−3nC

You touch A. Your body is a ground, so A's extra electrons flow into you:

QA=0,QB=−3nC,QC=−3nC

Touching gives the same sign. The total stayed −12 nC through every touch until the grounding step: then 6 nC worth of electrons left the spheres and went into your body.

Charging by induction

Charging a metal ball without touching it

The negative PVC pipe from the first example is held near a neutral metal ball on a plastic stand. It never touches the ball. While the pipe is close, you touch the far side of the ball with a finger. You take your finger away, and then the pipe. What charge does the ball end with? Explain each step with electrons.

1. Polarize. The − pipe repels the ball's electrons, so they crowd onto the far side. The near side is left +. The ball is still neutral overall.

2. Ground. Your finger touches the far side. The repelled electrons can now get even farther from the pipe, so some flow out of the ball into you.

3. Remove the finger, then the pipe. Those electrons can't come back. The ball is missing electrons.

Result: the ball is positive, the opposite of the pipe. The leftover + charge spreads out evenly over the metal.

The order matters. If you take the pipe away first, the electrons in the ball just slide back from your finger and the ball ends up neutral.

Coulomb's law: ratios

What if the charges and the distance change?

Two small charged beads repel each other with a force of 0.36 N. (a) One bead's charge is made 4 times bigger and the distance between them is tripled. What is the new force? (b) Starting over, both charges are cut in half and the distance is cut in half. What is the new force?

Multiply by the charge factors. Divide by the distance factor squared.

(a) Charge × 4, distance × 3:

Fnew=0.36×432=0.36×49 Fnew=0.16N

(b) Each charge × ½, distance × ½:

Fnew=0.36×(1/2)(1/2)(1/2)2 Fnew=0.36×1/41/4 Fnew=0.36N

In (b) the two changes cancel: the charges make the force ¼ as big, and moving closer makes it 4 times as big. No k needed for “what if” questions.

Coulomb's law: numbers

The force between two charged spheres

A sphere with +2.0 µC and a sphere with −5.0 µC are 30 cm apart (center to center). Find the size of the electric force on each one. Do they attract or repel?

Convert to C and m:

QA=2.0×10−6C,QB=5.0×10−6C d=30cm=0.30m

Coulomb's law (sizes only):

Felect=kQAQBd2 Felect=(9.0×109)(2.0×10−6)(5.0×10−6)(0.30)2 Felect=0.0900.090 Felect=1.0N, attract

Opposite signs, so they attract. By Newton's 3rd law each sphere feels the same 1.0 N, even though one has more charge.

Electric field lines

Reading a field-line picture

The drawing shows the field around a + charge and a − charge. (a) Which way does the field point at A? (b) Which way would a small negative charge at A be pushed? (c) Is the field stronger at A or at B?

(a) Field lines run from + to −. At A the line points right, toward the − charge. (A + charge placed there would be pushed right.)

(b) A negative charge is pushed the opposite way to the field: left, toward the + charge. (Opposites attract.)

(c) The lines are packed closer together near A and spread far apart near B. The field is stronger at A.

Arrows show direction; spacing shows strength. The field is strongest close to the charges, where the lines crowd together.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. A neutral object gains 5.0 × 10¹³ electrons. What is its charge, in C and in µC?
    Answer
    Q = (5.0 × 10¹³)(1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) = 8.0 × 10⁻⁶ C. It gained electrons, so the charge is −8.0 × 10⁻⁶ C = −8.0 µC.
  2. An object has a charge of +3.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ C. How many electrons is it missing?
    Answer
    n = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ ÷ 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 20 electrons.
  3. Balloon X is pushed away by a negatively charged rod. Balloon Y is pulled toward the same rod. What can you say for sure about each balloon's charge?
    Answer
    X is negative: repelling only happens between like charges. Y is either positive or neutral: both of those are attracted to a charged rod, so you can't tell which.
  4. Use the triboelectric series in the first worked example. You brush a rabbit-fur hat with a cotton glove. Which way do electrons move, and what sign does each end up with?
    Answer
    Cotton is higher on the series, so electrons move from the fur to the cotton. The glove becomes −; the fur becomes +.
  5. A charged plastic comb picks up tiny bits of neutral paper. Paper is an insulator, so its electrons can't travel across it. Why is it still attracted?
    Answer
    Polarization. Inside each atom of the paper, the electrons shift a little toward or away from the comb. The side of the paper nearest the comb ends up with the opposite charge to the comb. Opposites attract, and that side is closer, so the pull is stronger than the push.
  6. You touch a charged metal can and a charged plastic ruler. The can becomes neutral, but the ruler keeps most of its charge. Why?
    Answer
    Metal is a conductor: the charge from every part of the can can flow through it and through you (a ground). Plastic is an insulator: only the charge right where your finger touched can move, and the rest stays put.
  7. A positive glass rod is used to charge a neutral metal sphere by induction (rod near, touch the sphere, remove the finger, then the rod). Which way do electrons move when you touch it, and what charge does the sphere end with?
    Answer
    The + rod attracts electrons, so electrons flow from your finger into the sphere. The sphere ends negative, opposite to the rod.
  8. Two charged objects attract with a force of 0.20 N. The distance between them is tripled. What is the new force?
    Answer
    3 × the distance → 1/3² = 1/9 the force. 0.20 ÷ 9 ≈ 0.022 N.
  9. Two charged objects repel with force F. One charge is doubled and the other is halved, and the distance is cut in half. What happens to the force?
    Answer
    Charges: 2 × ½ = 1, so no change. Distance ÷ 2: × 2² = 4. The force becomes 4F.
  10. Two small spheres each carry +4.0 µC. They repel with a force of 3.6 N. How far apart are they?
    Answer
    Solve F = kQAQB/d² for d²: d² = kQAQB/F = (9.0 × 10⁹)(4.0 × 10⁻⁶)(4.0 × 10⁻⁶) ÷ 3.6 = 0.144 ÷ 3.6 = 0.040 m². d = √0.040 = 0.20 m (20 cm).
  11. A lone negative charge sits in empty space. Which way do the field lines point around it? Is the field stronger 2 cm away or 10 cm away?
    Answer
    The lines point straight in toward the negative charge from all sides. The field is stronger at 2 cm, where the lines are closer together.
