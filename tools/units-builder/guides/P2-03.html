Study guide

Unit 3 · Current Electricity and Magnetism

How to use this: read the key ideas, then cover each worked example and try it yourself before reading the solution. Do the self-check questions on paper before you open the answers, mark the ones you miss, and come back to them in a day or two without looking. Rereading won't do much; answering questions will.

Key ideas: circuits

  • Current I = ΔQ/Δt is the rate charge flows (amps = C/s). By convention it flows from + to − outside the battery; in metals, electrons actually drift the other way.
  • Potential difference ΔV is the energy given to or taken from each coulomb (volts = J/C). A battery's emf ε is the energy it gives each coulomb.
  • Resistance R = ΔV/I. A wire's resistance is R = ρL/A: longer or thinner means more resistance; ρ (resistivity) depends on the material.
  • Ohmic resistors keep a constant R, so their I–V graph is a straight line through the origin. A filament lamp is non-ohmic: as it heats up, its R increases and the graph curves.
  • Power P = IΔV = I²R = ΔV²/R is the rate energy is transferred, appearing as heat and light in a resistor.
  • Series: same current through each, voltages add, Rtotal = R₁ + R₂ + … Parallel: same voltage across each, currents add, 1/Rtotal = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + … Adding a branch in parallel lowers the total resistance.
  • Kirchhoff's rules: the current into a junction equals the current out (conservation of charge); the potential changes around any closed loop add to zero (conservation of energy).
  • Internal resistance: a real battery has resistance r inside, so ε = I(R + r). The terminal voltage ε − Ir drops when it delivers more current.
  • Meters: an ammeter goes in series and ideally has zero resistance; a voltmeter goes in parallel and ideally has infinite resistance.
  • Potential dividers split a voltage between two resistors in series, in proportion to their resistances.
  • IB Sensors in dividers: a thermistor's resistance falls as it gets hotter; a light-dependent resistor's (LDR's) resistance falls in brighter light.
  • Capacitors in circuits: in parallel, Ctotal = C₁ + C₂; in series, 1/Ctotal = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂. An uncharged capacitor first acts like a plain wire; once fully charged, no current flows through it.
  • RC circuits charge and discharge gradually. The time constant τ = RC sets the pace: after one τ, a charging capacitor is about 63% full.

Key ideas: magnetism

  • Magnetic field lines leave north poles, enter south poles, and form closed loops. There are no single magnetic poles.
  • Currents make magnetic fields. Around a long straight wire the field circles the wire (right-hand rule: thumb along I, fingers curl with B), with B = μ₀I/(2πr).
  • IB A current loop and a solenoid make fields like a bar magnet's; inside a long solenoid the field is strong and nearly uniform.
  • Force on a moving charge: F = qvB sinθ, perpendicular to both v and B (right-hand rule; reverse it for negative charges). A charge moving along the field feels nothing.
  • The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it does no work: it changes direction, not speed. A charge moving across a uniform field goes in a circle, r = mv/(qB).
  • Force on a current-carrying wire: F = BIL sinθ. Two parallel wires attract if their currents flow the same way and repel if opposite.
  • IB In crossed electric and magnetic fields, a charge passes straight through only if qE = qvB, so v = E/B (a velocity selector).
  • Magnetic flux Φ = BA cosθ measures how much field passes through a loop.
  • Faraday's law: a changing flux induces an emf, ε = −NΔΦ/Δt. The flux can change because B, the area, or the angle changes. A rod moving across a field has ε = BLv.
  • Lenz's law: the induced current flows so its own field opposes the change in flux. That's energy conservation: you have to do work to make the change.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking current gets “used up” as it goes around. Current is the same all along a series path; it's energy that gets transferred.
  • Thinking parallel resistors add up. Adding a parallel branch always lowers the total resistance.
  • Putting a voltmeter in series or an ammeter in parallel.
  • Forgetting the battery's internal resistance when the terminal voltage is less than the emf.
  • Using the right hand for an electron. For negative charges, the force is opposite to the right-hand-rule direction.
  • Saying a magnetic field speeds charges up. It only turns them.
  • Thinking a steady, strong field induces an emf. Only a changing flux does.

How to solve a circuit problem

  1. Redraw the circuit neatly. Spot which resistors are in series (one path, nothing branching off between them) and which are in parallel (connected across the same two points).
  2. Combine resistors step by step into one equivalent resistance.
  3. Find the total current from the battery: I = ε/(Req + r).
  4. Work back out: the same current flows through series parts; the same voltage is across parallel parts. Use ΔV = IR on each piece.
  5. If it won't simplify, use Kirchhoff's rules: one junction equation and enough loop equations.
  6. Check: voltages around each loop should add to the emf, and currents at each junction should balance.

Right-hand rules

  • Field of a wire: thumb along the current, fingers curl the way B circles.
  • Force on a charge or a wire: fingers along v (or I), bend them toward B; your thumb points along F for a positive charge.
  • Reverse the answer for an electron.

Induction problems

  • Ask what's changing: B, the area, or the angle. If nothing changes, there's no emf.
  • Size: ε = NΔΦ/Δt, then I = ε/R.
  • Direction (Lenz): if the flux is growing, the induced field points against it; if it's shrinking, the induced field points with it. Then use the right-hand rule to get the current.
Series and parallel

A combined circuit

A 12 V battery (no internal resistance) is connected to a 4.0 Ω resistor in series with a 6.0 Ω and a 3.0 Ω resistor that are in parallel with each other. Find the total current, the current in each parallel resistor, and the power in the 4.0 Ω resistor.

Parallel pair: 1/R = 1/6.0 + 1/3.0 → R = 2.0 Ω
Total: 4.0 + 2.0 = 6.0 Ω → I = 12/6.0 = 2.0 A
Voltage across the pair: (2.0)(2.0) = 4.0 V
  I₆ = 4.0/6.0 = 0.67 A, I₃ = 4.0/3.0 = 1.33 A
P₄ = I²R = (2.0)²(4.0) = 16 W

The branch currents add back to 2.0 A. More current takes the easier (lower-resistance) path.

Internal resistance

A real battery under load

A battery with emf 9.0 V and internal resistance 0.50 Ω drives a 4.0 Ω resistor. Find the current and the voltage across the battery's terminals.

I = ε/(R + r) = 9.0/4.5 = 2.0 A
Terminal voltage = ε − Ir = 9.0 − (2.0)(0.50) = 8.0 V

1.0 V is “lost” inside the battery, turning into heat there. The more current you draw, the more the terminal voltage drops.

Resistivity

A heating wire

A nichrome wire (ρ = 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m) is 2.0 m long with a diameter of 0.50 mm. Find its resistance.

A = πr² = π(0.25 × 10⁻³)² = 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ m²
R = ρL/A = (1.1 × 10⁻⁶)(2.0)/(1.96 × 10⁻⁷) = 11 Ω

Use the radius, not the diameter, in the area. Half the diameter would give a quarter of the area and four times the resistance.

RC circuit

Charging a capacitor

A 12 V battery charges a 100 µF capacitor through a 10 kΩ resistor. Find the time constant, the current at the moment the switch closes, and the charge on the capacitor after a long time.

τ = RC = (1.0 × 10⁴)(1.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 1.0 s
At t = 0 the capacitor is empty, so all 12 V is across R: I = 12/10 000 = 1.2 mA
Long time: current stops, VC = 12 V, Q = CV = 1.2 × 10⁻³ C

The current starts at its maximum and dies away as the capacitor fills. After about 5τ (5 s) the capacitor is essentially full.

Accelerate, then deflect

A proton through plates and into a magnetic field

A proton starts at rest, is accelerated across a 2000 V gap between two plates, then passes through a hole into a 0.20 T magnetic field pointing into the page. Find its speed, which way it curves, and the radius of its path.

Stage 1 (energy): ½mv² = qΔV
  v = √(2qΔV/m) = √[2(1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹)(2000)/(1.67 × 10⁻²⁷)] = 6.2 × 10⁵ m/s
Stage 2 (force): right-hand rule with v to the right, B into the page: F points up
  qvB = mv²/r → r = mv/(qB) = (1.67 × 10⁻²⁷)(6.2 × 10⁵)/[(1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹)(0.20)] = 3.2 cm

This is a mass spectrometer: the same ΔV and B send heavier ions around bigger circles. The magnetic force does no work, so the proton keeps its 2000 eV the whole way round.

Fields from two wires

The field halfway between two wires

Two long parallel wires 10 cm apart carry 10 A each, in opposite directions. Find the magnetic field at the point halfway between them.

Each wire, at r = 0.050 m: B = μ₀I/(2πr) = (2 × 10⁻⁷)(10)/(0.050) = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ T
Right-hand rule: with opposite currents, both fields point the same way at the midpoint
Btotal = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ + 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 8.0 × 10⁻⁵ T

If the currents flowed the same way, the two fields would point opposite ways at the midpoint and cancel to zero. Fields from several wires add as vectors.

Faraday's law

A coil in a growing field

A 50-turn coil of area 0.010 m² sits at right angles to a magnetic field that rises steadily from 0 to 0.40 T in 0.20 s. Find the induced emf. Which way does the induced field point?

ΔΦ = ΔB × A = (0.40)(0.010) = 4.0 × 10⁻³ Wb
ε = NΔΦ/Δt = (50)(4.0 × 10⁻³)/(0.20) = 1.0 V
Lenz: the flux is growing, so the induced field points opposite the applied field

Once the field stops changing at 0.40 T, the emf drops to zero, however strong the field is.

Self-check

Answer first, then open.

  1. Three bulbs are in series. One burns out. What happens to the others? What if they were in parallel?
    Answer
    In series, they all go out: the single path is broken. In parallel, the others stay lit, each still connected across the battery.
  2. You add another resistor in parallel with two already there. What happens to the total resistance and to the current from the battery?
    Answer
    The total resistance goes down (there's a new path), so the battery delivers more current.
  3. A 60 W bulb runs on 120 V. Find its current and resistance.
    Answer
    I = P/V = 60/120 = 0.50 A. R = V/I = 120/0.50 = 240 Ω.
  4. A wire is replaced by one of the same material that is twice as long and has twice the diameter. How does its resistance compare?
    Answer
    Twice the length doubles R; twice the diameter gives 4 times the area, which divides R by 4. Overall, half the resistance.
  5. Why does a filament lamp's I–V graph curve instead of being a straight line?
    Answer
    As the current rises, the filament gets hotter, and its resistance increases. So each extra volt adds less current than before.
  6. What do Kirchhoff's two rules say, and which conservation law is behind each?
    Answer
    Junction rule: current in = current out (conservation of charge). Loop rule: the potential changes around any closed loop add to zero (conservation of energy).
  7. A capacitor and a resistor are in series with a battery. What is the current just after the switch closes, and a long time later?
    Answer
    Just after: the empty capacitor acts like a wire, so I = ε/R (the biggest it gets). Long after: the capacitor is full and acts like a break in the circuit, so I = 0.
  8. A long straight wire carries 10 A. Find the magnetic field 2.0 cm from it.
    Answer
    B = μ₀I/(2πr) = (4π × 10⁻⁷)(10)/(2π × 0.020) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ T.
  9. Two parallel wires carry currents in the same direction. Do they attract or repel?
    Answer
    They attract. Each wire sits in the other's field, and the right-hand rule gives a force toward the other wire. (Opposite currents repel.)
  10. An electron moves parallel to a magnetic field. What force does the field exert on it? Can a magnetic field ever change a charge's speed?
    Answer
    No force: sin 0° = 0. A magnetic field can never change a charge's speed, because its force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it does no work.
  11. A strong magnet dropped down a copper pipe falls very slowly, even though copper isn't magnetic. Why?
    Answer
    The falling magnet changes the flux through the pipe, inducing currents in the copper. By Lenz's law, their fields oppose the magnet's motion, slowing it down.
  12. IB Crossed fields have E = 3.0 × 10⁴ V/m and B = 0.15 T. At what speed does a charged particle pass straight through? Does it depend on the charge?
    Answer
    v = E/B = (3.0 × 10⁴)/0.15 = 2.0 × 10⁵ m/s. It doesn't depend on the charge or the mass, because q cancels in qE = qvB.
  13. IB A thermistor (whose resistance falls as it warms) is in series with a fixed resistor across a battery. As the room warms, what happens to the voltage across the fixed resistor?
    Answer
    The thermistor's resistance falls, so it takes a smaller share of the battery voltage, and the fixed resistor's share rises. The circuit can switch on a fan when it gets hot.
