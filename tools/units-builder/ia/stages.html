Stage 1 · Topic Approval

Goal: one independent variable, one dependent variable, a system to study, and a reason the physics predicts a relationship. Homework.

  1. Look for “how does y depend on x?” These questions work best. Change one thing and measure one thing. Choose something with real physics to dig into: textbook labs everyone has done (a simple pendulum, a mass on a spring) are too easy to score well on. Browse the topic ideas or adapt an experiment you liked.
  2. Find the physics. Look for an equation linking your IV and DV. It tells you what to plot to get a straight line, and what the gradient means.
  3. Run the feasibility check (below). If any answer is no, change the topic now. It’s much cheaper than changing it later.
  4. Write your question in the form below: what you change, what you measure, and how you’ll measure it. Leave out the range; you choose it in Stage 2.
  5. State your objective: what you expect to find, e.g. “find how T depends on L, and use the gradient to measure g”.

Turn in

Avoid

  • Two IVs at once, or an IV you can’t set to chosen values.
  • A question whose answer is obvious and has no physics to test, e.g. distance = speed × time.
  • Categories instead of numbers (“different materials”). You want an IV you can set to 5 or more numerical values.

Stage 2 · Procedure Approval

Goal: a plan detailed enough that another student could repeat it exactly. Homework. Don’t collect data until it’s approved.

Fill in your plan in the planner below the steps. You can start it now; you don’t have to wait for your research question to be approved. Save draft as you go (you can carry on from another device), and Submit when it’s ready. Mr. Holmes leaves a note on each part and marks it Approved or Revise. Once it’s approved, the planner makes the start of your report as a Google Doc.

  1. Variables. IV: what you change, its unit, the values you’ll use and how many repeats. DV: what you measure, the instrument, its resolution and your reading uncertainty. Controls: what each one is, why it matters, and how you keep it constant.
  2. Choose the range and values. Use as wide a range as you practically can, with at least 5 values spread evenly and at least 3 repeats of each. More is better if time allows. Say why you chose them.
  3. Apparatus list, with sizes, and the resolution of every measuring instrument.
  4. Labelled diagram of the setup, clear enough to rebuild it from. Two ways:
    • Google Drawings: in Drive, New → More → Google Drawings. Use shapes, arrows and text boxes for the apparatus and labels. For freehand, use the Line tool’s drop-down → Scribble and draw with your finger or stylus; it smooths strokes, so use it for odd shapes and use text boxes for labels. Then File → Download → PNG, and upload it in the planner.
    • Paper: draw and label it on paper, take a clear photo at home, and upload it in the planner (PNG or JPG; iPhone photos may need to be a screenshot).
  5. Method as numbered steps. Be specific (“release from 10° marked on a protractor”) and skip the obvious (“gather the equipment”). No calculations here: they go in your analysis. Do include measuring anything you’ll need for them (e.g. the mass of each cart, the string’s diameter).
  6. Safety, ethics, environment. Always address all three, even if briefly: hazards and precautions, any ethical issues, and disposal or waste.
  7. Do a quick trial run. Class time for data collection is short, so your procedure has to work the first time. A trial run finds the problems while they’re still easy to fix. Note what you tried and what you changed.

Stage 3 · Collect data

Goal: complete, honest raw data before the setup comes apart. In class, or at home. You can collect data at home, but only after your procedure is approved. Phones can’t be used in school, so phone-based experiments are done at home.

  1. Prepare your raw data table first: headings with quantity, unit and uncertainty, and a column for each repeat.
  2. Record every reading exactly as read, to the instrument’s precision. Don’t average in your head and don’t round.
  3. Keep observation notes as you go (required): anything odd, anything that went wrong, anything you changed or had to redo. These notes are where your evaluation comes from: a weakness you didn’t notice now is hard to explain later.
  4. Sketch a quick graph as you go. A point off the trend can be re-measured now, not after the equipment is put away.
  5. Measure the fixed quantities you’ll need later (masses, lengths, diameters). Measure them rather than trusting a label.
  6. Take a photo of the setup if you can (at home, or ask Mr. Holmes in class), with no faces in it.

Before you leave

Turn in

  • Your raw data table and your observation notes.

Stage 4 · Analyse

Goal: turn raw data into a result with an uncertainty. In class.

  1. Processed data table: means, uncertainties, and any calculated quantities (like 1/L or T²), each column with its quantity, unit and uncertainty.
  2. One sample calculation for each kind of processing, including how the uncertainty was found.
  3. Straighten the graph. Rearrange your equation into y = mx + b and plot what it says to plot.
  4. Graph: labelled axes with units, error bars, a best-fit line, and steepest and shallowest lines.
  5. Gradient ± uncertainty (and intercept). Work out the physical quantity the gradient stands for.
  6. Compare with the theoretical or published value. Should the line pass through the origin? Does it?

All the rules are on the Uncertainties and graphs tab. Make your graph with the Graph Maker in the P1-00 sims: it draws error bars, max and min lines and gives the gradient with its uncertainty, ready to paste into your report.

Turn in

  • Processed table · sample calculations · graph · gradient with its uncertainty.

Stage 5 · Rough draft

Goal: a complete report. This is the one draft Mr. Holmes comments on, so make it as finished as you can. Homework.

  1. Use the report template headings, in order. Your Stage 1–4 work goes straight into them.
  2. Write the conclusion:
    • Answer the question with your result and its uncertainty.
    • Describe the relationship your graph shows.
    • Say what the gradient and intercept mean.
    • Compare with theory or a published value (cited): is it inside your uncertainty range? What is the percentage difference?
    • Explain any anomalies.
  3. Write the evaluation:
    • At least three specific weaknesses. For each: random or systematic, which way it pushed your result, and how big an effect compared with the others.
    • Limitations: the range, the system, your assumptions.
    • For each weakness, an improvement: what exactly you’d do (equipment or method), and how it would change the outcome.
  4. Count your words and put the number at the top.
  5. Go through the checklist yourself before you submit.

Turn in

  • The full draft. Go through the checklist first.

Stage 6 · Final draft

Goal: your final report. There’s no more feedback after this, and once it’s submitted to the IB it can’t be taken back. Homework.

  1. Act on every comment from your rough draft. Mr. Holmes can advise but can’t edit, so the changes must be yours.
  2. Recheck your numbers: every calculation, unit and decimal place.
  3. Word count: under 3,000 and stated honestly.
  4. Anonymous: no name, no school, no teacher’s name, and no faces in photos. Only your candidate code.
  5. Citations: every source cited in the text and listed in the references.
  6. Clean up: remove template prompts and notes to yourself. Save as a PDF.

Turn in

  • The final report, as a PDF.
