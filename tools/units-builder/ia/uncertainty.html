Class rules for uncertainty

The IB doesn’t require one method, but you must use a sensible one and use it consistently. In this class:

SituationUncertaintyExample
One reading, analogue scale (ruler, protractor, thermometer)± half the smallest divisionMetre stick marked in mm: (500.0 ± 0.5) mm
One reading, digital display (stopwatch, balance, meter, app)± 1 in the last decimal place shownStopwatch reads 2.34 s: (2.34 ± 0.01) s
Repeated readingsUse the mean, ± half the range: (max − min) ÷ 2. If that comes out smaller than the reading uncertainty (for example, all repeats are equal), use the reading uncertainty.2.31, 2.40, 2.35 s → mean 2.353 s, half range 0.045 s → (2.35 ± 0.05) s
Timing several cyclesTime n cycles, then divide both the time and its uncertainty by n10 swings: (14.25 ± 0.09) s → T = (1.425 ± 0.009) s

Put the uncertainty in each column heading, e.g. t / s (±0.01), and add one sentence under the table saying where it comes from.

Writing results

  • Round the uncertainty to 1 significant figure (2 is fine if it starts with a 1).
  • Round the value to the same decimal place as its uncertainty: (9.7 ± 0.2) m s−2, not (9.734 ± 0.2).
  • Keep extra digits while you calculate and round only the final answer.
  • Raw readings from one instrument all get the same number of decimal places.

Propagating uncertainties

The same rules as the data booklet:

If you…then…Example
add or subtractadd the absolute uncertaintiesx = a − b: Δx = Δa + Δb
multiply or divideadd the percentage (fractional) uncertaintiesv = d/t: %v = %d + %t
raise to a power nmultiply the percentage uncertainty by |n|T²: %(T²) = 2 × %T; √L: half of %L
multiply by an exact numbermultiply the absolute uncertainty by it2πr: Δ = 2π Δr

Worked example

  1. Ten swings timed three times: 14.23, 14.35, 14.18 s. Mean = 14.253 s. Half range = (14.35 − 14.18) ÷ 2 = 0.085 s, which is bigger than the stopwatch’s 0.01 s, so use 0.085 s.
  2. One swing: T = 14.253 ÷ 10 = 1.4253 s, ΔT = 0.0085 s → (1.425 ± 0.009) s. Percentage: 0.0085 ÷ 1.4253 = 0.60 %.
  3. Squared: T² = 2.0315 s², percentage = 2 × 0.60 % = 1.19 %, so Δ(T²) = 0.024 s² → (2.03 ± 0.02) s².

Graphs

  • Linearise: plot whatever makes your equation a straight line (see the Report template tab).
  • Axes: label each as quantity / unit, e.g. T² / s², with sensible scales.
  • Error bars on every point for the DV, and for the IV too if they’d be visible. If they’re too small to see, say so in a note under the graph.
  • Best-fit line: straight, balanced through the points. Don’t force it through the origin unless your physics says it must pass through (0, 0).
  • Steepest and shallowest lines: the steepest and shallowest straight lines that still pass through the error bars (the tool below uses the first and last points).
  • Gradient uncertainty = (steepest − shallowest) ÷ 2. Do the same for the intercept.
  • Graphing software is fine, but say what it did (e.g. “linear fit from Google Sheets”) and still find the uncertainty from the steepest and shallowest lines. A high R² is not an uncertainty.

Outliers, random and systematic error

  • Outlier: a point clearly off the trend. Circle it, explain the likely cause, and re-measure it if you can. You may leave it out of the fit if you give a reason. No statistical test is needed.
  • Random error scatters readings both ways. It shows as spread in your repeats and around the line, and repeats reduce its effect.
  • Systematic error shifts every reading the same way (a zero error, or always reading from one side). Repeats don’t remove it. It often shows up as an unexpected intercept.
  • Comparing with an accepted value: percentage difference = |yours − accepted| ÷ accepted × 100 %. If the accepted value lies inside your ± range, your result agrees with theory. If the difference is much bigger than your percentage uncertainty, look for a systematic error and discuss it in your evaluation.

Gradient and uncertainty tool

Type your data, one point per line: x, y, uncertainty in y. The tool draws error bars, the best-fit line, and the steepest and shallowest lines through the first and last error bars, then gives the gradient with its uncertainty. Use it to check your own graph; you still need to make the graph in your report.
